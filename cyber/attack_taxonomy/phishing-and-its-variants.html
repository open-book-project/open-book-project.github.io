    Chapter 6: Phishing and Its Variants¶

    Phishing is the use of deceptive messages to trick people into giving up credentials, clicking malicious links or running malware. It is the most common way into an organisation, and it comes in many variants that differ by medium (email, text message, voice, social media), by target (anyone, one person, an executive) and by technique. This chapter covers eight variants.

    Layman analogy

    Receiving a perfectly convincing fake letter from your bank, printed on what looks like official letterhead, asking you to call a number to "verify your account details for security purposes." The letter looks real; the phone number is not your bank's.

    6.1 Email Phishing (Mass Phishing)¶

    Bulk emails sent to thousands or millions of random recipients, impersonating well-known brands: banks, streaming services, online retailers, government agencies.

    • Look-alike domains: "paypa1.com", "netf1ix-support.com", "amazon-security-alert.net"
    • Malicious email attachments: Word documents with macros, PDFs with embedded scripts
    • Spoofed sender addresses that appear legitimate at first glance

    Example 6.1: Email Phishing (Mass Phishing)

    "Your Netflix account is suspended — verify immediately at netf1ix-support.com." Millions of such emails are sent. Even if only 0.1% of recipients click, that's still thousands of compromised accounts. The economics work for the attacker even with an almost total failure rate.

    6.2 Spear Phishing¶

    Highly targeted phishing aimed at a specific named individual, using personalised details gathered through research (LinkedIn, company website, social media, prior data breaches) to make the message appear completely legitimate.

    Layman analogy

    Instead of a generic "Dear Customer" letter, you receive: "Hi Sarah, following up on the Q3 budget review you discussed with Mark on Tuesday — the updated figures are in the attached document." Specific, plausible, and deeply alarming.

    Example 6.2: Spear Phishing

    The 2016 DNC Hack: A spear-phishing email was sent specifically to John Podesta (Hillary Clinton's campaign chairman), stating his Google password needed to be urgently changed. The email linked to a convincing fake Google login page. He entered his real credentials. Thousands of campaign emails were subsequently published.

    6.3 Whaling¶

    Spear phishing specifically aimed at C-suite executives (CEO, CFO, CISO, CTO) — the highest-value targets with authority to authorise large financial transactions, access the most sensitive data, or override security controls.

    Common scenario — Business Email Compromise (CEO Fraud): The attacker spoofs the CEO's email and messages the CFO: "This is confidential and urgent. Wire $2.3M to our acquisition partner's account immediately. Do not discuss with anyone. I'll explain after."

    Example 6.3: Whaling

    2019 — Toyota Boshoku Corporation: Employees were deceived via whaling/Business Email Compromise into changing wire transfer account details. The company lost $37 million in a single fraudulent transaction.

    6.4 Smishing (SMS Phishing)¶

    Phishing attacks conducted via SMS text messages. These are particularly dangerous because text messages bypass email filters and appear in the same inbox as genuine messages from your bank.

    Example message: "USPS: Your parcel could not be delivered due to incorrect address. Update your details at usps-parcel-update[.]ru within 24 hours or it will be returned."

    Example 6.4: Smishing (SMS Phishing)

    During the COVID-19 pandemic, smishing attacks impersonating government health agencies and tax authorities to offer "stimulus payments" or "test results" surged massively, with hundreds of millions of malicious texts sent across the US, UK, and Australia.

    6.5 Vishing (Voice Phishing)¶

    Phishing conducted via telephone calls, with attackers impersonating banks, the IRS/HMRC, Microsoft support, police, or other authorities to extract information or money.

    Common script: "Hello, this is Microsoft Technical Support. We have detected a critical virus on your computer. I need remote access to remove it immediately before your data is destroyed."

    Example 6.5: Vishing (Voice Phishing)

    Twitter Hack (2020): A teenager orchestrated a vishing campaign against Twitter employees, calling them and impersonating the IT department. He convinced multiple employees to grant access to internal administrative tools. This allowed compromise of 130 high-profile accounts (Barack Obama, Elon Musk, Apple, Joe Biden) to run a Bitcoin scam that generated $120,000 in 3 hours.

    6.6 Pharming¶

    Redirect users from legitimate websites to fake replica sites WITHOUT requiring them to click any malicious link — accomplished by poisoning DNS caches or modifying the local "hosts" file.

    Layman analogy

    You correctly type "paypal.com" into your browser. But someone has altered the internet's address book (DNS) so that paypal.com now resolves to the attacker's IP address. You see a pixel-perfect replica of PayPal. Everything you type goes to the attacker. You never clicked anything suspicious.

    6.7 Angler Phishing¶

    Attackers create fake customer service accounts on social media (Twitter/X, Facebook, Instagram) and monitor for users publicly complaining about real companies. They respond to those complaints offering "help" — which is actually credential theft.

    Example: User tweets "@Barclays my account is locked, please help!" — Attacker replies from @BarclaysHelpTeam1 (fake account): "We're sorry for the inconvenience! Please DM us your account number and date of birth to verify your identity and unlock your account."

    6.8 Clone Phishing¶

    A legitimate, previously delivered email (e.g., a DHL parcel tracking update, a LinkedIn notification) is cloned exactly. The real attachment or link is replaced with a malicious one, and it is resent appearing to be a "follow-up," "correction," or "updated version."

    Layman analogy

    Someone makes a perfect photocopy of a real letter from your bank, changes the phone number at the bottom to their own, and puts it in your post box. It looks identical to the real thing.

    6.9 Phishing variants at a glance¶

    The eight variants differ by medium, target and technique.

    Variant Medium Typical target Technique
    Email phishing Email Mass, sent to thousands at random Fake login pages, attachments, look-alike domains
    Spear phishing Email One researched individual Personalised with name, employer and colleagues
    Whaling Email C-suite executives Highly tailored; spoofed board members or lawyers
    Smishing SMS Any mobile user Link or urgent call to action in a text
    Vishing Voice call Elderly people, employees, customers Impersonates a bank, tax office, tech support or government
    Pharming Poisoned DNS or hosts file All web users Redirects a correct address to a fake site
    Angler phishing Social media Users who complain publicly Fake customer-service account replies
    Clone phishing Email Previous recipients Copies a real email, swaps the link or attachment

    6.10 Defence tips¶

    • Never click links in emails — type the organisation's address directly into your browser
    • Verify unexpected requests by calling the organisation on their OFFICIAL published number — never the number in the email
    • Enable MFA on ALL accounts — even if credentials are stolen, MFA prevents login without the second factor
    • Check for domain name oddities: "paypa1.com" vs "paypal.com", "amazon-alerts.net" vs "amazon.com"
    • Organisations should implement email authentication: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records
    • For wire transfers requested by email: ALWAYS call the requestor on a known number to verbally confirm

    6.11 Summary¶

    • Phishing variants differ by medium (email, SMS, voice, social media), target (mass, one person, executives) and technique (look-alike domains, cloned messages, poisoned DNS).
    • Mass phishing works on economics: even if 0.1% of recipients respond, the cost to the attacker is tiny.
    • Pharming needs no click: it poisons DNS or the hosts file so that the correct address leads to a fake site.
    • Defences: verify through an official channel you find yourself, use MFA, check the domain carefully, and have the organisation deploy SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

    6.12 Exercises¶

    1. Compare email phishing, spear phishing, whaling and clone phishing by target and technique.

      Answer

      Email phishing: mass, sent to thousands, with fake login pages and look-alike domains. Spear phishing: one researched individual, with personal details. Whaling: spear phishing aimed at C-suite executives, often business email compromise. Clone phishing: copies a legitimate email the victim already received and swaps in a malicious link or attachment.

    2. List three signs that "netf1ix-support.com" is not Netflix.

      Answer

      The "i" is replaced by the digit 1; the domain is not netflix.com but a different one with "-support" added; the message demands urgent verification. The domain, not the display name, decides who owns the site.

    3. Why do smishing messages tend to be believed more readily than phishing emails?

      Answer

      Text messages bypass email filters and arrive in the same inbox as genuine messages from the victim's bank or courier, and they are short and urgent.

    4. What is the key difference between phishing and pharming?

      Answer

      Phishing needs the victim to click a deceptive link or open a message. Pharming needs no click: DNS or the hosts file is poisoned so that typing the correct address leads to the fake site.

    5. What do SPF, DKIM and DMARC do?

      Answer

      SPF lists which servers may send mail for a domain. DKIM adds a cryptographic signature that proves the message was sent by the domain and not altered. DMARC tells receivers what to do with mail that fails those checks and reports back. Together they make spoofing the organisation's own domain much harder.
