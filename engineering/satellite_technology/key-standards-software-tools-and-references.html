    Key Standards, Software Tools, and References¶

    “Without standards, there can be no improvement.”
    —Taiichi Ohno

    The design, operation, and interoperability of satellite systems are governed by an extensive body of international standards, engineering specifications, and regulatory recommendations. This appendix consolidates the most essential standards across four principal domains—CCSDS spacecraft protocols, ITU-R radio regulations, ECSS/ISO quality and engineering requirements, and 3GPP Non-Terrestrial Network specifications—together with a curated list of widely used mission analysis and design software tools and the seminal textbooks that underpin graduate-level satellite engineering education. Readers are encouraged to consult the official issuing bodies for the latest versions, as revisions are issued regularly; document issue letters (e.g., -B-3, -G-3) indicate binding Blue Books or informational Green Books in the CCSDS system.

    CCSDS Standards¶

    The Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems (CCSDS) is an international standards body comprising 11 member space agencies (NASA, ESA, JAXA, ISRO, CNSA, CSA, UKSA, KARI, and others) and 32 observer agencies. CCSDS publishes protocol standards that enable interoperable data exchange between spacecraft and ground systems worldwide. Its “Blue Books” are mandatory standards; “Green Books” are informational. The structure is organised into four areas: Space Link (SL), Cross Support (CS), Mission Operations (MO), and Systems (SYS).

    CCSDS Layer Architecture¶

    The CCSDS protocol stack mirrors the ISO OSI model but is adapted for the specific characteristics of space links (long latency, asymmetric bit rates, intermittent connectivity, limited onboard processing). The stack from bottom to top is:

    • Physical Layer (CCSDS 401.0-B-31): defines RF carrier frequencies, modulation schemes (BPSK, QPSK, 8PSK, PCM/PM/NRZ-L), coding (LDPC, turbo, Reed-Solomon, convolutional), and antenna standards for deep space and near-Earth missions.

    • Data Link Layer: two variants: (1) Telecommand (TC) Space Data Link Protocol (CCSDS 231.0-B-4) for uplink with Communications Operation Procedure (COP-1) for reliable delivery; (2) Telemetry (TM) Frame Protocol (CCSDS 132.0-B-3) and Advanced Orbiting Systems (AOS) Frame Protocol (CCSDS 732.0-B-3) for downlink.

    • Network/Transport Layer: Space Packet Protocol (CCSDS 133.0-B-2) multiplexes multiple virtual channels; Licklider Transmission Protocol (LTP, CCSDS 734.2-B-1) provides reliable store-and-forward delivery suited for deep space delays; Bundle Protocol (CCSDS 735.1-B-1) implements DTN routing for the interplanetary internet.

    • Application Layer: CCSDS File Delivery Protocol (CFDP, CCSDS 727.0-B-5) provides reliable file transfer with metadata; SOIS (CCSDS 850.0-G-3) defines onboard service interfaces including device access services, memory access, and time access.

    Reed-Solomon vs. LDPC in CCSDS. The legacy CCSDS coding standard specified concatenated convolutional (r=1/2, K=7 Viterbi) plus Reed-Solomon (255,223) coding, still widely used on existing deep space missions. Modern missions adopt LDPC codes (defined in CCSDS 131.0-B-3 from 2011 onwards): rate-7/8 LDPC outperforms RS/Viterbi by approximately 2 dB in \(E_b/N_0\) at BER \(10^{-6}\), directly translating to either doubled data rate or halved transmit power. Turbo codes (CCSDS 131.0-B-3 turbo option) perform within 0.5 dB of the Shannon limit.

    Key ITU-R Concepts for Satellite Engineers¶

    EPFD (Equivalent Power Flux Density). NGSO constellations must demonstrate that their aggregate interference into GSO receivers does not exceed EPFD limits defined in RR Appendix 30B. The computation requires statistical modelling of all NGSO satellite positions relative to all GSO satellites over time—a computationally intensive analysis performed using ITU-R S.1503-3 compliant software tools. SpaceX, OneWeb, Amazon Kuiper, and Telesat Lightspeed have each performed extensive EPFD analyses as part of their ITU filing process.

    Coordination Arc. GSO satellites must coordinate with neighbours within \(\pm\)6\(^\circ\) of orbital longitude (the primary coordination arc defined in RR No. 9.7). Where GSO satellites share the same frequency band and orbit location, detailed coordination procedures per RR Article 9 must be completed.

    ITU-R P-series Propagation Models. The P-series recommendations are essential inputs to link budget analysis. ITU-R P.618-14 is the master reference for earth-space propagation, providing methods for computing: rain attenuation exceeded for \(p\)% of an average year; scintillation amplitude; tropospheric delay; ionospheric Faraday rotation; and cross-polarisation discrimination degradation. ITU-R P.676-12 provides the line-by-line model for gaseous absorption, critical for accurate Ka- and Q/V-band link budgets.

    SpaceWire and SpaceFibre¶

    SpaceWire (ECSS-E-ST-50-05C, derived from IEEE 1355) is the dominant high-speed onboard data bus standard for ESA and many NASA/JAXA missions. Operating at 2–400 Mbit/s per link in a network topology using 28-gauge twisted shielded pairs, SpaceWire provides deterministic routing via wormhole switching and is immune to SEU-induced protocol errors through its credit-based flow control. It is used on INTEGRAL, Rosetta, Mars Express, Sentinel-1/2/3, Solar Orbiter, and hundreds of other missions.

    SpaceFibre (ECSS-E-ST-50-12C), standardised in 2019, extends onboard networking to 6.25 Gbit/s per lane over plastic-clad silica optical fibre, with multiple lanes for up to 25 Gbit/s aggregate. SpaceFibre is targeted at high-data-rate applications including SAR processors, hyperspectral imager data buses, and future optical communications payloads where the copper SpaceWire physical layer cannot meet bandwidth or EMI requirements.

    ECSS Radiation Hardness Assurance¶

    ECSS-Q-ST-60-13C defines the RHA process for selecting and screening EEE components for use in the space radiation environment. Key steps include: (1) mission environment definition (TID, SEE, TNID fluxes from SPENVIS or OMERE tool); (2) component categorisation by sensitivity and mitigation options; (3) radiation lot acceptance test (RLAT) for TID, SEE testing for critical components; and (4) design mitigation (shielding, triple modular redundancy, EDAC) where radiation-tolerant parts are unavailable. The standard mandates a radiation design margin (RDM) of typically \(\times\)2 in TID and 3-sigma margins on SEE rates.

    3GPP NTN Standards¶

    Third Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) began integrating Non-Terrestrial Network (NTN) support into the 5G New Radio (NR) standard from Release 15 onwards, motivated by the prospect of direct-to-device satellite connectivity using standard 5G user equipment. NTN standardisation addresses the unique challenges of satellite propagation: large and variable round-trip delays (600 ms for GEO, 25 ms for LEO), Doppler shifts exceeding 100 kHz at LEO for handheld UE, and the need for ephemeris-based timing advance pre-compensation.

    Key NTN Technical Adaptations¶

    The most fundamental adaptations in 3GPP NTN standards relative to terrestrial NR are:

    Timing Advance (TA) Pre-Compensation. The round-trip propagation delay for LEO NTN (600 km altitude) is 4–20 ms; for GEO it is 480–600 ms. Standard NR assumes a maximum TA of approximately 0.67 ms. 3GPP Rel-17 extends the TA range to accommodate 600 km LEO propagation, and introduces UE-based TA pre-compensation: the UE computes its own propagation delay from satellite ephemeris (broadcast by the satellite on PBCH) and GPS position, pre-compensates its uplink timing, and signals only the residual error to the network.

    Doppler Pre-Compensation. At 600 km LEO altitude, a 2.6 GHz signal experiences a maximum Doppler shift of \(\pm\)48 kHz as the satellite passes overhead. 3GPP Rel-17 defines a common Doppler pre-compensation (from the satellite for a reference point) that all UEs apply, supplemented by UE-specific residual Doppler compensation based on ephemeris and UE position. This keeps the residual Doppler within the NR subcarrier spacing tolerance.

    HARQ Disabling. Hybrid Automatic Repeat reQuest (HARQ) in terrestrial NR assumes 8 ms round-trip for retransmission. In GEO NTN, the 1200 ms round-trip makes conventional HARQ unusable. 3GPP Rel-17 allows HARQ disabling for GEO NTN, instead relying on outer-loop ARQ (RLC layer) for reliability. For LEO, HARQ with extended process count (up to 32) accommodates the longer round-trip.

    SPICE: Spacecraft Geometry and Navigation¶

    The SPICE toolkit (Spacecraft, Planet, Instrument, C-matrix, Events), developed and maintained by NASA’s Navigation and Ancillary Information Facility (NAIF) at JPL, is the de facto standard for computing spacecraft geometry and navigation quantities. SPICE “kernels” (binary and text data files) encode planetary ephemerides (SPK), spacecraft attitude (CK), spacecraft clock coefficients (SCLK), frame definitions (FK), and instrument geometry (IK). SPICE is available in C, Fortran, IDL, MATLAB, and Python (via the SpiceyPy wrapper) and is used on every major NASA planetary mission including Voyager, Cassini, Mars Science Laboratory, MAVEN, Juno, New Horizons, Psyche, and DART.

    GMAT: Open-Source Mission Design¶

    NASA Goddard’s General Mission Analysis Tool is the leading open-source platform for spacecraft trajectory analysis and mission design. GMAT supports: force modelling (spherical harmonic gravity, solar radiation pressure, atmospheric drag with multiple density models including NRLMSISE-00 and JB2008); numerical integrators (Runge-Kutta 8(7,8) Prince-Dormand, Bulirsch-Stoer); optimization (differential corrector, gradient, SQP); and thruster models (chemical and electric). GMAT is used by NASA missions, ESA’s ESOC, and commercial satellite operators for launch targeting, orbit maintenance design, and end-of-life manoeuvre planning.

    Annotated Notes on Selected References¶

    Vallado (2013). The definitive reference for orbital mechanics computation. Contains fully documented algorithms (including SGP4, Lambert, Gooding, Halley) with test vectors, and covers coordinate frame transformations (ECF, ECI, RSW, NTW) in exhaustive detail. The companion code library (Python, MATLAB, C, Fortran) is maintained at celestrak.org, making this the practitioner’s first choice for implementing astrodynamics software.

    Markley & Crassidis (2014). The graduate-level standard for spacecraft attitude. Uniquely rigorous in its treatment of attitude representations—direction cosine matrices, quaternions, Euler angles, modified Rodrigues parameters—and their kinematic and dynamic equations. Covers the full spectrum of attitude determination (TRIAD, q-method, QUEST, EKF) and control (PD, LQR, momentum management). Essential for ADCS engineers.

    Maral et al. (2020). The most comprehensive system-level treatment of satellite communications, covering GEO, MEO, and LEO constellation design, link budget methodology, modulation standards (DVB-S2/S2X/RCS2), VSAT networks, broadband multimedia services, and regulatory frameworks. The 6th edition incorporates NGSO mega-constellations and 5G NTN developments.

    Goebel & Katz (2008). Published by JPL with full access to decades of Hall thruster and gridded ion engine development, this is the authoritative reference on electric propulsion physics and engineering. Covers discharge chamber design, electron transport, sheath physics, propellant (Xe, Kr, Ar) performance, power processing units, and flight heritage through Dawn, Hayabusa, and Deep Space 1.

    Klinkrad (2006). Written by the ESA debris programme leader, this is the most comprehensive treatment of the space debris environment, fragmentation modelling (NASA standard breakup model), collision probability computation, debris mitigation standards, and reentry survivability analysis. Essential reading for any satellite programme addressing end-of-life disposal.

    Kessler & Cour-Palais (1978). The foundational paper predicting that collisions between orbiting objects could generate a self-sustaining cascade of debris—the “Kessler syndrome.” Written when the debris population was a fraction of its current level, the paper’s prescient analysis motivates the 25-year disposal rule and active debris removal programmes discussed in Chapter 28. Every satellite engineer should read the original 10-page paper.

    Primary Literature Venues¶

    The following peer-reviewed publication venues are the primary sources for advances in satellite technology:

    • IEEE Transactions on Aerospace and Electronic Systems (TAES): covers radar, navigation, orbital mechanics, satellite systems, and signal processing for aerospace applications.

    • IEEE Transactions on Communications and IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications (JSAC): satellite communication systems, NGSO constellation networks, NTN.

    • Acta Astronautica (Elsevier / IAA): systems engineering, mission design, space policy, orbital debris.

    • Journal of Spacecraft and Rockets (AIAA): propulsion, structures, thermal, ADCS, mission analysis.

    • Advances in Space Research (Elsevier / COSPAR): space science, remote sensing, environment.

    • GPS Solutions (Springer): GNSS, PNT, timing, geodesy.

    • IAC Proceedings (International Astronautical Federation): conference proceedings of the premier annual international astronautics congress.

    • AIAA SPACE and SmallSat Conference Proceedings: commercial space, smallsat technology, newspace developments.

    Standards Currency. Standards are living documents. CCSDS revises Blue Books on a 5–10 year cycle; ITU-R Recommendations are revised after each World Radiocommunication Conference (WRC, held every four years); ECSS standards are revised as technology and mission requirements evolve. Always verify the current issue of any standard before design finalisation. The current issue letter (e.g., -B-3 for 3rd revision of a Blue Book) and date are shown on the document cover page and in the CCSDS, ITU, and ECSS online document repositories. For regulatory matters, the edition in force at the time of satellite network coordination filing is binding.
