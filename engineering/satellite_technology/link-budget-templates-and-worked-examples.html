    Link Budget Templates and Worked Examples¶

    “A link budget that closes in analysis often does not close in practice, and one that closes in practice is always the result of careful attention to every term.”
    —Spacecraft systems engineering maxim

    Link budget analysis is the accounting exercise that determines whether a radio or optical communication link will deliver adequate signal quality to support the required data rate under defined propagation conditions. Every communications satellite, every TT&C system, and every ground station design begins with a link budget. This appendix provides a systematic treatment of the methodology, complete worked examples for four representative link types encountered in satellite engineering, a summary of key formulae, and annotated templates suitable for adaptation to new designs.

    Introduction to Link Budget Analysis¶

    The Fundamental Link Equation¶

    The received carrier-to-noise density ratio \(C/N_0\) (in dBHz) is the central quantity of any link budget. It expresses the ratio of received carrier power to the noise power per unit bandwidth, and is the quantity that determines how much information can be recovered from the received signal before any bandwidth-limiting process is applied. The fundamental link equation in logarithmic form is:

    \[\begin{align*} \frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{dBHz}} &= \text{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} - \text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} - L_{\text{atm,dB}} - L_{\text{misc,dB}} + \left(\frac{G}{T}\right)_{\text{dB/K}} - k_B \end{align*}\]

    where:

    • \(\text{EIRP} = P_t + G_t - L_{\text{tx}}\) (dBW): Effective Isotropic Radiated Power, the transmit power augmented by antenna gain and reduced by transmit path losses

    • \(\text{FSPL} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)\) (dB): Free-Space Path Loss, the geometric spreading loss over slant range \(d\) at wavelength \(\lambda\)

    • \(L_{\text{atm}}\) (dB): Atmospheric absorption including gaseous (O\(_2\), H\(_2\)O), cloud, and rain losses

    • \(L_{\text{misc}}\) (dB): Miscellaneous losses including polarisation mismatch, pointing error, Faraday rotation, and implementation loss

    • \(G/T\) (dB/K): Receive system figure of merit, the receive antenna gain divided by system noise temperature

    • \(k_B = -228.6\) dBW/K/Hz: Boltzmann’s constant in logarithmic form

    System Noise Temperature¶

    The receive system noise temperature \(T_{\text{sys}}\) is the sum of all noise contributions referred to the antenna output port:

    \[\begin{align*} T_{\text{sys}} &= T_{\text{ant}} + T_{\text{feed}} + \frac{T_{\text{LNA}}}{G_{\text{ant}}} + \frac{T_{\text{backend}}}{G_{\text{ant}} G_{\text{LNA}}} \end{align*}\]

    In practice, for high-gain antennas where \(G_{\text{ant}} \gg 1\), the system noise temperature is dominated by the antenna noise temperature \(T_{\text{ant}}\) (which includes both sky noise and ground pickup through sidelobes) and the LNA noise temperature \(T_{\text{LNA}}\). For a receive system with an antenna noise temperature of 30 K (pointing at cold sky), a feed loss of 0.3 dB (equivalent to 20 K), and an LNA with 1 dB noise figure (75 K), the system noise temperature is approximately:

    \[\begin{align*} T_{\text{sys}} &\approx 30 + 20 + 75 = 125~\text{K} \end{align*}\]

    Link Margin and Availability¶

    The link margin \(M\) is the difference between the received \(C/N_0\) and the minimum required \(C/N_0\) needed to close the link at the specified modulation and coding with the required bit error rate (BER):

    \[\begin{align*} M &= \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{received}} - \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{required}} \end{align*}\]

    A positive margin indicates the link closes; a negative margin indicates link failure. Design convention typically requires a minimum margin of 3 dB for geostationary links in clear sky, with additional margins for rain fade specified at an exceedance probability (e.g., 5 dB rain fade margin for 99.9% annual availability at mid-latitudes for Ka-band). The relationship between bit error rate and \(E_b/N_0\) (energy-per-bit to noise density ratio) is:

    \[\begin{align*} \frac{E_b}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{dB}} &= \frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{dBHz}} - 10\log_{10}(R_b) \end{align*}\]

    where \(R_b\) is the information bit rate in bit/s.

    Shannon Capacity Bound¶

    The Shannon–Hartley theorem provides the theoretical maximum channel capacity \(C\) (bit/s) as a function of bandwidth \(B\) (Hz) and signal-to-noise ratio SNR:

    \[\begin{align*} C &= B \log_2\!\left(1 + \text{SNR}\right) \end{align*}\]

    where \(\text{SNR} = C/N = (C/N_0)/B\). Modern forward error correction codes (turbo codes, LDPC) operate within about 0.5–1.0 dB of the Shannon limit. The spectral efficiency \(\eta_s = C/B = \log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\) in bit/s/Hz determines the maximum data rate achievable within a given bandwidth allocation.

    Example 1: Ka-band GEO Downlink¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    This example computes the link budget for a Ka-band downlink from a geostationary communications satellite to a small VSAT terminal. All inputs are assumed, illustrative values and not the parameters of a specific system.

    Scenario: GEO satellite at 35,786 km altitude (slant range taken as 36,000 km), 20 GHz downlink frequency, 10 W transmit power, a shaped-beam spacecraft antenna with a gain of 35 dBi, and a 0.9 m ground dish; the link is evaluated for a rain fade margin appropriate to a temperate climate.

    Link Budget Calculation¶

    The free-space path loss at 20 GHz (wavelength \(\lambda = c/f = 3\times10^8/20\times10^9 = 0.015\) m) over 36,000 km is:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 36{,}000 \times 10^3}{0.015}\right) = 20\log_{10}(3.02 \times 10^{10}) = 209.6~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    The receive antenna gain for a 0.9 m dish at 20 GHz with 60% efficiency is:

    \[\begin{align*} G_r &= 0.60 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.9 \times 20 \times 10^9}{3 \times 10^8}\right)^2 = 0.60 \times 35{,}531 = 21{,}319 \Rightarrow 43.3~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    A pointing and off-axis allowance of 3.8 dB (a conservative allowance for operation at the edge of the satellite coverage) reduces this to an effective \(G_r = 39.5\) dBi.

    The link closes with a positive but thin margin of 2.4 dB even with the 5.0 dB rain fade margin already included. In clear sky the margin is 7.4 dB (2.4 dB plus the 5.0 dB allocated to rain), which an adaptive coding and modulation scheme can convert into a higher data rate, as described in the note below. Margins of this size are typical of a system designed to run close to its limits to maximise capacity; a design without ACM would normally carry more fade margin or a lower data rate.

    DVB-S2 Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM). Modern Ka-band broadband systems use ACM: when the link margin is high (clear sky), the modem switches to a higher spectral efficiency MODCOD (for example 16APSK 3/4); when rain reduces the margin, it falls back to a more robust MODCOD (for example QPSK 1/2). This turns the rain margin from a fixed loss into a variable throughput gain.

    Example 2: S-band TT&C Uplink¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    This example computes the command uplink from a ground station to a LEO spacecraft. The scenario represents typical mission operations for a small satellite, where the ground station must reliably command the spacecraft even at the lowest usable elevation angle. All inputs are assumed, illustrative values.

    Scenario: 2.1 GHz S-band command uplink, 5 W transmitter at the ground station with a 3 m dish, LEO satellite at 600 km circular orbit, worst-case elevation angle 10\(^\circ\) (slant range \(\approx 1930\) km), omnidirectional spacecraft receive antenna, 4 kbps BPSK command link.

    Link Budget Calculation¶

    At 2.1 GHz, wavelength \(\lambda = 3\times10^8 / 2.1\times10^9 = 0.143\) m. For the worst-case 10\(^\circ\) elevation geometry, the slant range from a 600 km circular orbit is:

    \[\begin{align*} d_{\text{slant}} &= \sqrt{(R_E + h)^2 - R_E^2\cos^2(\varepsilon)} - R_E\sin(\varepsilon) \approx 1930~\text{km at } \varepsilon=10^\circ \end{align*}\]

    The free-space path loss at worst-case range is:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 1{,}930 \times 10^3}{0.143}\right) = 20\log_{10}(1.70 \times 10^{8}) = 164.6~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    The ground antenna gain for a 3 m dish at 2.1 GHz with 55% efficiency is \(0.55\,(\pi \times 3 \times 2.1\times10^9/3\times10^8)^2 = 2{,}394 \Rightarrow 33.8\) dBi. For BPSK at BER \(= 10^{-5}\), the required \(E_b/N_0 = 9.6\) dB. At \(R_b = 4000\) bps:

    \[\begin{align*} \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{req}} &= \frac{E_b}{N_0} + 10\log_{10}(R_b) = 9.6 + 10\log_{10}(4000) = 9.6 + 36.0 = 45.6~\text{dBHz} \end{align*}\]

    Interpretation¶

    The 27 dB margin at worst-case 10\(^\circ\) elevation is deliberately generous for a command uplink: TT&C reliability is safety-critical, and the 4 kbps command rate is intentionally conservative. The large margin enables robust command reception even under interference, ground-antenna mispointing or a spacecraft in a degraded attitude, and it would allow a much smaller ground antenna or lower transmit power. If the spacecraft’s attitude is uncertain (for example during an emergency safe mode), the omnidirectional receive antenna provides full-sphere coverage. At higher elevation angles (for example 45\(^\circ\), slant range about 815 km) the path loss falls by \(20\log_{10}(1930/815) \approx 7.5\) dB, increasing the margin accordingly.

    Example 3: X-band LEO Data Downlink¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    Earth Observation (EO) satellites, particularly Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) missions, generate data at rates that challenge X-band downlink capacity. This example computes the link budget for an X-band data downlink from a LEO spacecraft to a mid-sized ground station. All inputs are assumed, illustrative values and not the parameters of a specific mission.

    Scenario: 8.1 GHz X-band downlink in the Earth-exploration satellite allocation, 2 W (3 dBW) transmit power, a shaped-beam spacecraft antenna with 12 dBi gain, a 5 m ground receiving antenna, 600 km circular orbit, worst-case 10\(^\circ\) elevation angle, and a target of 150 Mbps.

    At 8.1 GHz, \(\lambda = 3\times10^8 / 8.1\times10^9 = 0.037\) m. The slant range at 10\(^\circ\) elevation from a 600 km orbit is about 1930 km:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 1{,}930 \times 10^3}{0.037}\right) = 20\log_{10}(6.55 \times 10^{8}) = 176.3~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    Ground receive antenna gain (5 m dish, 70% efficiency at 8.1 GHz):

    \[\begin{align*} G_r &= 0.70 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 5 \times 8.1 \times 10^9}{3 \times 10^8}\right)^2 = 0.70 \times 179{,}857 = 125{,}900 \Rightarrow 51.0~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    Pointing loss \(-0.5\) dB

    Implementation loss \(+1.0\) dB
    Required \(C/N_0\) \(\mathbf{+86.4}\) dBHz
    Link margin \(= 93.3 - 86.4\) \(\mathbf{+6.9}\) dB
    Max rate at 0 dB margin (same MODCOD) \(\mathbf{\sim}\)730 Mbps (\(= 150 \times 10^{0.69}\))

    Interpretation¶

    The link closes with a 6.9 dB margin at the lowest elevation, so the radio link is not the binding constraint for this scenario. The practical limits are more likely to be the spacecraft data storage capacity, the contact duration (a few minutes to about ten minutes per pass for a 600 km orbit, depending on the minimum elevation used), and the bandwidth available in the Earth-exploration satellite allocation at X-band. With 8PSK 2/3 (2 bit/s per symbol before roll-off), 150 Mbps needs roughly 75 Msymbol/s, or about 90 MHz of bandwidth with a typical roll-off, which fits within the 8025–8400 MHz allocation. At higher elevations the margin grows, and the surplus can be used for a higher-order MODCOD, a higher data rate, or reduced transmit power.

    Example 4: Optical Free-Space Link (LEO Downlink)¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    Optical free-space communication (FSO) links offer orders-of-magnitude higher bandwidth density than radio frequency links at comparable aperture sizes. This example computes the link budget for a 1550 nm downlink from a LEO spacecraft to an optical ground station, representative of LEO optical downlink demonstrations such as NASA’s TBIRD CubeSat mission. All inputs are assumed, illustrative values and not the parameters of a specific mission.

    Scenario: Wavelength \(\lambda = 1550\) nm (\(1.55\times10^{-6}\) m), 1 W transmit laser power, 10 cm transmit telescope on the spacecraft, 0.5 m receive telescope at the ground station, 600 km altitude, 45\(^\circ\) elevation angle (slant range \(\approx\)815 km), direct-detection receiver, target data rate 10 Gbps.

    Optical Link Budget Calculation¶

    For a diffraction-limited beam from a 10 cm transmit aperture, the divergence (full angle to the first null) is:

    \[\begin{align*} \theta_{\text{div}} &= \frac{2.44\,\lambda}{D_t} = \frac{2.44 \times 1.55 \times 10^{-6}}{0.10} \approx 38~\mu\text{rad} \end{align*}\]

    which produces a beam diameter of about 31 m at 815 km. The transmit telescope gain (analogous to antenna gain) with aperture efficiency \(\eta_t = 0.8\) is:

    \[\begin{align*} G_t &= \eta_t \left(\frac{\pi D_t}{\lambda}\right)^2 = 0.8 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.10}{1.55 \times 10^{-6}}\right)^2 = 0.8 \times (2.03 \times 10^5)^2 = 3.3\times10^{10} \Rightarrow 105.2~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    The free-space path loss at \(\lambda = 1550\) nm over 815 km is:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL}_{\text{opt}} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 815 \times 10^3}{1.55 \times 10^{-6}}\right) = 20\log_{10}(6.61 \times 10^{12}) = 256.4~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    The receive telescope gain with \(\eta_r = 0.7\) and \(D_r = 0.5\) m is:

    \[\begin{align*} G_r &= \eta_r \left(\frac{\pi D_r}{\lambda}\right)^2 = 0.7 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.5}{1.55 \times 10^{-6}}\right)^2 = 0.7 \times 1.03 \times 10^{12} \Rightarrow 118.6~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    As a cross-check, the geometric spreading loss is approximately \((D_r/(\theta_{\text{div}}\,d))^2 = (0.5/31)^2 \Rightarrow -35.8\) dB, compared with \(-32.6\) dB from \(G_t - \text{FSPL} + G_r\); the difference reflects the top-hat versus Gaussian beam assumption.

    For the receiver, an assumed requirement of about 1000 photons per bit (a value representative of a direct-detection receiver; the photon energy at 1550 nm is \(1.28\times10^{-19}\) J) at 10 Gbps gives a minimum received power of

    \[\begin{align*} P_{\text{req}} &= 1000 \times 1.28\times10^{-19}~\text{J} \times 10^{10}~\text{bit/s} = 1.28~\mu\text{W} \approx -29~\text{dBm} = -59~\text{dBW} \end{align*}\]

    Total path losses \(\mathbf{-261.9}\) dB
    Receive Side (Ground Telescope)

    Link margin \(= -44.6 - (-59.0)\) \(\mathbf{+14.4}\) dB
    Notes
    Margin without cirrus and scintillation losses: \(14.4 + 2.0 + 3.0 = +19.4\) dB
    Adaptive optics can recover part of the turbulence loss in good seeing
    Photon-counting (SNSPD) detectors can operate at far fewer photons per bit than assumed here

    Interpretation¶

    The 14.4 dB margin for a 10 Gbps optical link from 600 km is healthy in clear conditions but depends strongly on the assumed receiver requirement, on atmospheric turbulence and on pointing. Atmospheric turbulence and cloud blockage impose deep fades that require one or more of: (1) optical ground station networks (diversity between multiple stations reduces the probability of cloud blockage; turbulence is site-specific); (2) adaptive optics at the ground telescope to compensate the wavefront distortion induced by the atmosphere; or (3) more sensitive detectors such as superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors (SNSPDs), which operate at far fewer photons per bit than conventional receivers but require cryogenic cooling.

    NASA’s DSOC technology demonstration on the Psyche spacecraft (Section 31.4) uses a superconducting nanowire detector at Palomar for this reason: the sensitivity improvement allows photon-counting communication from tens to hundreds of millions of kilometres with a 22 cm flight transceiver aperture. For LEO commercial applications, the trade between link budget, ground-station network coverage, and APT (Acquisition, Pointing, and Tracking) complexity will determine the practical data rates achievable with 5–15 cm spacecraft apertures.

    Link Budget Summary Formulae¶

    Key Equations¶

    The following equations encapsulate the complete link budget calculation chain. All quantities are in SI units unless subscript dB, dBW, dBi, dBK, or dBHz is specified.

    Complete Link Budget Formula Set

    Free-space path loss:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right) = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) - 147.55 \end{align*}\]

    where \(d\) is in metres, \(f\) in Hz, and the constant \(-147.55 = 20\log_{10}(4\pi/c)\).

    Effective Isotropic Radiated Power:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} &= P_{t,\text{dBW}} + G_{t,\text{dBi}} - L_{tx,\text{dB}} \end{align*}\]

    Carrier-to-noise density ratio:

    \[\begin{align*} \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dBHz}} &= \text{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} - \text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} - L_{\text{atm,dB}} - L_{\text{misc,dB}} + \left(\frac{G}{T}\right)_{\text{dB/K}} + 228.6 \end{align*}\]

    Energy-per-bit to noise density:

    \[\begin{align*} \left(\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dB}} &= \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dBHz}} - 10\log_{10}(R_b) \end{align*}\]

    Shannon channel capacity:

    \[\begin{align*} C &= B\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{C/N_0}{B}\right)~\text{bit/s} \end{align*}\]

    Antenna gain from aperture:

    \[\begin{align*} G_{\text{dBi}} &= 10\log_{10}\!\left[\eta\left(\frac{\pi D f}{c}\right)^2\right] \end{align*}\]

    System noise temperature:

    \[\begin{align*} T_{\text{sys}} &= T_{\text{ant}} + T_{\text{feed}} + T_{\text{LNA}} + \frac{T_{\text{post-LNA}}}{G_{\text{LNA}}}~[\text{K}] \end{align*}\]

    Noise Figure to Noise Temperature Conversion¶

    The noise figure \(F\) (linear, dimensionless) of a device at physical temperature \(T_0 = 290\) K is related to the equivalent noise temperature \(T_e\) by:

    \[\begin{align*} T_e &= T_0(F - 1) = 290(F - 1)~\text{K} \end{align*}\]

    Rain Fade Statistics¶

    Rain attenuation at Ka-band (20 GHz) is the dominant variable impairment in GEO downlinks at mid-to-tropical latitudes. The ITU-R P.618 model calculates the attenuation exceeded for \(p\)% of the average year from the local rain rate exceeded for 0.01% of the time (ITU-R P.837), the path geometry and the frequency. Typical results at 20 GHz and 45\(^\circ\) elevation for 0.01% of the time are roughly 3–7 dB in temperate northern and central Europe and of the order of 15–25 dB or more in heavy-rain tropical regions; Table 7 gives indicative orders of magnitude only, and site-specific values must be computed with ITU-R P.618 using local data.

    Table 7. Indicative Ka-band Rain Attenuation Orders of Magnitude (20 GHz, 45\(^\circ\) elevation); compute site-specific values with ITU-R P.618
    Climate 0.1% of time 0.01% of time

    Mediterranean / subtropical about 3–6 dB about 8–15 dB

    Satellite Link Budget Design Rules of Thumb

    • For GEO Ka-band: without ACM, the clear-sky margin must cover the rain fade for the required availability (often several dB in temperate climates and more than 10 dB in the tropics).

    • For LEO S/X-band TT&C: command links are normally designed with generous margin (well above the typical 3–6 dB minimum) at worst-case elevation, because reliable command is safety-critical.

    • For optical links: diversity among 3 ground stations at \(>\)500 km separation reduces cloud outage probability below 1%.

    • In the power-limited regime, every 3 dB increase in EIRP or \(G/T\) doubles the achievable data rate at the same \(E_b/N_0\).

    • Rule of thumb FSPL: \(\text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} \approx 92.4 + 20\log_{10}(f_{\text{GHz}}) + 20\log_{10}(d_{\text{km}})\) (exact for SI: constant \(= 92.44\)).

    • System noise temperature: a 1 dB LNA noise figure adds only 75 K; a 0.3 dB LNA adds only 21 K. LNA quality matters.
