    Link Budget Templates and Worked Examples¶

    “A link budget that closes in analysis often does not close in practice, and one that closes in practice is always the result of careful attention to every term.”
    —Spacecraft systems engineering maxim

    Link budget analysis is the accounting exercise that determines whether a radio or optical communication link will deliver adequate signal quality to support the required data rate under defined propagation conditions. Every communications satellite, every TT&C system, and every ground station design begins with a link budget. This appendix provides a systematic treatment of the methodology, complete worked examples for four representative link types encountered in satellite engineering, a summary of key formulae, and annotated templates suitable for adaptation to new designs.

    Introduction to Link Budget Analysis¶

    The Fundamental Link Equation¶

    The received carrier-to-noise density ratio \(C/N_0\) (in dBHz) is the central quantity of any link budget. It expresses the ratio of received carrier power to the noise power per unit bandwidth, and is the quantity that determines how much information can be recovered from the received signal before any bandwidth-limiting process is applied. The fundamental link equation in logarithmic form is:

    \[\begin{align*} \frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{dBHz}} &= \text{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} - \text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} - L_{\text{atm,dB}} - L_{\text{misc,dB}} + \left(\frac{G}{T}\right)_{\text{dB/K}} - k_B \end{align*}\]

    where:

    • \(\text{EIRP} = P_t + G_t - L_{\text{tx}}\) (dBW): Effective Isotropic Radiated Power, the transmit power augmented by antenna gain and reduced by transmit path losses

    • \(\text{FSPL} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)\) (dB): Free-Space Path Loss, the geometric spreading loss over slant range \(d\) at wavelength \(\lambda\)

    • \(L_{\text{atm}}\) (dB): Atmospheric absorption including gaseous (O\(_2\), H\(_2\)O), cloud, and rain losses

    • \(L_{\text{misc}}\) (dB): Miscellaneous losses including polarisation mismatch, pointing error, Faraday rotation, and implementation loss

    • \(G/T\) (dB/K): Receive system figure of merit, the receive antenna gain divided by system noise temperature

    • \(k_B = -228.6\) dBW/K/Hz: Boltzmann’s constant in logarithmic form

    System Noise Temperature¶

    The receive system noise temperature \(T_{\text{sys}}\) is the sum of all noise contributions referred to the antenna output port:

    \[\begin{align*} T_{\text{sys}} &= T_{\text{ant}} + T_{\text{feed}} + \frac{T_{\text{LNA}}}{G_{\text{ant}}} + \frac{T_{\text{backend}}}{G_{\text{ant}} G_{\text{LNA}}} \end{align*}\]

    In practice, for high-gain antennas where \(G_{\text{ant}} \gg 1\), the system noise temperature is dominated by the antenna noise temperature \(T_{\text{ant}}\) (which includes both sky noise and ground pickup through sidelobes) and the LNA noise temperature \(T_{\text{LNA}}\). For a receive system with an antenna noise temperature of 30 K (pointing at cold sky), a feed loss of 0.3 dB (equivalent to 20 K), and an LNA with 1 dB noise figure (75 K), the system noise temperature is approximately:

    \[\begin{align*} T_{\text{sys}} &\approx 30 + 20 + 75 = 125~\text{K} \end{align*}\]

    Link Margin and Availability¶

    The link margin \(M\) is the difference between the received \(C/N_0\) and the minimum required \(C/N_0\) needed to close the link at the specified modulation and coding with the required bit error rate (BER):

    \[\begin{align*} M &= \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{received}} - \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{required}} \end{align*}\]

    A positive margin indicates the link closes; a negative margin indicates link failure. Design convention typically requires a minimum margin of 3 dB for geostationary links in clear sky, with additional margins for rain fade specified at an exceedance probability (e.g., 5 dB rain fade margin for 99.9% annual availability at mid-latitudes for Ka-band). The relationship between bit error rate and \(E_b/N_0\) (energy-per-bit to noise density ratio) is:

    \[\begin{align*} \frac{E_b}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{dB}} &= \frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{dBHz}} - 10\log_{10}(R_b) \end{align*}\]

    where \(R_b\) is the information bit rate in bit/s.

    Shannon Capacity Bound¶

    The Shannon–Hartley theorem provides the theoretical maximum channel capacity \(C\) (bit/s) as a function of bandwidth \(B\) (Hz) and signal-to-noise ratio SNR:

    \[\begin{align*} C &= B \log_2\!\left(1 + \text{SNR}\right) \end{align*}\]

    where \(\text{SNR} = C/N = (C/N_0)/B\). Modern forward error correction codes (turbo codes, LDPC) operate within 0.5–1.0 dB of the Shannon limit. The spectral efficiency \(\eta_s = C/B = \log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\) in bit/s/Hz determines the maximum data rate achievable within a given bandwidth allocation.

    Example 1: Ka-band GEO Downlink¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    This example computes the link budget for a Ka-band downlink from a geostationary communications satellite to a small VSAT terminal. The scenario represents a typical direct-to-home or enterprise broadband downlink. Parameters are representative of a Ku-to-Ka band transition era satellite.

    Scenario: GEO satellite at 35,786 km altitude, 20 GHz downlink frequency, 10 W transmit power, 0.5 m spacecraft transmit dish, receiving at a 0.9 m ground dish at 45\(^\circ\) elevation angle in a temperate European location.

    Link Budget Calculation¶

    The free-space path loss at 20 GHz (wavelength \(\lambda = c/f = 3\times10^8/20\times10^9 = 0.015\) m) over 36,000 km slant range is:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 36{,}000 \times 10^3}{0.015}\right) = 20\log_{10}(30.16 \times 10^9) = 209.6~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    The transmit antenna gain for a 0.5 m dish at 20 GHz with 55% efficiency (\(\eta\)):

    \[\begin{align*} G_t &= \eta \left(\frac{\pi D f}{c}\right)^2 = 0.55 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.5 \times 20 \times 10^9}{3 \times 10^8}\right)^2 = 0.55 \times 10{,}966 = 6031 \Rightarrow 37.8~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    Rounding to account for sidelobe level correction and actual beam efficiency: \(G_t \approx 35.0\) dBi (conservative design value for a shaped-beam antenna optimised for coverage rather than peak gain).

    The receive antenna gain for a 0.9 m dish at 20 GHz with 60% efficiency:

    \[\begin{align*} G_r &= 0.60 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.9 \times 20 \times 10^9}{3 \times 10^8}\right)^2 = 0.60 \times 35,531 = 21,318 \Rightarrow 43.3~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    Applying an additional 3.8 dB pointing loss at 45\(^\circ\) elevation (edge-of-coverage reduction for a non-European GEO slot): \(G_r = 39.5\) dBi.

    Interpretation¶

    The 10.2 dB link margin is healthy for a Ka-band GEO system. The 5.0 dB rain fade margin is separately accounted: removing the rain fade entry (\(-5.0\) dB), the clear-sky margin is 15.2 dB, of which 5.0 dB is allocated to rain and the remaining 10.2 dB is the operational margin available for pointing errors, antenna degradation, and interference. In a real system, the 5.0 dB rain fade margin ensures 99.99% annual availability at a zone E European location per ITU-R P.618-14. Increasing the receive dish from 0.9 m to 1.2 m would add approximately 2.5 dBi in antenna gain and thus 2.5 dB of margin, allowing either higher data rate (from 18 Mbps to \(\sim\)32 Mbps at QPSK 3/4) or lower power transmit (from 10 W to 5.5 W with same margin).

    DVB-S2 Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM). Modern Ka-band broadband systems use ACM: when the link margin is high (clear sky), the modem switches to a higher spectral efficiency MODCOD (e.g., 8PSK 2/3 or 16APSK 3/4) to increase throughput; when rain reduces margin, it falls back to a more robust MODCOD (QPSK 1/2). This exploits the available margin dynamically, increasing average throughput by 30–60% compared to a fixed-modulation link designed for availability.

    Example 2: S-band TT&C Uplink¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    This example computes the command uplink from a ground station to a LEO spacecraft. The scenario represents typical mission operations for a small satellite, where the ground station must reliably command the spacecraft during a pass with potentially poor geometry. The worst-case link occurs at low elevation angle, where both path length and atmospheric absorption are maximised.

    Scenario: 2.1 GHz S-band command uplink, 5 W TWTA at ground station with 3 m dish, LEO satellite at 600 km circular orbit, worst-case elevation angle 10\(^\circ\) (slant range \(\approx\)2,060 km), spacecraft omnidirectional receive antenna, BPSK modulation at 4 kbps, target BER \(10^{-5}\).

    Link Budget Calculation¶

    At 2.1 GHz, wavelength \(\lambda = 3\times10^8 / 2.1\times10^9 = 0.143\) m.

    For the worst-case 10\(^\circ\) elevation geometry, the slant range from a 600 km circular orbit is:

    \[\begin{align*} d_{\text{slant}} &= \sqrt{(R_E + h)^2 - R_E^2\cos^2(\varepsilon)} - R_E\sin(\varepsilon) \approx 2060~\text{km at } \varepsilon=10^\circ \end{align*}\]

    Free-space path loss at worst-case range:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 2{,}060 \times 10^3}{0.143}\right) = 20\log_{10}(1.806 \times 10^{11}) = 165.1~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    For BPSK at BER \(= 10^{-5}\), the required \(E_b/N_0 = 9.6\) dB. At \(R_b = 4000\) bps:

    \[\begin{align*} \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{req}} &= \frac{E_b}{N_0} + 10\log_{10}(R_b) = 9.6 + 10\log_{10}(4000) = 9.6 + 36.0 = 45.6~\text{dBHz} \end{align*}\]

    Interpretation¶

    The 18.7 dB margin at worst-case 10\(^\circ\) elevation is deliberately generous for a command uplink: TT&C reliability is safety-critical, and the 4 kbps command rate is intentionally conservative. The large margin enables robust command reception even under severe scintillation, interference, or antenna mispointing. If the spacecraft’s attitude is uncertain (e.g., during an emergency safe mode), the omnidirectional receive antenna provides full-sphere coverage. Note that for higher elevation angles (e.g., 45\(^\circ\), slant range \(\sim\)800 km), the margin increases by approximately \(20\log_{10}(2060/800) = 8.2\) dB to over 26 dB—the link is extremely robust at favourable geometry. The command rate of 4 kbps is more than adequate for standard uplink packets; rates of 64–256 kbps are possible at zenith with the same link budget.

    Example 3: X-band LEO Data Downlink¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    Earth Observation (EO) satellites, particularly Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) missions, generate data at rates that challenge X-band downlink capacity. This example computes the link budget for an X-band SAR data downlink from a LEO spacecraft to a large direct-readout ground station, representative of missions such as Sentinel-1, RADARSAT-2, or Capella Space.

    Scenario: 8.1 GHz X-band downlink, 2 W (3 dBW) transmit power, 0.5 m parabolic dish on the spacecraft, 13 m ground receiving antenna at the DLR/GSOC complex (Weilheim, Germany), 600 km circular orbit, 45\(^\circ\) elevation angle, target 150 Mbps data throughput.

    At 8.1 GHz: \(\lambda = 3\times10^8 / 8.1\times10^9 = 0.037\) m.

    Slant range at 45\(^\circ\) elevation from 600 km orbit:

    \[\begin{align*} d_{45} &\approx \sqrt{(6371+600)^2 - 6371^2\cos^2(45^\circ)} - 6371\sin(45^\circ) \approx 880~\text{km} \end{align*}\]
    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 880 \times 10^3}{0.037}\right) = 20\log_{10}(2.98 \times 10^{11}) = 169.5~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    Spacecraft transmit antenna gain (0.5 m dish, 60% efficiency):

    \[\begin{align*} G_t &= \eta\!\left(\frac{\pi D f}{c}\right)^2 = 0.60 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.5 \times 8.1 \times 10^9}{3 \times 10^8}\right)^2 = 0.60 \times 17{,}841 = 10{,}705 \Rightarrow 40.3~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    Ground receive antenna gain (13 m dish, 70% efficiency at 8.1 GHz):

    \[\begin{align*} G_r &= 0.70 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 13 \times 8.1 \times 10^9}{3 \times 10^8}\right)^2 = 0.70 \times 120{,}893{,}770 \Rightarrow 60.3~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    Interpretation¶

    The extremely large 50.8 dB margin reveals that for this scenario, the link is far from being the binding constraint. The actual binding constraint is the spacecraft data storage capacity, the contact window duration (\(\sim\)7 minutes for a 600 km orbit at 45\(^\circ\) minimum elevation), and the allocated bandwidth in the Earth Exploration Satellite Service (EESS) at X-band. With 400 MHz of allocated bandwidth (EESS allocation per ITU-R SA.1026-4) and 8PSK 2/3 (spectral efficiency 2 bit/s/Hz), the maximum throughput is approximately 600 Mbps—well within the link’s capability. The margin surplus could be used to reduce transmit power to 0.002 W (thus conserving spacecraft power), increase altitude to 1200 km, or operate to a much smaller (2 m) ground antenna while maintaining the 150 Mbps link. In practice, X-band EO downlinks are constrained by regulatory bandwidth limits and data volume rather than link budget.

    Example 4: Optical Free-Space Link (LEO Downlink)¶

    Scenario Definition¶

    Optical free-space communication (FSO) links offer orders-of-magnitude higher bandwidth density than radio frequency links at comparable aperture sizes. This example computes the link budget for a 1550 nm downlink from a LEO spacecraft to an optical ground station, representative of missions such as ESA’s LCOT (Laser Communication Terminal) or JAXA’s LLCD-equivalent.

    Scenario: Wavelength \(\lambda = 1550\) nm (\(1.55\times10^{-6}\) m), 10 W transmit laser power, 15 cm transmit telescope on spacecraft, 1 m receive telescope at ground station, 600 km altitude, 45\(^\circ\) elevation angle (slant range \(\approx\)880 km), InGaAs avalanche photodiode (APD) receiver, target 1 Gbps data rate.

    Optical Link Budget Calculation¶

    For a diffraction-limited Gaussian beam from a 15 cm transmit aperture, the far-field beam divergence half-angle is:

    \[\begin{align*} \theta_{\text{div}} &= \frac{2.44\,\lambda}{D_t} = \frac{2.44 \times 1.55 \times 10^{-6}}{0.15} = 25.2~\mu\text{rad} \end{align*}\]

    The transmit telescope gain (analogous to antenna gain) with aperture efficiency \(\eta_t = 0.8\):

    \[\begin{align*} G_t &= \eta_t \left(\frac{\pi D_t}{\lambda}\right)^2 = 0.8 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 0.15}{1.55 \times 10^{-6}}\right)^2 = 0.8 \times (3.038 \times 10^5)^2 \Rightarrow 109.7~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    The geometric (free-space spreading) loss for an optical beam, expressed in dB, using the receive aperture diameter \(D_r = 1\) m:

    \[\begin{align*} L_{\text{geom}} &= -20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{D_r}{\theta_{\text{div}} \cdot d}\right) = -20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{1.0}{25.2 \times 10^{-6} \times 880 \times 10^3}\right) \end{align*}\]
    \[\begin{align*} &= -20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{1.0}{22.2}\right) = +26.9~\text{dB (this is a loss, so $-26.9$~dB)} \end{align*}\]

    Alternatively, using the FSPL formula directly at \(\lambda = 1550\) nm over 880 km:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL}_{\text{opt}} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 880 \times 10^3}{1.55 \times 10^{-6}}\right) = 20\log_{10}(7.136 \times 10^{15}) = 317.1~\text{dB} \end{align*}\]

    The receive telescope gain with \(\eta_r = 0.7\):

    \[\begin{align*} G_r &= \eta_r \left(\frac{\pi D_r}{\lambda}\right)^2 = 0.7 \times \left(\frac{\pi \times 1.0}{1.55 \times 10^{-6}}\right)^2 = 0.7 \times 4.112 \times 10^{12} \Rightarrow 125.6~\text{dBi} \end{align*}\]

    For a cooled InGaAs APD receiver with sensitivity of \(-\)58 dBm (2.5 pW per bit) at 1 Gbps (BER \(10^{-9}\)), the minimum required received power is:

    \[\begin{align*} P_{\text{req}} &= -58~\text{dBm} = -88~\text{dBW} \quad \text{(at 1 Gbps)} \end{align*}\]

    Interpretation¶

    The 4.2 dB clear-sky margin for a 1 Gbps optical link from 600 km is achievable but tight, reflecting the fundamental challenge of optical LEO communications: atmospheric turbulence and cloud blockage impose deep fades that require either: (1) optical ground station networks (diversity between multiple OGSs eliminates cloud; turbulence is site-specific); (2) adaptive optics at the ground telescope to pre-compensate the wavefront distortion induced by the atmosphere; or (3) more sensitive detectors (superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors, SNSPDs, provide 10–15 dB sensitivity advantage over APDs, but require cooling to \(<\)4 K).

    DSOC on the Psyche mission (Section 31.4) uses an SNSPD at Palomar for exactly this reason: the sensitivity improvement allows photon-counting communication from hundreds of millions of kilometres with a 22 cm transmit aperture. For LEO commercial applications, the trade between link budget, OGS network coverage, and APT (Acquisition, Pointing, and Tracking) complexity will determine the practical data rates achievable with 5–15 cm spacecraft apertures.

    Link Budget Summary Formulae¶

    Key Equations¶

    The following equations encapsulate the complete link budget calculation chain. All quantities are in SI units unless subscript dB, dBW, dBi, dBK, or dBHz is specified.

    Complete Link Budget Formula Set

    Free-space path loss:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} &= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right) = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) - 147.55 \end{align*}\]

    where \(d\) is in metres, \(f\) in Hz, and the constant \(-147.55 = 20\log_{10}(4\pi/c)\).

    Effective Isotropic Radiated Power:

    \[\begin{align*} \text{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} &= P_{t,\text{dBW}} + G_{t,\text{dBi}} - L_{tx,\text{dB}} \end{align*}\]

    Carrier-to-noise density ratio:

    \[\begin{align*} \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dBHz}} &= \text{EIRP}_{\text{dBW}} - \text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} - L_{\text{atm,dB}} - L_{\text{misc,dB}} + \left(\frac{G}{T}\right)_{\text{dB/K}} + 228.6 \end{align*}\]

    Energy-per-bit to noise density:

    \[\begin{align*} \left(\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dB}} &= \left(\frac{C}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dBHz}} - 10\log_{10}(R_b) \end{align*}\]

    Shannon channel capacity:

    \[\begin{align*} C &= B\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{C/N_0}{B}\right)~\text{bit/s} \end{align*}\]

    Antenna gain from aperture:

    \[\begin{align*} G_{\text{dBi}} &= 10\log_{10}\!\left[\eta\left(\frac{\pi D f}{c}\right)^2\right] \end{align*}\]

    System noise temperature:

    \[\begin{align*} T_{\text{sys}} &= T_{\text{ant}} + T_{\text{feed}} + T_{\text{LNA}} + \frac{T_{\text{post-LNA}}}{G_{\text{LNA}}}~[\text{K}] \end{align*}\]

    Noise Figure to Noise Temperature Conversion¶

    The noise figure \(F\) (linear, dimensionless) of a device at physical temperature \(T_0 = 290\) K is related to the equivalent noise temperature \(T_e\) by:

    \[\begin{align*} T_e &= T_0(F - 1) = 290(F - 1)~\text{K} \end{align*}\]

    Satellite Link Budget Design Rules of Thumb

    • For GEO Ka-band: design for at least 10 dB clear-sky margin to accommodate rain fade without ACM.

    • For LEO S/X-band TT&C: design for at least 15 dB margin at worst-case elevation to ensure reliable command.

    • For optical links: diversity among 3 ground stations at \(>\)500 km separation reduces cloud outage probability below 1%.

    • Every 3 dB increase in EIRP or \(G/T\) doubles the achievable data rate (Shannon) or halves the required bandwidth.

    • Rule of thumb FSPL: \(\text{FSPL}_{\text{dB}} \approx 92.4 + 20\log_{10}(f_{\text{GHz}}) + 20\log_{10}(d_{\text{km}})\) (exact for SI: constant \(= 92.44\)).

    • System noise temperature: a 1 dB LNA noise figure adds only 75 K; a 0.3 dB LNA adds only 21 K. LNA quality matters.
