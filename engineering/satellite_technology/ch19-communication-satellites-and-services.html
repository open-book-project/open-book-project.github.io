    Chapter 19: Communication Satellites and Services¶

    Communication satellites represent the largest commercial segment of the space industry, enabling global telecommunications, broadcasting, broadband internet, and maritime and aeronautical connectivity. From the first experimental TELSTAR 1 (1962) to today’s high-throughput satellites (HTS) delivering hundreds of gigabits per second, the evolution of satellite communications has been driven by advances in transponder technology, antenna design, modulation schemes, and orbital architectures. This chapter presents the engineering principles underlying GEO communication satellite design, transponder performance, frequency reuse strategies, HTS architectures, direct broadcast satellite systems, VSAT networks, and emerging LEO broadband constellations (Maral et al. 2009; Pratt, T., Bostian, C., Allnutt, J. 2002; Elbert, B.R. 2008).

    19.1 GEO Communication Satellite Architecture¶

    19.1.1 Historical Development and Major Operators¶

    Geostationary Earth orbit (GEO) at approximately 35,786 km altitude remains the dominant orbit for commercial communication satellites because a single satellite provides continuous coverage of nearly one-third of Earth’s surface. The concept was proposed by Arthur C. Clarke in 1945, and the first operational GEO communication satellite, INTELSAT I (Early Bird), was launched in 1965 providing 240 telephone circuits or one television channel across the Atlantic.

    By 2025, several hundred operational GEO communication satellites occupy the Clarke belt, operated by companies including Intelsat, SES, Eutelsat, Inmarsat, ViaSat, Hughes Network Systems, and numerous national operators. The geostationary arc is managed through International Telecommunication Union (ITU) coordination to avoid interference between co-frequency satellites.

    Key Operators and Fleets (2025)

    • Intelsat: C-band/Ku-band global coverage, Galaxy/Intelsat Epic series (Intelsat was acquired by SES in 2025)

    • SES: GEO and MEO (O3b) satellites, Astra/NSS/SES series, SES-17 HTS

    • Eutelsat: GEO satellites plus the OneWeb LEO constellation, Ku/Ka-band, Konnect series HTS

    • ViaSat: ViaSat-1/2/3 Ka-band HTS, focus on broadband

    • Hughes: EchoStar/Jupiter series HTS, HughesNet service

    • Inmarsat: L/Ka-band mobile satcom, BGAN/GX fleet

    19.1.2 Spacecraft Bus Architecture¶

    A modern GEO communication satellite comprises two primary elements: the satellite bus (platform) providing structural support, power, attitude control, propulsion, thermal management, and telemetry; and the payload (communications mission equipment) comprising transponders, antennas, and associated electronics.

    Typical mass ranges from 2,000 kg to 7,000 kg at launch (including \(\sim\)40–50% propellant), with end-of-life (EOL) dry mass between 1,000 and 3,000 kg. Solar array power at BOL ranges from 5 kW (small satellites) to 25 kW or more for large HTS platforms. Design lifetime is typically 15–18 years, limited primarily by propellant consumption for station-keeping and battery degradation.

    The spacecraft must maintain geostationary position within \(\pm0.05^\circ\) to \(\pm0.1^\circ\) in both longitude (east-west) and latitude (north-south). Station-keeping maneuvers consume approximately 42 m/s per year for north-south and 2 m/s per year for east-west control, yielding a total \(\Delta v\) budget of approximately \(650\)–\(700\) m/s over 15 years.

    \[\Delta v_{\mathrm{total}} \approx \left(\dot{v}_{NS} + \dot{v}_{EW}\right) \times T_{\mathrm{life}}\tag{19.1}\]

    where \(\dot{v}_{NS} \approx 42\) m/s/yr and \(\dot{v}_{EW} \approx 2\) m/s/yr for a near-circular GEO orbit.

    19.2 Transponder Design and Performance¶

    19.2.1 Bent-Pipe Transponder Architecture¶

    The classic bent-pipe (or transparent) transponder receives uplink signals, frequency-converts them to the downlink band, amplifies them, and retransmits without on-board signal processing. A standard C-band transponder chain consists of:

    1. Receive antenna (uplink 5.925–6.425 GHz)

    2. Low-noise amplifier (LNA) — typical noise figure 1.5–2.5 dB

    3. Input multiplexer (IMUX) — channelizes the broadband received signal

    4. Frequency converter (upconverter/downconverter) — translates to downlink band (3.7–4.2 GHz)

    5. High-power amplifier (HPA) — TWTA or SSPA

    6. Output multiplexer (OMUX) — recombines channels

    7. Transmit antenna (downlink)

    For Ku-band, uplink is 14.0–14.5 GHz and downlink is 10.95–12.75 GHz. For Ka-band, uplink is 27.5–31.0 GHz and downlink is 17.7–21.2 GHz (FSS) or 18.3–18.8 GHz (BSS uplink 17.3–17.8 GHz).

    19.2.2 Traveling Wave Tube Amplifiers (TWTA)¶

    The Traveling Wave Tube Amplifier remains the dominant HPA technology for GEO communication satellites due to its high power efficiency at microwave frequencies. A TWTA comprises an electron gun, a helix slow-wave structure, and a collector. The electron beam interacts with the RF signal propagating along the helix, achieving gain through velocity modulation and energy transfer.

    Key performance parameters:

    • Saturated output power: 40–300 W per tube (Ku-band), up to 500 W (C-band)

    • Small-signal gain: 40–55 dB

    • DC-to-RF efficiency: 65–75% at saturation (depressed collector designs)

    • Operating bandwidth: 500 MHz (typical transponder)

    • AM/AM conversion: nonlinear gain compression

    • AM/PM conversion: phase shift as function of drive level

    The TWTA output back-off (OBO) and input back-off (IBO) characterize operating point relative to saturation:

    \[\mathrm{OBO} = P_{\mathrm{sat}} - P_{\mathrm{out}} \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{19.2}\]
    \[\mathrm{IBO} = P_{\mathrm{in,sat}} - P_{\mathrm{in}} \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{19.3}\]

    For multi-carrier operation (MCPC or FDMA), the amplifier must operate at substantial back-off (typically 5–8 dB OBO) to limit intermodulation distortion (IMD). The third-order intercept point (OIP3) relates to IM3 product levels:

    \[\mathrm{IM3} = 2(P_{\mathrm{OIP3}} - P_{\mathrm{out}}) \quad [\mathrm{dBc}]\tag{19.4}\]

    Linearizers preceding the TWT reduce AM/AM and AM/PM distortion, enabling operation 2–3 dB closer to saturation while maintaining acceptable IM levels. Pre-distortion linearizers are analog or digital circuits that apply the inverse of the TWTA nonlinearity.

    19.2.3 Solid-State Power Amplifiers (SSPA)¶

    SSPAs based on Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) or Gallium Nitride (GaN) pseudomorphic high-electron-mobility transistors (pHEMTs) are increasingly used for lower-power applications and HTS spot beam amplifiers. Advantages over TWTAs:

    • Higher reliability (no moving parts, no high-voltage electron gun)

    • Lower power supply voltage requirements

    • Better linearity without back-off (GaN devices)

    • Suitable for per-beam amplification in multibeam payloads

    GaN SSPAs achieve 40–60% power-added efficiency (PAE) at Ku/Ka-band, compared to 65–75% for optimized TWTAs with depressed collectors. Typical SSPA output powers range from 2–20 W for spot beam applications.

    \[\eta_{\mathrm{PAE}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{out}} - P_{\mathrm{in}}}{P_{\mathrm{DC}}} \times 100\%\tag{19.5}\]

    19.2.4 Noise Temperature and G/T¶

    The satellite receive figure of merit \(G/T\) (dB/K) characterizes the uplink receive chain:

    \[G/T = G_{\mathrm{rx}} - 10\log_{10}(T_{\mathrm{sys}}) \quad [\mathrm{dB/K}]\tag{19.6}\]

    where \(G_{\mathrm{rx}}\) is the receive antenna gain (dBi) and the system noise temperature:

    \[T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{ant}} + T_{\mathrm{LNA}} + \frac{T_{\mathrm{converter}}}{G_{\mathrm{LNA}}} + \cdots \quad [\mathrm{K}]\tag{19.7}\]

    Typical GEO satellite \(G/T\) values range from \(-3\) to \(+8\) dB/K for global beams and \(+8\) to \(+20\) dB/K for high-gain spot beams.

    19.3 Frequency Reuse Techniques¶

    19.3.1 Spatial Isolation¶

    Frequency reuse through spatial beam separation allows the same frequency band to be used simultaneously by multiple beams serving different geographic areas. The carrier-to-interference ratio (C/I) arising from beam overlap must be sufficient for acceptable link performance.

    For two cochannel beams separated by angle \(\theta\), the C/I is determined by the antenna discrimination pattern. For a parabolic reflector with Gaussian beam approximation:

    \[\frac{C}{I} = \frac{G(\theta=0)}{G(\theta_{\mathrm{sep}})} = \exp\!\left(\frac{2.77 \theta_{\mathrm{sep}}^2}{\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}^2}\right)\tag{19.8}\]

    where \(\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}\) is the half-power beamwidth. Practical multibeam designs target \(C/I > 25\) dB for QPSK and \(C/I > 30\) dB for higher-order modulations such as 8PSK or 16APSK.

    The antenna discrimination between spot beams is typically 25–35 dB for beams separated by 2–3 beamwidths, enabling 4-color or even 2-color frequency reuse schemes.

    19.3.2 Polarization Reuse¶

    Orthogonal polarizations (linear horizontal/vertical or left/right circular) provide an additional frequency reuse factor of 2. Cross-polarization isolation (XPI) must exceed 25–30 dB for acceptable interference levels. Rain depolarization in Ku and Ka bands degrades XPI, requiring fade margin or uplink power control (ULPC).

    The overall frequency reuse factor combining spatial and polarization techniques:

    \[F_{\mathrm{reuse}} = N_{\mathrm{beams}} \times N_{\mathrm{pol}} / N_{\mathrm{colors}}\tag{19.9}\]

    For a 4-color scheme with 2 polarizations and 72 beams: \(F_{\mathrm{reuse}} = 72 \times 2 / 4 = 36\).

    19.3.3 Interference Budget¶

    The total interference budget in a multibeam system includes:

    • Co-channel interference (CCI): from other beams using the same frequency

    • Adjacent channel interference (ACI): from imperfect IMUX/OMUX filtering

    • Cross-polarization interference (XPI): from polarization leakage

    • Terrestrial interference: from co-frequency terrestrial systems (C-band)

    The total \(C/(N+I)\) combining noise and interference:

    \[\frac{C}{N+I} = \left[\frac{N_0}{C} + \frac{I_{\mathrm{total}}}{C}\right]^{-1}\tag{19.10}\]

    19.4 High-Throughput Satellite (HTS) Architecture¶

    19.4.1 HTS Design Principles¶

    High-throughput satellites employ aggressive frequency reuse through large numbers of narrow spot beams, achieving system throughput 50–100 times greater than conventional wide-beam satellites for the same allocated spectrum. Key architectural features:

    • Multispot beam antenna: 50–500+ narrow beams (0.3\(^\circ\)–1\(^\circ\) diameter) covering the service area

    • Aggressive frequency reuse: same Ka-band spectrum reused 50–100+ times across the beam pattern

    • High EIRP per beam: 60–70 dBW per spot beam enables small user terminals

    • Gateway ground infrastructure: multiple large gateway stations aggregate traffic

    • On-board processing (OBP): some HTS use digital transparent processors for switching

    19.4.2 Spot Beam Frequency Assignment¶

    For a 4-color frequency reuse plan, the Ka-band user downlink (17.7–20.2 GHz, 2.5 GHz) is divided into 4 sub-bands of 625 MHz each, with 2 polarizations. Each color cell uses one sub-band/polarization combination. The frequency reuse factor:

    \[F_r = \frac{N_{\mathrm{beams}}}{N_{\mathrm{colors}}} = \frac{500}{4} = 125\tag{19.11}\]

    Total system capacity with 500 beams at 1 Gbps per beam:

    \[C_{\mathrm{total}} = N_{\mathrm{beams}} \times C_{\mathrm{beam}} = 500 \times 1~\mathrm{Gbps} = 500~\mathrm{Gbps}\tag{19.12}\]

    Spectral efficiency per beam depends on the modulation/coding scheme and operating \(E_b/N_0\). Modern HTS with adaptive coding and modulation (ACM) achieve 3–5 bit/s/Hz, yielding per-beam capacities of 1.5–3 Gbps over 500–600 MHz.

    19.4.3 Gateway Architecture¶

    HTS gateways connect the satellite spot beams to the terrestrial internet backbone. Each gateway serves multiple spot beams, with the gateway transmitting uplink on dedicated gateway beams (feeder links) separate from the user link beams. Typical gateway characteristics:

    • Antenna diameter: 4.5–13 m for Ka-band feeder links

    • Uplink EIRP: 80–90 dBW (high-power amplifiers 200–2000 W)

    • Site diversity (2–4 sites per gateway cluster) to mitigate rain fade

    • Aggregate gateway throughput: 10–50 Gbps per gateway cluster

    • Number of gateways: 8–50 per HTS system

    Smart gateway diversity switches traffic between diverse gateway sites when one site experiences heavy rain fade, maintaining link availability without user-link degradation.

    SES-17¶

    SES-17, launched in October 2021 on Ariane 5 ECA, is a 200 Gbps Ka-band HTS built on the Thales Alenia Space Spacebus Neo platform. Key features include a fully digital transparent processor (DTP) enabling flexible frequency and power allocation across 200 spot beams serving the Americas and Atlantic Ocean region. The satellite targets aviation connectivity (inflight entertainment) and maritime services.

    ViaSat-3¶

    The ViaSat-3 constellation (3 satellites in GEO) aims to deliver 1 Tbps capacity per satellite using a massive array of narrow spot beams and 4-color frequency reuse. ViaSat-3 F1 (Americas) was launched in April 2023 on Falcon Heavy, but an antenna reflector deployment anomaly reduced its capacity well below the design value. The architecture uses advanced reflector antennas with 1,000+ beams.

    19.4.5 Multibeam Antenna Design¶

    Multibeam antennas for HTS employ either:

    1. Multiple feed array reflector (MFAR): single large reflector illuminated by an array of feed horns, each producing a separate beam. Economic but limited flexibility.

    2. Direct radiating array (DRA): planar phased array producing beams electronically, maximum flexibility.

    3. Multiple reflectors: 2–4 separate reflectors each handling a subset of beams.

    The beamwidth of a circular aperture of diameter \(D\) at wavelength \(\lambda\):

    \[\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{70\lambda}{D} \quad [\mathrm{degrees}]\tag{19.13}\]

    For a 2.8 m reflector at Ka-band (20 GHz, \(\lambda = 15\) mm):

    \[\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{70 \times 0.015}{2.8} \approx 0.375^\circ\tag{19.14}\]

    A \(0.375^\circ\) beam covers approximately \(220 \times 220\) km at GEO, suitable for regional HTS coverage.

    19.5 Direct Broadcast Satellite (DBS) Systems¶

    19.5.1 DBS Architecture and Regulatory Framework¶

    Direct broadcast satellite (DBS) services transmit high-power Ku or Ka-band signals directly to consumer dish antennas of 45–90 cm diameter. DBS satellites operate in the BSS (Broadcasting Satellite Service) frequency bands allocated by the ITU, with high EIRP (\(>\)55 dBW) enabling small consumer dishes.

    Regulatory planning in ITU Region 2 (Americas) allocates specific orbital positions and frequency assignments to each country under the BSS plan (ITU Radio Regulations, Appendix 30). In Region 1 (Europe/Africa), the WARC-77 plan similarly coordinates orbital positions.

    Major DBS systems include:

    • DirecTV (USA): 32\(^\circ\)W, 99\(^\circ\)W, 101\(^\circ\)W, 103\(^\circ\)W, 110\(^\circ\)W positions; Ku/Ka-band; \(>\)24 million subscribers

    • DISH Network (USA): 61.5\(^\circ\)W, 72.7\(^\circ\)W, 77\(^\circ\)W; Ku/Ka-band; EchoStar fleet

    • BSkyB/Sky UK: Astra 2 satellites at 28.2\(^\circ\)E; Ku-band; \(>\)11 million subscribers

    • Astra/SES (Europe): 19.2\(^\circ\)E (German-speaking market); Ku/Ka-band; 125+ million households

    • BSAT (Japan): BS orbital positions; 110\(^\circ\)E; 12.2–12.75 GHz

    • TeleSat (Canada): Anik satellites; Ku-band

    19.5.2 DVB-S2 and DVB-S2X Standards¶

    The Second Generation Digital Video Broadcasting via Satellite (DVB-S2) standard (ETSI EN 302 307) was published in 2005 and superseded the original DVB-S standard. DVB-S2X (extended) was published in 2014 (ETSI EN 302 307-2), introducing additional MODCODs and narrower roll-offs for improved spectral efficiency.

    DVB-S2 key features:

    • Modulation: QPSK, 8PSK, 16APSK, 32APSK

    • Forward error correction (FEC): concatenated LDPC + BCH

    • LDPC code lengths: 64,800 bits (normal) or 16,200 bits (short)

    • Code rates: 1/4, 1/3, 2/5, 1/2, 3/5, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5, 5/6, 8/9, 9/10

    • Roll-off: 0.20, 0.25, 0.35

    • Adaptive coding and modulation (ACM) mode: per-frame MODCOD selection

    • Spectral efficiency: 0.49–4.53 bit/s/Hz

    DVB-S2X enhancements:

    • Additional modulations: 64APSK, 128APSK, 256APSK

    • Finer granularity code rates and additional MODCODs

    • Narrower roll-offs: 0.05, 0.10, 0.15 (5–15% improvement in spectral efficiency)

    • Super-frame structure for channel bonding and synchronization

    • Linear pre-compensation (LPC) for satellite nonlinearity

    • Extended bandwidth (500 MHz MCPC channels)

    Adaptive Coding and Modulation¶

    In ACM mode, the MODCOD is selected per-frame (or per-user in MIS mode) based on the instantaneous channel condition reported by the receiver. The spectral efficiency gain of ACM over constant coding and modulation (CCM):

    \[\eta_{\mathrm{ACM}} = \frac{\sum_i f_i \eta_i}{\eta_{\mathrm{CCM}}}\tag{19.15}\]

    where \(f_i\) is the fraction of time spent in MODCOD \(i\) and \(\eta_i\) is its spectral efficiency. ACM systems can achieve 20–50% throughput improvement over CCM in variable rain fade environments.

    19.5.3 DTH Services: Encryption and Conditional Access¶

    Direct-to-home (DTH) services use conditional access systems (CAS) to restrict viewing to authorized subscribers. The CAS comprises:

    • Subscriber Management System (SMS): manages subscriptions and entitlements

    • Conditional Access System (CAS): generates and distributes encrypted keys

    • Smart card or software CA: stores the decryption key hierarchy in the receiver

    The DVB Common Scrambling Algorithm (DVB-CSA) or newer AES-128 based DVB-CSA3 encrypts MPEG transport streams at the PID level. The key hierarchy:

    1. Control word (CW): 64-bit key, changed every 10 s (crypto period)

    2. Service key: encrypts CW in entitlement control message (ECM)

    3. Operator key: protects entitlement management messages (EMM)

    4. Personal distribution key (PDK): subscriber-specific, stored in smart card

    Major CAS providers include Nagravision (NagraVision 3), Irdeto, Viaccess-Orca, Conax, and CISCO VideoGuard. DirecTV uses DIRECTV’s NDS VideoGuard system.

    19.5.4 Video Compression Standards¶

    DTH services use standardized video compression to maximize the number of channels per transponder:

    • MPEG-2: 4–8 Mbps per SD channel; legacy standard, widely deployed

    • MPEG-4 AVC (H.264): 2–5 Mbps SD, 4–8 Mbps HD; 2\(\times\) MPEG-2 efficiency

    • HEVC (H.265): 2–4 Mbps HD, 8–15 Mbps UHD/4K; 2\(\times\) H.264 efficiency

    • AV1: open-source alternative to HEVC, similar compression ratio

    A 36 MHz Ku-band transponder operating at QPSK 3/4 (DVB-S2) provides approximately 55 Mbps, accommodating:

    • 7–14 HD channels (MPEG-4, 4–8 Mbps each)

    • 3–7 UHD/4K channels (HEVC, 8–15 Mbps each)

    19.6 VSAT Networks¶

    19.6.1 VSAT System Architecture¶

    Very Small Aperture Terminal (VSAT) networks provide two-way satellite communications using small (0.6–2.4 m) dish antennas at remote sites. Applications include corporate enterprise networks, rural broadband, ATM connectivity, point-of-sale terminals, and government/military communications.

    Hub-and-spoke topology: A large hub station (3.7–7 m antenna) provides the network control point. Outbound (hub-to-VSAT) traffic uses a Time Division Multiplex (TDM) broadcast carrier. Inbound (VSAT-to-hub) traffic uses Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) or Multi-Frequency TDMA (MF-TDMA).

    Mesh topology: VSATs communicate directly with each other through the satellite without hub intervention. Each VSAT requires higher EIRP and G/T. Suitable for point-to-point circuits and VoIP.

    19.6.2 Return Link Multiple Access¶

    TDMA assigns time slots to VSATs within a single shared carrier. TDMA burst rate is much higher than the average data rate, requiring burst buffers:

    \[R_{\mathrm{burst}} = R_{\mathrm{avg}} \times \frac{T_{\mathrm{frame}}}{T_{\mathrm{slot}}}\tag{19.16}\]

    MF-TDMA divides the return link bandwidth into multiple frequency sub-bands, each carrying TDMA bursts. VSATs may be assigned to different carriers depending on load. iDirect platforms (now ST Engineering iDirect) use MF-TDMA with SCPC fallback for high-demand sites.

    Demand-Assigned Multiple Access (DAMA) dynamically assigns capacity in response to traffic demand, improving efficiency compared to Fixed Assignment Multiple Access (FAMA).

    19.6.3 iDirect and HughesNet Platforms¶

    iDirect (ST Engineering): Industry-leading VSAT platform using MF-TDMA return link, DVB-S2 ACM outbound. iQ platform adds DVB-S2X and supports HTS spot beam operation. Typical remote terminal: 0.76–1.2 m antenna, 1–4 W BUC.

    HughesNet/Hughes EchoStar: JUPITER system uses Ka-band HTS (Jupiter-1 and Jupiter-3 satellites), proprietary waveform on return link, DVB-S2 forward link. Delivers consumer broadband at 25–100 Mbps receive rate. EchoStar XIX (Jupiter-2) provided 220 Gbps capacity.

    19.7 Broadband Satellite Internet: LEO Constellations¶

    19.7.1 Motivation: LEO vs GEO Latency¶

    The fundamental limitation of GEO satellite systems for interactive broadband is the propagation delay. The one-way propagation time from ground to GEO satellite:

    \[t_{\mathrm{GEO}} = \frac{d_{\mathrm{GEO}}}{c} = \frac{35{,}786~\mathrm{km}}{3 \times 10^5~\mathrm{km/s}} \approx 119~\mathrm{ms}\tag{19.17}\]

    The round-trip time (RTT) for a GEO bent-pipe link (user-satellite-hub-satellite-user):

    \[\mathrm{RTT}_{\mathrm{GEO}} \approx 4 \times 119~\mathrm{ms} = 476~\mathrm{ms} \quad \text{(+ processing)}\tag{19.18}\]

    In practice, GEO satellite broadband exhibits RTT of 550–650 ms, severely impacting real-time applications (VoIP, gaming, video conferencing).

    For a LEO constellation at 550 km altitude:

    \[t_{\mathrm{LEO}} = \frac{550~\mathrm{km}}{c} \approx 1.8~\mathrm{ms}\tag{19.19}\]

    With inter-satellite links (ISL) or ground gateway routing, Starlink achieves RTT of 20–50 ms, comparable to terrestrial broadband.

    Parameter GEO (ViaSat-3) LEO (Starlink)
    Orbital altitude 35,786 km 540–570 km
    One-way delay (zenith) \(\sim\)119 ms \(\sim\)2 ms
    RTT (typical) 550–650 ms 20–50 ms

    User terminal dish or flat panel electronically steered flat panel
    Free-space path loss (Ka, 20 GHz, zenith) 209.5 dB 173.3 dB

    19.7.2 Starlink¶

    SpaceX’s Starlink constellation, whose first production satellites were launched in 2019 and which began public beta service in 2020, is the most rapidly deployed satellite broadband system to date. Key technical parameters:

    • Orbital shells: 540 km (Gen1), 570 km (Gen1), 340 km (Gen2 planned)

    • Frequency: Ku-band (user link: 10.7–12.7 GHz downlink, 14.0–14.5 GHz uplink), Ka-band (gateway), E-band (backhaul)

    • Inter-satellite links (ISL): laser links on later Gen1 and on Gen2 satellites

    • User terminal: electronically steered phased array (flat panel)

    • Satellite mass: \(\sim\)260 kg (Gen1), \(\sim\)800 kg (Gen2)

    • Constellation size: 4,408 (Gen1 authorized) + 7,500 (Gen2 authorized); more than 7,000 in orbit in 2025

    • User performance: typically tens to a few hundred Mbps receive, with latency of a few tens of ms

    • Subscribers: several million (company statements, as of 2025)

    19.7.3 OneWeb¶

    OneWeb (now part of Eutelsat) completed its first-generation constellation of 648 satellites (about 650 in orbit with spares), planned in near-polar orbits at an altitude of about 1,200 km, providing Ku-band connectivity primarily for enterprise, government, and maritime customers. OneWeb uses gateway stations operated at teleports around the world.

    Key characteristics:

    • Altitude: 1,200 km (higher than Starlink, lower latency than GEO)

    • Coverage: Polar-inclusive global coverage

    • Latency: higher propagation delay than at 550 km but far lower than GEO

    • User link: Ku-band; Gateway: Ka-band

    • Target markets: maritime, aviation, enterprise, government

    19.7.4 Amazon Kuiper¶

    Amazon’s Project Kuiper (FCC license: 3,236 satellites at 590–630 km altitude) targets mass-market consumer broadband using Ka-band communications. Two prototype satellites were launched in October 2023 and the first production satellites in 2025.

    19.8 Maritime and Aviation Satellite Communications¶

    19.8.1 Inmarsat BGAN and Fleet Systems¶

    Inmarsat operates a global L-band network (1.5–1.6 GHz) using GEO satellites (I-4 and I-5 fleet) for mobile satellite services (MSS). The Broadband Global Area Network (BGAN) provides:

    • Up to 492 kbps streaming IP (land portable terminals)

    • Near-global coverage (excluding the polar regions)

    • ISDN circuits and voice services

    • Terminal sizes: laptop-sized (Inmarsat-C), briefcase (BGAN), maritime dome

    Inmarsat Fleet Xpress (FX) combines L-band (Global Xpress via I-5 Ka-band) with a backup L-band link, providing multi-Mbps broadband to maritime vessels.

    19.8.2 Inflight Connectivity (IFC)¶

    Aviation satcom delivers in-flight broadband to commercial aircraft. Three main architectures:

    1. GEO Ku-band: ViaSat IFC uses Ku-band spot beams; Gogo 2Ku; speeds of the order of tens of Mbps per aircraft

    2. GEO Ka-band: ViaSat-3 Ka IFC; SES/O3b for short-haul routes; typically higher speeds per aircraft than Ku-band

    3. LEO: Starlink Aviation (introduced 2023) with low latency; electronically steered antenna (ESA)

    Aircraft antennas must be low-profile aerodynamic radomes. Mechanically steered Ku-band antennas weigh 15–25 kg; flat-panel ESAs for LEO links weigh 10–15 kg with \(<\)25 mm height.

    19.9 Satellite News Gathering and Professional Services¶

    19.9.1 SNG Systems¶

    Satellite News Gathering (SNG) refers to the use of portable/fly-away satellite uplink systems for live broadcast from remote locations. SNG systems typically employ:

    • Antenna: 0.9–2.4 m deployable offset-fed reflector

    • Uplink frequency: Ku-band (14.0–14.5 GHz) or C-band (5.925–6.425 GHz)

    • HPA: 25–400 W SSPA or TWTA

    • Video encoder: HEVC or H.264, 2–20 Mbps depending on quality

    • DVB-S2 modulation for satellite uplink

    • Setup time: 2–15 minutes

    SCPC (Single Channel Per Carrier) provides dedicated bandwidth for each SNG link, while MCPC (Multiple Channels Per Carrier) multiplexes several feeds on a shared carrier, managed by a hub station.

    19.9.2 SCPC vs MCPC¶

    SCPC: Each carrier occupies a dedicated bandwidth slice of the transponder. Advantages: simple link, no hub required, low latency. Disadvantages: inefficient for bursty traffic, each carrier has overhead.

    MCPC: Multiple program channels are statistically multiplexed (MPEG transport stream) on a single carrier. A headend multiplexer combines channels and outputs a single DVB carrier. Advantages: higher efficiency (statistical multiplexing gain 20–40%), easier management.

    Statistical multiplexing gain:

    \[G_{\mathrm{StatMux}} = \frac{N \cdot R_{\mathrm{peak}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \bar{R}_i} \approx 1.2 \text{ to } 1.4\tag{19.20}\]

    19.10 Interference Management and ITU Coordination¶

    19.10.1 ITU Orbital Coordination¶

    The ITU Radio Regulations govern satellite network coordination through two processes:

    1. Advance Publication Information (API): published up to 7 years (and at least 2 years) before the planned date of bringing the network into use, establishes the initial claim

    2. Coordination: Bilateral coordination between potentially interfering networks (for example, GSO networks within about \(6^\circ\) of each other)

    3. Notification: Filed with the ITU to record the frequency assignments in the Master International Frequency Register after coordination

    For non-geostationary systems, the limits on equivalent power flux density (EPFD) in Article 22 of the Radio Regulations protect geostationary networks.

    19.10.2 Carrier-to-Interference Calculations¶

    The C/I from an interfering satellite at angular separation \(\Delta\phi\) from the desired satellite:

    \[\frac{C}{I} = \frac{G_t(\phi_t)}{G_t(\Delta\phi)} \cdot \frac{P_t G_s}{P_i G_i} \cdot \frac{d_i^2}{d_s^2}\tag{19.21}\]

    For GSO satellite spacing, the receive earth station antenna discrimination against an adjacent satellite at angle \(\Delta\phi\):

    \[G(\Delta\phi) = 32 - 25\log_{10}(\Delta\phi) \quad \text{dBi, for } \Delta\phi > \phi_{\min}\tag{19.22}\]

    where \(\phi_{\min} = 20\lambda/D\) (degrees) per ITU-R S.580.

    19.10.3 HTS Capacity Economics¶

    The commercial viability of HTS systems is driven by the cost per Gbps of capacity, which has fallen by large factors from the first HTS generation to the latest ones (published cost-per-bit figures differ between sources and are not quoted here).

    An illustrative exponential model of the capacity cost trend is:

    \[C_{\mathrm{capacity}}(t) \approx C_0 \cdot 10^{-\alpha(t-t_0)}\tag{19.23}\]

    where, for example, \(\alpha = 0.08\) year\(^{-1}\) corresponds to about a 17% annual decline (an assumed value for illustration).

    19.11 Summary¶

    Communication satellites remain the backbone of global telecommunications infrastructure, with GEO satellites providing broadcast, VSAT, and trunking services while HTS systems deliver broadband at costs approaching terrestrial alternatives. The DVB-S2X standard provides spectral efficiency from 0.49 to nearly 6 bit/s/Hz through adaptive coding and modulation. High-throughput satellites employing aggressive spot-beam frequency reuse deliver 100 Gbps to 1 Tbps of system capacity from a single GEO position. LEO broadband constellations (Starlink, OneWeb, Kuiper) address the latency limitation of GEO systems, achieving round-trip times of 20–50 ms compared to 550–650 ms for GEO. Maritime and aviation connectivity markets are growing rapidly as LEO systems enable high-bandwidth IFC at prices competitive with GEO solutions (Maral et al. 2009; Pratt, T., Bostian, C., Allnutt, J. 2002; Elbert, B.R. 2008).
