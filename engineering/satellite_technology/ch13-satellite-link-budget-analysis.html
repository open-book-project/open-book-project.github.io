    Chapter 13: Satellite Link Budget Analysis¶

    Key Concepts: Effective Isotropic Radiated Power (EIRP), Free Space Path Loss (FSPL), Figure of Merit (\(G/T\)), Carrier-to-Noise Ratio (\(C/N\)), \(E_b/N_0\), System Noise Temperature, Atmospheric Attenuation, Rain Fade Margin, Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM), DVB-S2X.

    13.1 Introduction¶

    The satellite link budget is the fundamental accounting tool of satellite communications engineering. It is a systematic, end-to-end tabulation of all the gains and losses that a signal experiences as it propagates from a transmitter, through the space environment, to a receiver. The result of a link budget calculation is a carrier-to-noise ratio or energy-per-bit-to-noise-density ratio (\(E_b/N_0\)) at the demodulator input, which is then compared against the threshold required to achieve the target bit error rate (BER) (Pratt et al. 2003; Roddy 2006).

    The philosophy of link budget engineering is to account for every decibel. In a resource- constrained satellite system—where every watt of transmit power, every centimeter of antenna aperture, and every kilogram of spacecraft mass comes at extraordinary cost— understanding the precise signal budget is essential. Link margins of even a fraction of a decibel can represent millions of dollars of spacecraft hardware or years of system lifetime.

    This chapter develops the link budget from first principles. We begin with the transmitter and systematically account for each element of the propagation path: antenna gain, free-space spreading loss, atmospheric attenuation, noise contributions, and receiver figure of merit. We conclude with complete worked examples for Ku-band GEO links, high-throughput satellite (HTS) spot beam systems, LEO broadband (Starlink-style), inter-satellite links (ISL), and optical links.

    13.2 Transmitter and Effective Isotropic Radiated Power¶

    13.2.1 Transmit Power and Antenna Gain¶

    The first element of any link budget is the transmitter. A satellite or earth station transmitter produces a radio-frequency signal at power \(P_t\) (Watts). This power is fed to a transmit antenna through a feed network, which may introduce insertion loss \(L_f\) (expressed as a ratio \(\geq 1\), i.e., a loss). The antenna concentrates energy in a preferred direction characterized by its directional gain \(G_t\) (dimensionless ratio relative to an isotropic radiator).

    The Effective Isotropic Radiated Power (EIRP) is defined as the product of the net power delivered to the antenna and the antenna gain in the direction of the target:

    \[\mathrm{EIRP} = \frac{P_t}{L_f} \cdot G_t \quad [\mathrm{W}]\tag{13.1}\]

    Converting to the decibel domain, which is standard practice in link budgeting:

    \[\mathrm{EIRP} \;[\mathrm{dBW}] = P_t \;[\mathrm{dBW}] + G_t \;[\mathrm{dBi}] - L_f \;[\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.2}\]

    where dBi denotes decibels relative to an isotropic antenna. The EIRP represents the power that would need to be transmitted by an isotropic source to produce the same power flux density at the receiver as the actual directional transmitter.

    13.2.2 Aperture Antenna Gain¶

    For a uniformly illuminated circular aperture of diameter \(D\), the gain at wavelength \(\lambda\) is:

    \[G_t = \eta_a \left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2\tag{13.3}\]

    where \(\eta_a\) is the aperture efficiency (typically 0.55–0.70 for parabolic reflectors). In decibels:

    \[G_t \;[\mathrm{dBi}] = 10 \log_{10}(\eta_a) + 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)\tag{13.4}\]

    The half-power beamwidth (HPBW) of a circular aperture antenna is approximately:

    \[\theta_{\mathrm{HPBW}} \approx \frac{70\lambda}{D} \quad [\mathrm{degrees}]\tag{13.5}\]

    This inverse relationship between gain and beamwidth is fundamental: narrow beams provide high gain but stringent pointing requirements.

    13.3 Free Space Path Loss¶

    Once the signal leaves the transmit antenna, it spreads in all directions according to the inverse square law. The free space path loss (FSPL) represents the geometric spreading of the wavefront as it propagates from the transmitter at distance \(d\) with no atmospheric effects.

    The power flux density \(S\) at distance \(d\) from an isotropic source of power \(P\) is:

    \[S = \frac{P}{4\pi d^2} \quad [\mathrm{W/m^2}]\tag{13.6}\]

    A receive antenna with effective area \(A_{\mathrm{eff}} = \lambda^2 G_r / (4\pi)\) intercepts power:

    \[P_r = S \cdot A_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{P \cdot G_r \lambda^2}{(4\pi d)^2}\tag{13.7}\]

    This is the Friis transmission equation. The FSPL is defined as the ratio of transmitted to received power for isotropic antennas (\(G_t = G_r = 1\)):

    \[\mathrm{FSPL} = \left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi d f}{c}\right)^2\tag{13.8}\]

    In decibels:

    \[\mathrm{FSPL} \;[\mathrm{dB}] = 20\log_{10}(4\pi) + 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) - 20\log_{10}(c)\tag{13.9}\]
    \[\boxed{\mathrm{FSPL} \;[\mathrm{dB}] = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}(d \;[\mathrm{km}]) + 20\log_{10}(f \;[\mathrm{GHz}])}\tag{13.10}\]

    For a GEO satellite at \(d = 35{,}786\) km and \(f = 11.7\) GHz:

    \[\mathrm{FSPL} = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}(35{,}786) + 20\log_{10}(11.7) = 92.45 + 91.07 + 21.36 = 205.5 \;\mathrm{dB}\tag{13.11}\]

    This enormous loss—over 205 dB—is the dominant term in any GEO link budget and explains why satellite receivers require extremely low noise figures and large antennas.

    13.4 Received Signal Power¶

    The received signal power in decibels is:

    \[P_r \;[\mathrm{dBW}] = \mathrm{EIRP} - \mathrm{FSPL} + G_r - L_{\mathrm{misc}} \;[\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.12}\]

    where \(L_{\mathrm{misc}}\) encompasses all additional losses:

    \[L_{\mathrm{misc}} = L_{\mathrm{atm}} + L_{\mathrm{rain}} + L_{\mathrm{pol}} + L_{\mathrm{point}} + L_{\mathrm{feed,rx}} \;[\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.13}\]

    • \(L_{\mathrm{atm}}\): gaseous absorption (water vapor + oxygen)

    • \(L_{\mathrm{rain}}\): rain attenuation (dominant impairment at Ku/Ka-band)

    • \(L_{\mathrm{pol}}\): polarization mismatch loss (0–3 dB, typically 0.1–0.5 dB)

    • \(L_{\mathrm{point}}\): antenna pointing error loss

    • \(L_{\mathrm{feed,rx}}\): receive feed and waveguide losses

    13.5 Noise Power and System Noise Temperature¶

    13.5.1 Fundamental Noise Concepts¶

    Thermal noise is the fundamental limit to receiver sensitivity. The available noise power from a resistor at physical temperature \(T\) in bandwidth \(B\) is given by the Nyquist formula:

    \[N = k T_s B \quad [\mathrm{W}]\tag{13.14}\]

    where \(k = 1.3806 \times 10^{-23}\) J/K is Boltzmann’s constant (equivalently \(k = -228.6\) dBW/K/Hz), \(T_s\) is the system noise temperature in Kelvin, and \(B\) is the noise bandwidth in Hz. In decibels:

    \[N \;[\mathrm{dBW}] = k \;[\mathrm{dBW/K/Hz}] + 10\log_{10}(T_s) + 10\log_{10}(B)\tag{13.15}\]
    \[N \;[\mathrm{dBW}] = -228.6 + 10\log_{10}(T_s \;[\mathrm{K}]) + 10\log_{10}(B \;[\mathrm{Hz}])\tag{13.16}\]

    13.5.2 Cascaded Noise Figure and System Noise Temperature¶

    A receive chain consists of multiple components in cascade: antenna, feed waveguide, low-noise amplifier (LNA), bandpass filter, downconverter, IF amplifier, and modem. Each component contributes noise characterized by its noise figure \(F_n = T_n/290 + 1\) where \(T_n\) is the equivalent noise temperature of the component.

    The Friis formula for cascaded noise temperature gives the system noise temperature referred to the antenna output terminal:

    \[T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{ant}} + T_{\mathrm{feed}} + \frac{T_{\mathrm{LNA}}}{G_{\mathrm{feed}}} + \frac{T_2}{G_{\mathrm{feed}} G_{\mathrm{LNA}}} + \frac{T_3}{G_{\mathrm{feed}} G_{\mathrm{LNA}} G_2} + \cdots\tag{13.17}\]

    where \(G_{\mathrm{feed}} = 1/L_f < 1\) for a lossy feed (loss = gain < 1). A lossy passive component at physical temperature \(T_{\mathrm{phys}}\) contributes noise temperature:

    \[T_{\mathrm{comp}} = (L - 1) T_{\mathrm{phys}} \quad [K]\tag{13.18}\]

    where \(L > 1\) is the component loss ratio. The practical implication is that the LNA must come first in the receive chain to minimize the noise temperature contribution of subsequent (noisier) stages.

    13.5.3 Antenna Noise Temperature¶

    The antenna noise temperature \(T_{\mathrm{ant}}\) depends on what the antenna “sees”: the physical temperature of materials in its field of view, weighted by the antenna pattern. Three contributions are important:

    1. Sky (cosmic) background: At frequencies above 3 GHz the cosmic microwave background contributes \(T_{\mathrm{cmb}} = 2.73\) K. Galactic emission adds a frequency-dependent term: \(T_{\mathrm{gal}} \approx 25 (f/408\,\mathrm{MHz})^{-2.75}\) K.

    2. Atmospheric emission: The atmosphere emits thermal noise that couples into the antenna’s main beam. By the reciprocity of Kirchhoff’s law, atmospheric opacity \(\alpha_{\mathrm{atm}}\) at physical temperature \(T_{\mathrm{atm}} \approx 270\) K contributes:

      \[T_{\mathrm{sky}} = T_{\mathrm{atm}}(1 - e^{-\tau/\sin\varepsilon}) + T_{\mathrm{cmb}} e^{-\tau/\sin\varepsilon}\tag{13.19}\]

      where \(\tau\) is the zenith opacity and \(\varepsilon\) is the elevation angle.

    3. Ground spillover: Sidelobe and spillover radiation that couples to the warm ground (\(T_{\mathrm{gnd}} \approx 290\) K). For a well-designed earth station antenna: \(T_{\mathrm{spill}} \approx \eta_{\mathrm{spill}} \cdot 290\) K with \(\eta_{\mathrm{spill}} < 0.01\) for high-quality antennas.

    Typical system noise temperatures for earth station receivers:

    • C-band (4 GHz) with LNA: \(T_{\mathrm{sys}} \approx 50\)–80 K

    • Ku-band (11–12 GHz) with LNA: \(T_{\mathrm{sys}} \approx 100\)–150 K

    • Ka-band (20 GHz) with LNA: \(T_{\mathrm{sys}} \approx 200\)–400 K (atmospheric contribution increases)

    13.6 Figure of Merit and Carrier-to-Noise Ratio¶

    13.6.1 Figure of Merit G/T¶

    The receive antenna gain \(G_r\) and system noise temperature \(T_{\mathrm{sys}}\) always appear together in the link budget. Their ratio, the figure of merit \(G/T\), completely characterizes the receive system quality independent of bandwidth:

    \[\frac{G}{T} \;[\mathrm{dBK^{-1}}] = G_r \;[\mathrm{dBi}] - 10\log_{10}(T_{\mathrm{sys}} \;[\mathrm{K}])\tag{13.20}\]

    13.6.2 Carrier-to-Noise Ratio¶

    The carrier-to-noise ratio (CNR or \(C/N\)) is the ratio of received signal power to noise power in the receiver bandwidth \(B\). Combining all previous equations:

    \[\boxed{\frac{C}{N} \;[\mathrm{dB}] = \mathrm{EIRP} - \mathrm{FSPL} + \frac{G}{T} - k - 10\log_{10}(B) - L_{\mathrm{misc}}}\tag{13.21}\]

    The carrier-to-noise density \(C/N_0\) eliminates the bandwidth dependence, making it a fundamental figure of system performance:

    \[\frac{C}{N_0} \;[\mathrm{dB\cdot Hz}] = \mathrm{EIRP} + \frac{G}{T} - \mathrm{FSPL} - k - L_{\mathrm{misc}}\tag{13.22}\]

    where \(k = -228.6\) dBW/K/Hz. \(C/N_0\) is the standard metric used in satellite system specifications as it is independent of the modulation bandwidth.

    13.6.3 Energy per Bit to Noise Density¶

    For digital communications, the key performance metric is the energy-per-bit to noise density ratio \(E_b/N_0\), which relates directly to the BER through modulation-specific expressions:

    \[\frac{E_b}{N_0} \;[\mathrm{dB}] = \frac{C}{N_0} \;[\mathrm{dB\cdot Hz}] - 10\log_{10}(R_b \;[\mathrm{bits/s}])\tag{13.23}\]

    where \(R_b\) is the information bit rate after channel decoding. For a QPSK signal at \(R_s = 36\) Msps with rate-3/4 coding, the information bit rate is: \(R_b = 2 \times (3/4) \times 36\times10^6 = 54\) Mbps.

    13.7 BER Performance of Satellite Modulation Schemes¶

    13.7.1 Phase-Shift Keying¶

    For BPSK, the exact BER expression is:

    \[\mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{BPSK}} = Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{N_0}}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\right)\tag{13.24}\]

    QPSK achieves the same BER vs \(E_b/N_0\) performance as BPSK but with twice the spectral efficiency (2 bits/symbol vs. 1 bit/symbol):

    \[\mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{QPSK}} = Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2E_b}{N_0}}\right)\tag{13.25}\]

    For 8-PSK with Gray coding, the BER approximation is:

    \[\mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{8PSK}} \approx \frac{2}{3}\,Q\!\left(\sqrt{2 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{E_b}{N_0}} \cdot \sin\frac{\pi}{8}\right)\tag{13.26}\]

    where the factor \(\log_2(8) = 3\) accounts for bits-per-symbol. 8-PSK requires approximately 4 dB more \(E_b/N_0\) than QPSK at BER \(= 10^{-6}\).

    13.7.2 Amplitude and Phase Shift Keying for Nonlinear Channels¶

    The DVB-S2 standard introduced 16-APSK and 32-APSK, which are optimized for operation through a nonlinear TWTA transponder at near-saturation. The 16-APSK constellation uses two rings: 4 inner points at radius \(r_1\) and 12 outer points at radius \(r_2\), with ring ratio \(\gamma = r_2/r_1 = 2.57\) (for code rate 3/4 per DVB-S2 specification).

    The 32-APSK uses three rings: 4 + 12 + 16 points. The constellation was designed using optimization over a nonlinear channel model (Saleh model for TWTA) to maximize minimum Euclidean distance in the presence of AM/AM and AM/PM conversion.

    13.8 Atmospheric Propagation Effects¶

    13.8.1 Gaseous Absorption¶

    The primary gaseous absorbers in the microwave region are water vapor (centered near 22.2 GHz and 183 GHz) and molecular oxygen (60 GHz and 118.7 GHz). The ITU-R P.676 model provides the specific attenuation coefficients \(\gamma_o\) (dry air) and \(\gamma_w\) (water vapor) in dB/km. Total zenith gaseous attenuation:

    \[A_{\mathrm{gas}} = \frac{\gamma_o h_o + \gamma_w h_w}{\sin\varepsilon} \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.27}\]

    where \(h_o \approx 6\) km and \(h_w \approx 2\) km are equivalent scale heights, and \(\varepsilon\) is elevation angle. At Ku-band (12 GHz) and \(\varepsilon = 30\degree\), \(A_{\mathrm{gas}} \approx 0.3\) dB; at Ka-band (20 GHz), \(A_{\mathrm{gas}} \approx 0.5\)–1.5 dB.

    13.8.2 Cloud and Water Vapor Attenuation¶

    Cloud liquid water content \(W\) [g/m\(^3\)] contributes specific attenuation:

    \[\gamma_c = K_l W \quad [\mathrm{dB/km}]\tag{13.28}\]

    where \(K_l\) is the mass absorption coefficient (temperature-dependent, approximately \(K_l \approx 0.06\) dB/(g/m\(^3\)) at 11 GHz). The ITU-R P.840 model provides global cloud statistics. Cloud attenuation at Ku-band typically ranges 0.1–2 dB.

    13.8.3 Rain Attenuation¶

    Rain attenuation is the dominant propagation impairment at Ku-band and above. Rain drops absorb and scatter electromagnetic energy. The specific attenuation of rain is modeled as:

    \[\gamma_R = a R^b \quad [\mathrm{dB/km}]\tag{13.29}\]

    where \(R\) [mm/hr] is the rain rate and \(a\), \(b\) are frequency- and polarization-dependent coefficients from ITU-R P.838. At 12 GHz: \(a = 0.0168\), \(b = 1.217\) (horizontal polarization). At 20 GHz: \(a = 0.0751\), \(b = 1.099\).

    The ITU-R P.618 rain attenuation prediction method gives the effective rain path length as:

    \[d_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{(h_R - h_s) / \sin\varepsilon}{1 + 0.78\sqrt{\gamma_R L_s / f} - 0.38(1-e^{-2L_s})}\tag{13.30}\]

    where \(h_R\) is the rain height, \(h_s\) is the earth station height above MSL, \(\varepsilon\) is the elevation angle, and \(L_s\) is the slant path through rain. Total rain attenuation:

    \[A_{0.01} = \gamma_R \cdot d_{\mathrm{eff}} \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.31}\]

    This gives the attenuation exceeded 0.01% of the time (i.e., exceeded for 52.6 minutes/year). For other percentages \(p\) (in range 0.001–5%):

    \[A_p = A_{0.01} \cdot \left(\frac{p}{0.01}\right)^{-(0.655 + 0.033\ln p - 0.045\ln A_{0.01})}\tag{13.32}\]
    Table 13.1. Typical rain attenuation at Ku-band (12 GHz, 30elevation, Temperate Europe)
    Availability [%] Time Exceeded [min/year] Attenuation [dB]
    99.0 5,256 \(\sim\)1.5
    99.5 2,628 \(\sim\)2.5
    99.9 526 \(\sim\)5.0

    99.999 5.26 \(\sim\)16.0

    13.9 Miscellaneous Link Losses¶

    13.9.1 Antenna Pointing Loss¶

    For a Gaussian beam approximation to the antenna pattern, the gain reduction due to pointing error \(\theta_e\) (smaller than the half-power beamwidth) is:

    \[L_{\mathrm{point}} = 12 \left(\frac{\theta_e}{\theta_{\mathrm{HPBW}}}\right)^2 \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.33}\]

    Earth station antennas with servo tracking achieve \(\theta_e < 0.01\degree\) for Ku-band, giving \(L_{\mathrm{point}} < 0.1\) dB. Fixed VSAT antennas may have \(L_{\mathrm{point}} \approx 0.3\)–0.5 dB if not precisely aligned.

    13.9.2 Polarization Loss¶

    Polarization mismatch loss occurs when the transmit and receive polarization states are not perfectly matched. For linear polarization with angle offset \(\psi\):

    \[L_{\mathrm{pol}} = -20\log_{10}(\cos\psi) \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.34}\]

    Faraday rotation in the ionosphere can rotate the polarization angle by 0–30at L-band and by less than 1at Ku-band. For circular polarization, Faraday rotation causes no loss, which is why circular polarization is preferred for lower-frequency systems.

    13.10 Combined Uplink and Downlink Budget¶

    For a bent-pipe (non-regenerative) satellite transponder, the satellite receives the uplink signal with noise, amplifies the signal-plus-noise, and retransmits it. The downlink adds further noise. The overall \(C/N\) is:

    \[\left(\frac{C}{N}\right)_{\mathrm{total}}^{-1} = \left(\frac{C}{N}\right)_{\mathrm{uplink}}^{-1} + \left(\frac{C}{N}\right)_{\mathrm{downlink}}^{-1} + \left(\frac{C}{N}\right)_{\mathrm{intermod}}^{-1}\tag{13.35}\]

    The intermodulation term \((C/N)_{\mathrm{intermod}}\) arises from nonlinear distortion in the TWTA when multiple carriers share a transponder. It is minimized by operating the TWTA 3–6 dB below saturation (input backoff, IBO).

    For a regenerative (on-board processing) satellite, the uplink and downlink are independent:

    \[\mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{total}} \approx \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{uplink}} + \mathrm{BER}_{\mathrm{downlink}}\tag{13.36}\]

    (for small BER values). Regenerative payloads completely decouple the uplink and downlink budgets.

    13.11 Worked Example: Ku-Band GEO Link¶

    13.11.1 System Parameters¶

    We analyze a standard Ku-band GEO communication system with the following parameters:

    Uplink (Earth station to satellite):

    • Earth station transmit power: \(P_t = 100\) W = 20 dBW

    • Earth station antenna diameter: \(D = 3\) m, \(\eta_a = 0.65\), at \(f_u = 14.0\) GHz (\(\lambda_u = 21.4\) mm)

    • Earth station antenna gain: \(G_t = 10\log_{10}(0.65 \cdot (\pi \cdot 3/0.0214)^2) = 52.5\) dBi

    • Earth station EIRP: \(52.5 + 20 - 0.5 = 72.0\) dBW

    • Satellite orbit: GEO at 36,000 km

    • FSPL at 14 GHz, 36,000 km: \(92.45 + 91.12 + 22.92 = 206.5\) dB

    • Satellite \(G/T = -1.0\) dBK\(^{-1}\) (includes feed loss and LNA noise figure 1.5 dB)

    • Atmospheric losses: \(A_{\mathrm{gas}} = 0.35\) dB, rain fade margin analysis below

    Uplink \(C/N_0\):

    \[\frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\mathrm{UL}} = 72.0 - 206.5 + (-1.0) - (-228.6) - 0.35 = 92.75 \;\mathrm{dB\cdot Hz}\tag{13.37}\]

    Downlink (satellite to earth station):

    • Satellite EIRP: 52 dBW (includes 20W HPA at 13 dBW, 40 dBi spot beam antenna, minus 1 dB losses)

    • Downlink frequency: \(f_d = 11.7\) GHz (\(\lambda_d = 25.6\) mm)

    • FSPL at 11.7 GHz, 36,000 km: \(92.45 + 91.12 + 21.36 = 204.9\) dB

    • Earth station receive dish: \(D_r = 0.75\) m, \(\eta_r = 0.65\)

    • Earth station receive gain: \(G_r = 10\log_{10}(0.65 \cdot (\pi \cdot 0.75/0.0256)^2) = 39.2\) dBi

    • Earth station LNA noise figure: \(F = 0.8\) dB, \(T_{\mathrm{LNA}} = 54\) K

    • Sky noise temperature at 30elevation: \(T_{\mathrm{sky}} = 35\) K

    • System noise temperature: \(T_{\mathrm{sys}} = 35 + 5 + 54 \approx 94\) K = 19.7 dBK

    • Earth station \(G/T = 39.2 - 19.7 = 19.5\) dBK\(^{-1}\)

    • Atmospheric losses: 0.3 dB (clear sky)

    Downlink \(C/N_0\):

    \[\frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\mathrm{DL}} = 52.0 - 204.9 + 19.5 - (-228.6) - 0.3 = 94.9 \;\mathrm{dB\cdot Hz}\tag{13.38}\]

    Overall \(C/N_0\):

    \[\frac{C}{N_0}\bigg|_{\mathrm{total}} = -10\log_{10}\!\left(10^{-92.75/10} + 10^{-94.9/10}\right) = 91.3 \;\mathrm{dB\cdot Hz}\tag{13.39}\]

    13.11.2 Required E_b/N₀ for QPSK at BER = 10⁻8¶

    For QPSK with rate-1/2 LDPC coding (DVB-S2 QPSK 1/2), the threshold \(E_b/N_0\) for BER \(= 10^{-8}\) is approximately 3.3 dB (from AWGN performance curves including implementation loss of 0.3 dB).

    Information bit rate: \(R_b = 2 \times (1/2) \times R_s = R_s\). For \(R_s = 36\) Msps: \(R_b = 36\) Mbps; \(10\log_{10}(36\times10^6) = 75.6\) dB.

    \[\frac{E_b}{N_0}\bigg|_{\mathrm{total}} = 91.3 - 75.6 = 15.7 \;\mathrm{dB}\tag{13.40}\]

    Link margin = \(15.7 - 3.3 = 12.4\) dB. This substantial margin accommodates rain fade: at Ku-band in temperate Europe, 99.9% availability requires approximately 5 dB of rain fade margin, leaving an additional 7.4 dB of implementation margin.

    13.12 DVB-S2X Adaptive Coding and Modulation¶

    The DVB-S2X standard (ETSI EN 302 307-2) extends DVB-S2 with higher-order modulation formats and finer code rate granularity. Adaptive Coding and Modulation (ACM) dynamically selects the modcod based on real-time link conditions, maximizing throughput under varying propagation. The link budget must therefore be computed for the nominal (clear sky) condition and separately for the edge-of-coverage and rain fade conditions.

    13.13 High-Throughput Satellite Link Budget¶

    High-throughput satellites (HTS) achieve terabit-level aggregate throughput through aggressive frequency reuse. A system with \(N_b\) beams, frequency reuse factor \(F_r\), and transponder bandwidth \(B_t\) has aggregate bandwidth:

    \[B_{\mathrm{total}} = N_b \cdot B_t / F_r \quad [\mathrm{Hz}]\tag{13.41}\]

    The spectral efficiency \(\eta_s\) [bits/s/Hz] depends on the modcod and the carrier-to- interference ratio (CIR) from co-frequency beams. Adjacent beam interference (ABI) adds to the noise budget:

    \[\left(\frac{C}{N+I}\right)^{-1} = \left(\frac{C}{N}\right)^{-1} + \left(\frac{C}{I}\right)^{-1}\tag{13.42}\]

    For a 4-color frequency reuse plan, adjacent beams use different frequencies, giving \(C/I \approx 20\)–25 dB. With \(C/N = 14\) dB and \(C/I = 22\) dB, \(C/(N+I) \approx 13.4\) dB.

    Viasat-3 (launched 2023) exemplifies state-of-the-art HTS design: approximately 1 Tbps aggregate capacity over 500+ spot beams, each 200–400 MHz wide at Ka-band, with 4-color reuse, adaptive coding, and beam hopping.

    13.14 LEO Broadband Link Budget¶

    LEO broadband constellations such as Starlink (SpaceX) and OneWeb operate at altitude \(h \approx 550\) km. The much shorter path dramatically reduces free space loss:

    \[\mathrm{FSPL}_{\mathrm{LEO}} = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}(550) + 20\log_{10}(14) = 92.45 + 54.8 + 22.9 = 170.1 \;\mathrm{dB}\tag{13.43}\]

    Compare with GEO: \(\Delta\mathrm{FSPL} = 206.5 - 170.1 = 36.4\) dB improvement. This allows much smaller user terminals (flat-panel phased arrays rather than 0.75–1.2 m dishes).

    At minimum elevation angle \(\varepsilon = 25\degree\), the slant range increases to:

    \[d = \sqrt{(R_e + h)^2 - R_e^2\cos^2\varepsilon} - R_e\sin\varepsilon = 1{,}000 \;\mathrm{km} \quad (\varepsilon=25\degree)\tag{13.44}\]
    \[\mathrm{FSPL}_{25\degree} = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}(1000) + 20\log_{10}(14) = 92.45 + 60 + 22.9 = 175.4 \;\mathrm{dB}\tag{13.45}\]

    Additional 5.3 dB compared to zenith. The Starlink user terminal (Generation 2) uses a 480-element Ku-band phased array (approximately 0.5 m \(\times\) 0.3 m flat panel) with electronically steered beam tracking the satellite across the sky. Typical downlink performance: \(\geq 100\) Mbps with latency \(\leq 45\) ms.

    13.15 Inter-Satellite Link Budget¶

    Inter-satellite links (ISL) operate in a benign environment: no atmosphere, no rain, no ionospheric effects. However, the distances can be significant. For adjacent orbital plane ISLs at 550 km altitude, separation \(\approx 1{,}500\) km.

    V-band ISL at 60 GHz, separation 1,500 km:

    \[\mathrm{FSPL}_{\mathrm{ISL}} = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}(1500) + 20\log_{10}(60) = 92.45 + 63.5 + 35.6 = 191.6 \;\mathrm{dB}\tag{13.46}\]

    With 25 dBi gain antennas (each side) and 0 dBW transmit power, the received power is: \(P_r = 0 + 25 - 191.6 + 25 = -141.6\) dBW. With \(T_{\mathrm{sys}} = 500\) K (space environment), \(C/N_0 = -141.6 + 228.6 - 27.0 = 60.0\) dB\(\cdot\)Hz for 10 Gbps (\(R_b = 100\) dBHz): \(E_b/N_0 = 60 - 100 = -40\) dB — insufficient. Therefore ISL links use higher EIRP (e.g., 40 dBW satellite EIRP with high-gain directional antenna).

    For Starlink optical ISL (1550 nm, 1 W transmit, 10 cm aperture):

    \[\mathrm{FSPL}_{\mathrm{opt}} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi R}{\lambda}\right) = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \cdot 1.5\times10^6}{1.55\times10^{-6}}\right) = 262 \;\mathrm{dB}\tag{13.47}\]

    With about 106 dBi optical gain each side (ideal aperture, \(G = (\pi D/\lambda)^2\)) and 0 dBW transmit, \(P_r = 0 + 106 - 262 + 106 = -50\) dBW (about \(-20\) dBm) before pointing and implementation losses, which can support multi-Gbps coherent links.

    13.16 Link Margin Philosophy and Availability¶

    The link margin is the difference between the available \(C/N\) and the required \(C/N\) for the target BER:

    \[M_{\mathrm{link}} = (C/N)_{\mathrm{available}} - (C/N)_{\mathrm{required}} \quad [\mathrm{dB}]\tag{13.48}\]

    The required margin \(M_{\mathrm{req}}\) must account for:

    1. Rain fade margin \(M_{\mathrm{rain}}\): The attenuation not exceeded for the required availability percentage (e.g., 99.9% \(\rightarrow\) 5 dB at Ku-band in Europe).

    2. Pointing loss margin \(M_{\mathrm{point}} \approx 0.3\)–0.5 dB: Variability in earth station alignment due to thermal deformation, wind loading, and tracking jitter.

    3. Implementation margin \(M_{\mathrm{impl}} \approx 0.5\)–1.0 dB: Allows for deviation from ideal AWGN performance due to phase noise, frequency error, filter distortion, and A/D converter quantization.

    4. Interference margin \(M_{\mathrm{int}} \approx 0.5\)–2.0 dB: Allowance for adjacent satellite interference (ASI) and co-channel interference.

    5. Aging/end-of-life margin \(M_{\mathrm{EOL}} \approx 1\)–3 dB: Accounts for solar cell degradation and TWT cathode emission reduction over a 15-year mission life.

    Total required margin: \(M_{\mathrm{req}} = M_{\mathrm{rain}} + M_{\mathrm{point}} + M_{\mathrm{impl}} + M_{\mathrm{int}} + M_{\mathrm{EOL}}\)

    For a 99.9% Ku-band link in central Europe: \(M_{\mathrm{req}} = 5.0 + 0.4 + 0.8 + 1.0 + 2.0 = 9.2\) dB.

    13.17 Satellite Capacity and Spectral Efficiency¶

    The Shannon channel capacity provides the theoretical upper bound:

    \[C = B \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right) \quad [\mathrm{bits/s}]\tag{13.49}\]

    A satellite transponder with bandwidth \(B = 500\) MHz and operating at \(C/N = 20\) dB (SNR linear = 100) has theoretical capacity \(C = 500 \times 10^6 \times \log_2(101) \approx 3.3\) Gbps. Modern DVB-S2X with 256APSK 3/4 achieves 5.84 bits/s/Hz spectral efficiency, approaching Shannon limit to within 2–3 dB in ideal AWGN conditions.

    Aggregate HTS throughput:

    \[\Gamma_{\mathrm{total}} = N_b \cdot B_{\mathrm{beam}} \cdot \eta_s \cdot (1 - \mathrm{FEC overhead}) \quad [\mathrm{bits/s}]\tag{13.50}\]

    For a system with \(N_b = 300\) beams, \(B_{\mathrm{beam}} = 500\) MHz, \(\eta_s = 3.0\) bits/s/Hz: \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{total}} = 300 \times 500\times10^6 \times 3.0 = 450\) Gbps aggregate throughput.

    13.18 Summary¶

    This chapter has derived the satellite link budget from electromagnetic first principles and applied it to representative GEO, HTS, LEO, ISL, and optical communication scenarios. The key equations governing link budget analysis are:

    1. EIRP [dBW] = \(P_t\) [dBW] + \(G_t\) [dBi] \(-\) \(L_f\) [dB]

    2. FSPL [dB] = \(92.45 + 20\log_{10}(d[\mathrm{km}]) + 20\log_{10}(f[\mathrm{GHz}])\)

    3. \(C/N_0\) [dBHz] = EIRP + \(G/T\) \(-\) FSPL \(-\) \(k\) \(-\) \(L_{\mathrm{misc}}\)

    4. \(E_b/N_0\) [dB] = \(C/N_0\) \(-\) \(10\log_{10}(R_b)\)

    5. \((C/N)_{\mathrm{total}}^{-1} = (C/N)_{\mathrm{UL}}^{-1} + (C/N)_{\mathrm{DL}}^{-1}\) (for bent-pipe)

    The DVB-S2X ACM framework allows dynamic modcod selection, optimizing throughput under varying propagation conditions. Modern HTS systems approach terabit aggregate throughput through spatial frequency reuse, and LEO constellations exploit the shorter propagation path to deliver broadband services with consumer-grade user terminals.

    Link Budget Design Rules of Thumb:

    • Every 3 dB of path loss requires doubling the transmit power or quadrupling the antenna area.

    • The LNA noise figure dominates: a 1 dB improvement in LNA noise figure directly improves \(G/T\) by 1 dB.

    • LEO offers \(\sim\)36 dB lower free space path loss than GEO at same frequency.

    • At Ka-band, rain attenuation can exceed 20 dB for 0.01% exceedance in tropical regions.

    • DVB-S2X ACM can recover 8–12 dB of link margin in clear sky, trading margin for throughput.
