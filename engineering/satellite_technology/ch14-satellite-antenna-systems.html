    Chapter 14: Satellite Antenna Systems¶

    Key Concepts: Antenna gain and directivity, aperture efficiency, parabolic reflector systems, phased array antennas, active electronically scanned arrays (AESA), deployable mesh reflectors, digital beamforming (DBF), multi-beam antennas, polarization diversity.

    14.1 Introduction¶

    The antenna is the interface between the guided electromagnetic wave within the satellite or earth station hardware and the radiated field propagating through space. In the link budget, the antenna appears twice: as the transmit gain \(G_t\) multiplying the transmitter power to produce EIRP, and as the receive gain \(G_r\) in the figure of merit \(G/T\). Consequently, antenna performance has a one-to-one impact on link margin in decibels, making antenna engineering one of the highest-leverage disciplines in satellite system design.

    Modern satellite missions impose extraordinarily demanding antenna requirements: 0.5 m diameter antennas on CubeSats that must provide regional coverage, 22 m deployable reflectors on mobile satellite service (MSS) spacecraft, 1,000-element active phased arrays for LEO broadband user terminals, and multi-beam antennas generating hundreds of spot beams from a single GEO spacecraft. This chapter addresses all of these, developing the underlying physics before describing each antenna type in detail.

    14.2 Antenna Fundamentals¶

    14.2.1 Gain, Directivity, and Efficiency¶

    An antenna’s directivity \(D\) is defined as the ratio of the radiation intensity \(U(\theta,\phi)\) in the direction of maximum radiation to the average radiation intensity over all directions:

    \[D = \frac{4\pi U_{\max}}{P_{\mathrm{rad}}} = \frac{4\pi U_{\max}}{\oint U(\theta,\phi)\,d\Omega}\tag{14.1}\]

    The gain \(G\) incorporates the antenna radiation efficiency \(\eta_r\) (accounting for ohmic and dielectric losses):

    \[G = \eta_r \cdot D\tag{14.2}\]

    For an aperture antenna, directivity and gain are related to the physical aperture area \(A\) and aperture efficiency \(\eta_a\) through:

    \[G = \eta_a \cdot \frac{4\pi A}{\lambda^2}\tag{14.3}\]

    The aperture efficiency \(\eta_a\) is the product of several sub-efficiency factors:

    \[\eta_a = \eta_{\mathrm{ill}} \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{spill}} \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{phase}} \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{block}} \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{surface}} \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{pol}}\tag{14.4}\]

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{ill}}\): illumination efficiency — accounts for non-uniform amplitude taper across the aperture (ranges from 0.75 to 1.0 for typical tapers; 1.0 for uniform illumination)

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{spill}}\): spillover efficiency — fraction of feed radiation intercepted by the reflector (typically 0.85–0.95)

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{phase}}\): phase efficiency — reduction due to phase error across aperture (defocus, surface distortion)

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{block}}\): blockage efficiency — reduction due to the feed/subreflector shadowing the main aperture (0 for offset-fed systems, 0.95–0.99 for prime focus)

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{surface}}\): Ruze surface efficiency — \(\eta_{\mathrm{surface}} = \exp[-(4\pi\epsilon/\lambda)^2]\) where \(\epsilon\) is the RMS surface error

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{pol}}\): polarization efficiency — loss due to cross-polarized radiation

    Typical overall aperture efficiency for a well-designed parabolic reflector: \(\eta_a \approx 0.55\)–\(0.70\).

    14.2.2 Half-Power Beamwidth and Sidelobe Levels¶

    The half-power beamwidth (HPBW) of a circular aperture of diameter \(D\) is approximately:

    \[\theta_{\mathrm{HPBW}} \approx \frac{70\lambda}{D} \quad [\mathrm{degrees}]\tag{14.5}\]

    This expression holds for \(-10\) dB edge taper illumination, which is a common design target. For a rectangular aperture with dimensions \(a \times b\):

    \[\theta_{\mathrm{HPBW,a}} \approx \frac{51\lambda}{a}\quad [\mathrm{deg}], \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{HPBW,b}} \approx \frac{51\lambda}{b}\quad [\mathrm{deg}]\tag{14.6}\]

    The first sidelobe level of a uniformly illuminated aperture is \(-13.3\) dBc. Tapering the aperture illumination (e.g., Taylor distribution) reduces sidelobes at the cost of reduced gain and wider main beam. A \(-25\) dB Taylor taper reduces sidelobes to \(-25\) dBc but increases beamwidth by approximately 30%.

    14.2.3 Polarization¶

    Satellite antennas can transmit and receive linear polarization (horizontal or vertical, or \(\pm 45\degree\)) or circular polarization (left-hand or right-hand, LHCP/RHCP). Key polarization metrics include:

    • Cross-polarization discrimination (XPD): ratio of co-polarized to cross-polarized gain in the main beam direction. Required \(\mathrm{XPD} \geq 30\) dB for dual-polarization frequency reuse systems.

    • Axial ratio (AR): for circular polarization, the ratio of major to minor axes of the polarization ellipse. AR = 1 (0 dB) for perfect circular polarization. The power loss due to axial ratio mismatch is:

      \[L_{\mathrm{AR}} = \frac{(AR_t \cdot AR_r + 1)^2 + (AR_t - AR_r)^2}{(AR_t^2 + 1)(AR_r^2 + 1)} \quad [\mathrm{ratio}]\tag{14.7}\]

    • Faraday rotation: At L-band, the ionosphere rotates linear polarization by \(\Delta\phi \approx 40.3 \cdot \mathrm{TEC} / f^2\) [rad], where TEC is the total electron content (typically 1–100 TECU). At 1.5 GHz with 30 TECU: \(\Delta\phi \approx 35\degree\), necessitating circular polarization for robust operation.

    14.3 Horn Antennas¶

    Horn antennas are the workhorses of satellite TT&C (telemetry, tracking, and command) systems and the primary feeds for reflector antennas. A conical corrugated horn is the preferred feed design because it produces a circularly symmetric, low-sidelobe radiation pattern with excellent polarization purity. The corrugations (grooves cut into the inner wall perpendicular to the propagation axis) force the boundary conditions to support the \(\mathrm{HE}_{11}\) hybrid mode rather than the \(\mathrm{TE}_{11}\) fundamental mode, suppressing asymmetric sidelobes and cross-polarization.

    The far-field radiation pattern of a corrugated horn with aperture radius \(a\) and half-flare angle \(\psi\) can be expressed using Gaussian beam optics as:

    \[E(\rho,z) = E_0 \frac{w_0}{w(z)} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\rho^2}{w^2(z)}\right) \exp\!\left(-j\!\left[kz + k\frac{\rho^2}{2R(z)} - \arctan\!\frac{z}{z_R}\right]\right)\tag{14.8}\]

    where \(w_0\) is the beam waist radius, \(w(z) = w_0\sqrt{1 + (z/z_R)^2}\) is the beam radius at distance \(z\), \(R(z) = z[1 + (z_R/z)^2]\) is the wavefront radius of curvature, and \(z_R = \pi w_0^2/\lambda\) is the Rayleigh range.

    Potter horns (step-discontinuity horns) use a simple diameter step transition to generate the \(\mathrm{TM}_{11}\) mode and combine it with \(\mathrm{TE}_{11}\) to produce a similar symmetric pattern to corrugated horns, with lower fabrication complexity.

    14.4 Parabolic Reflector Antennas¶

    14.4.1 Prime Focus Parabolic Reflector¶

    The parabolic reflector focuses a plane wave incident from the direction of the parabola axis onto a single point (the focus). Equivalently, radiation from the focus reflects into a plane wave. The parabola equation in polar coordinates from the focus is:

    \[r = \frac{2f}{1 + \cos\theta} = \frac{f}{\cos^2(\theta/2)}\tag{14.9}\]

    where \(f\) is the focal length and \(\theta\) is measured from the parabola axis. The \(f/D\) ratio governs the illumination angle \(\psi_0\):

    \[\tan\!\left(\frac{\psi_0}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{4(f/D)}\tag{14.10}\]

    For \(f/D = 0.4\) (common for satellite antennas), \(\psi_0 = 64\degree\). The feed must illuminate the reflector within \(\pm\psi_0\) while spilling minimal power outside this angle.

    Prime focus designs suffer aperture blockage from the feed structure, reducing gain and increasing sidelobe levels. Aperture blockage efficiency for a feed support of diameter \(d_s\) in an aperture of diameter \(D\):

    \[\eta_{\mathrm{block}} \approx \left(1 - \frac{d_s^2}{D^2}\right)^2\tag{14.11}\]

    14.4.2 Offset-Fed Parabolic Reflector¶

    The offset-fed reflector displaces the feed from the axis of the parent parabola, so the feed and feed support structure are moved outside the projected aperture. This eliminates aperture blockage completely (\(\eta_{\mathrm{block}} = 1\)), typically improving gain by 0.5–1.0 dB and dramatically reducing sidelobe levels. The trade-off is structural asymmetry and the generation of cross-polarized radiation, which must be compensated by the feed. Offset reflectors are standard for Ku-band direct-to-home (DTH) receive antennas and for satellite transmit antennas where high gain and low cross-polarization are required simultaneously.

    14.4.3 Cassegrain and Gregorian Dual-Reflector Systems¶

    The Cassegrain system places a convex hyperboloidal subreflector between the feed and the main paraboloidal reflector. The two foci of the hyperboloid coincide: one with the feed phase center and the other with the focal point of the paraboloid. This arrangement allows the feed to be located at the vertex of the main reflector, shortening the feed transmission line and improving overall system efficiency for large antennas.

    The Gregorian system uses a concave ellipsoidal subreflector located beyond the prime focus. This allows more physical clearance between the feed and subreflector, accommodating larger feed assemblies. The Gregorian geometry is preferred when a large feed cluster is required (e.g., for multi-beam antenna feeds), or when shaping both reflectors to achieve specific far-field coverage patterns.

    For shaped-beam satellites, both reflectors are numerically optimized using methods such as physical optics (PO) and physical theory of diffraction (PTD) to produce prescribed coverage footprints with minimum EIRP variation across a service region. Commercial software (GRASP from TICRA) is the industry standard for such reflector synthesis.

    14.5 Shaped Reflector Antennas for Coverage Optimization¶

    A shaped beam antenna produces a radiation pattern whose gain contour closely matches the boundary of a specified service zone—e.g., the continental United States, Europe, or a country. This maximizes EIRP toward the intended service area while minimizing radiation toward interference-sensitive adjacent regions.

    The far-field pattern of a reflector antenna is related to the aperture field distribution \(E_a(x',y')\) by the Fourier transform:

    \[E(\theta,\phi) = \iint_A E_a(x',y') \exp\!\left[j k (x'\sin\theta\cos\phi + y'\sin\theta\sin\phi)\right] dx'\,dy'\tag{14.12}\]

    Reflector shaping adjusts the surface curvature (equivalently, the phase of \(E_a\)) to redistribute energy in the far field. The inverse problem—finding the reflector surface that produces a desired pattern—is solved iteratively using physical optics methods.

    14.6 Phased Array Antennas¶

    14.6.1 Array Factor and Beam Steering¶

    A linear array of \(N\) elements, uniformly spaced by \(d\), with complex weights \(w_n = a_n e^{j\psi_n}\) (amplitude \(a_n\), phase \(\psi_n\)), produces an array factor:

    \[\mathrm{AF}(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} w_n \exp[j n k d \sin\theta]\tag{14.13}\]

    where \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) is the free-space wavenumber. To steer the main beam to angle \(\theta_0\), a progressive phase shift is applied:

    \[\psi_n = -n k d \sin\theta_0\tag{14.14}\]

    For a 2D rectangular array of \(N_x \times N_y\) elements with spacings \(d_x\) and \(d_y\):

    \[\mathrm{AF}(\theta,\phi) = \sum_{m=0}^{N_x-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_y-1} w_{mn} \exp\!\left[j(mkd_x\sin\theta\cos\phi + nkd_y\sin\theta\sin\phi)\right]\tag{14.15}\]

    14.6.2 Grating Lobes and Element Spacing¶

    Grating lobes are spurious main-lobe replicas that appear at angles where the progressive phase shift produces constructive interference in an unintended direction. For a linear array, grating lobes occur at:

    \[\sin\theta_g = \sin\theta_0 \pm \frac{m\lambda}{d}, \quad m = 1, 2, 3, …\tag{14.16}\]

    To prevent grating lobes within real space (\(|\sin\theta| \leq 1\)) for all scan angles \(|\theta_0| \leq \theta_{\max}\):

    \[d \leq \frac{\lambda}{1 + |\sin\theta_{\max}|}\tag{14.17}\]

    For a maximum scan angle of \(\theta_{\max} = 60\degree\) (typical for LEO user terminals): \(d \leq \lambda / 1.866 \approx 0.54\lambda\). Standard element spacing is \(d = \lambda/2\), which prevents grating lobes for scan to \(\pm 90\degree\).

    14.6.3 Scan Loss¶

    The gain of a phased array decreases as the beam is scanned away from broadside. For an element pattern with \(\cos^q\theta\) directivity (commonly \(q = 1\) for patch elements, \(q = 1.5\) for dipoles over a ground plane), the array gain at scan angle \(\theta_0\) is:

    \[G(\theta_0) = G_{\mathrm{broadside}} \cdot \cos^q\theta_0\tag{14.18}\]

    For \(q = 1\): scan to \(60\degree\) incurs \(10\log_{10}(\cos 60\degree) = -3\) dB scan loss. Practical phased array designs for satellite user terminals accept up to \(-4\) dB scan loss at \(60\degree\) scan (corresponding to \(25\degree\) elevation angle when the satellite is overhead at \(55\degree\) elevation and the terminal tracks).

    14.7 Active Electronically Scanned Arrays (AESA)¶

    14.7.1 T/R Module Architecture¶

    An AESA integrates a transmit/receive (T/R) module at each radiating element, or at each subarray of 4–16 elements. A complete T/R module contains:

    • Transmit path: Driver amplifier \(\rightarrow\) Power amplifier (PA) \(\rightarrow\) Phase shifter \(\rightarrow\) Attenuator \(\rightarrow\) Antenna element

    • Receive path: Antenna element \(\rightarrow\) Low-noise amplifier (LNA) \(\rightarrow\) Phase shifter \(\rightarrow\) Attenuator \(\rightarrow\) Combiner

    • Control: DC/DC converter, thermal management, digital control interface (SPI/I2C)

    For a satellite user terminal T/R module at Ku-band:

    • LNA noise figure: 1.5–2.0 dB at 10.7–12.7 GHz

    • PA output power: 23–27 dBm per element

    • Phase shifter resolution: 5 or 6 bits (11.25or 5.625steps)

    • Attenuator range: 0–31.5 dB in 0.5 dB steps (6-bit)

    • MMIC technology: GaAs pHEMT for LNA, GaN HEMT for PA

    14.7.2 MMIC Technology¶

    Monolithic Microwave Integrated Circuits (MMICs) fabricate all active and passive components on a single semiconductor die using semiconductor process technology:

    • GaAs pHEMT: Noise figure 0.5–1.5 dB at 10–18 GHz; electron mobility \(\mu_e \approx 8{,}500\) cm\(^2\)/V\(\cdot\)s; standard for receive-side LNAs.

    • GaN HEMT: Breakdown voltage \(>100\) V; power density 5–10 W/mm vs. 0.5–1.0 W/mm for GaAs; preferred for high-power transmit modules; noise figure 1.5–2.5 dB.

    • SiGe BiCMOS: Lower cost than III-V compounds; integrated digital control; noise figure 2–4 dB at Ku-band; used in consumer-grade satellite terminals (Starlink, OneWeb, flat panel designs targeting sub-$1000 terminal cost).

    • InP HEMT: Highest frequency capability (\(f_T > 600\) GHz); noise figure \(< 0.3\) dB at millimeter-wave; used in ground-based very high-performance receivers.

    14.7.3 Thermal Management¶

    The PA dissipated power in an AESA is:

    \[P_{\mathrm{diss}} = P_{\mathrm{DC}} - P_{\mathrm{RF,out}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{RF,out}}}{\mathrm{PAE}} - P_{\mathrm{RF,out}} = P_{\mathrm{RF,out}}\left(\frac{1 - \mathrm{PAE}}{\mathrm{PAE}}\right)\tag{14.19}\]

    where PAE is power added efficiency (typically 20–40% for Ku-band GaN PAs). For a 1000-element array with 100 mW per element and 30% PAE: \(P_{\mathrm{diss}} = 100 \times 1000 \times (0.7/0.3) = 233\) W total dissipated power, requiring conductive thermal management to a cold plate or radiator.

    14.8 Multi-Beam Antennas¶

    A multi-beam antenna (MBA) generates multiple simultaneous spot beams from a single large reflector, using a cluster of feeds at or near the focal plane. Each feed illuminates the full reflector aperture and produces a spot beam pointing in the direction determined by the feed’s offset from the reflector focal point. The angular offset \(\Delta\theta\) of the beam from boresight is related to the feed lateral displacement \(\Delta s\) from focus by:

    \[\Delta\theta \approx \frac{\Delta s}{f} \quad [\mathrm{rad}]\tag{14.20}\]

    For an HTS with \(f = 1.5\) m and beam spacing of \(0.3\degree\) (\(\approx 5.2\) mrad): \(\Delta s \approx 5.2 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.5 = 7.8\) mm between feed centers.

    The fundamental limit to MBA performance is that each feed subtends a significant portion of the reflector (since the feeds fill the focal plane rather than a single focal point), degrading gain and increasing spillover. The maximum number of beams that can be efficiently served from a single reflector is roughly:

    \[N_{\mathrm{beam,max}} \approx \left(\frac{D}{2\lambda f/D}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{D^2}{2\lambda f}\right)^2\tag{14.21}\]

    In practice, 100–300 beams from a single reflector are achievable in HTS designs, with beam efficiency (ratio of in-beam to total radiated power) of 80–90%.

    14.8.1 Beam Hopping¶

    Beam hopping is a time-division approach where the satellite illuminates different beams at different time slots within a frame. A single active power channel is shared across many beams, with the beam selection controlled by a high-speed switch matrix or digital beamformer. This approach is efficient when traffic demand is highly non-uniform across beams, as the dwell time per beam can be adapted to demand. DVB-S2X includes physical layer support for beam hopping through the Super-Frame structure.

    14.9 Deployable Antenna Systems¶

    Large deployable antennas enable mobile satellite services at L- and S-band, where wavelengths are large (150–300 mm) and small antenna gain would result in extremely low EIRP. A 1 dBi gain omnidirectional handset antenna on a mobile phone can close an L-band satellite link only with a very large satellite antenna.

    14.9.1 Mesh Reflector Antennas¶

    The AstroMesh reflector (developed by Northrop Grumman, formerly TRW) is the most widely deployed large deployable mesh reflector in commercial satellite service. Key features:

    • Reflective mesh: gold-plated molybdenum wire, mesh density optimized to achieve \(<10\%\) RF transmission (reflectivity \(> 0.9\))

    • Maximum demonstrated diameter: 22 m (Inmarsat-4 satellites, L-band at 1.5/1.6 GHz)

    • Surface accuracy: RMS \(\leq 1\) mm (suitable for L-band and lower S-band)

    • Stowed volume: fits within 1.5 m diameter envelope for launch vehicle shroud compatibility

    • Deployment: driven by spring-loaded booms and tensioned cable network

    • Gain at 1.6 GHz with 12 m diameter: \(G = 10\log_{10}(0.40 \times (\pi \times 12/0.1875)^2) \approx 48\) dBi

    Viasat-3, launched in 2023, uses a large deployable reflector at Ka-band (20/30 GHz) with approximately 6 m diameter and surface accuracy \(\approx 0.1\) mm RMS required to achieve \(\eta_{\mathrm{surface}} > 0.9\) at 30 GHz (\(\lambda = 10\) mm, Ruze criterion: surface error \(< \lambda/16 \approx 0.6\) mm RMS).

    14.9.2 Wrap-Rib Reflectors¶

    Wrap-rib reflectors use a set of ribs that wrap around a central hub in the stowed configuration and spring out to support the mesh surface when deployed. This approach is simpler mechanically than AstroMesh for smaller apertures (3–6 m diameter) and was used on early MSS satellites including the Intelsat VI series.

    14.9.3 Inflatable Reflectors¶

    Inflatable antenna concepts use pressurized membrane structures. The IRMA (Inflatable Reflector Membrane Antenna) design uses a rigid foam-supported membrane inflated to maintain parabolic shape. These concepts are attractive for their exceptionally high stowage efficiency but have seen limited flight heritage due to leak concerns and susceptibility to micrometeorite damage.

    14.10 Flat Panel Phased Array User Terminals¶

    The emergence of LEO broadband constellations has driven unprecedented commercial interest in flat-panel phased array terminals. These electronically steered antennas eliminate the mechanical gimbal required for tracking a moving satellite, providing faster acquisition and higher reliability at lower cost in mass production.

    14.10.1 Starlink User Terminal (Generation 2)¶

    The SpaceX Starlink Generation 2 user terminal (“Flat High Performance” model) provides:

    • Physical dimensions: approximately 0.59 m \(\times\) 0.38 m (wide rectangular aperture)

    • Operating band: Ku-band receive (10.7–12.7 GHz), Ka-band transmit (14.0–14.5 GHz)

    • Approximate element count: 1,500–2,000 elements

    • Maximum downlink throughput: \(\geq 200\) Mbps

    • Transmit EIRP: \(\approx 46\) dBW

    • Receive \(G/T\): \(\approx 13\) dB/K

    • Scan range: \(\pm 75\degree\) from boresight (full sky hemisphere coverage)

    • Power consumption: 50–75 W during operation

    • Form factor: weatherproof, tiltable mount with 5–35recommended mounting angle

    Mass production has driven terminal cost below $500 (subsidized), with per-unit manufacturing cost targets of $200–$300 as volume scales to millions of units, enabled primarily by the transition to SiGe BiCMOS T/R modules fabricated in standard CMOS foundries (GlobalFoundries, TSMC 65 nm and below).

    14.10.2 OneWeb and Competitive Designs¶

    The OneWeb terminal (Kymeta FlatSat derivative and Hughes concept) uses a similar electronically steered aperture at Ku-band. The Intelsat/SES flat-panel terminal products for aero and maritime applications use separate transmit and receive apertures to avoid the complexity of a duplex T/R module. The maritime market, with higher terminal price tolerance, adopted electronically steered antennas earlier than residential markets.

    14.11 Digital Beamforming¶

    Digital beamforming (DBF) replaces the analog phase shifters and combiners of a classical phased array with digital processing. Each receive element (or subarray) has its own analog-to-digital converter (ADC), and beamforming weights are applied in the digital domain:

    \[y_k[n] = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} w_m^{(k)*} \cdot x_m[n]\tag{14.22}\]

    where \(y_k[n]\) is the \(k\)-th beam output, \(x_m[n]\) is the digitized signal from element \(m\), and \(w_m^{(k)}\) are the complex beamforming weights for beam \(k\). DBF enables:

    1. Simultaneous formation of \(K\) independent beams, each with arbitrary pointing, tapering, and null placement

    2. Null steering to suppress interference from specific directions

    3. Adaptive beamforming: MVDR (Minimum Variance Distortionless Response) weights:

      \[\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{MVDR}} = \frac{\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}(\theta_0)}{\mathbf{a}^H(\theta_0)\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}(\theta_0)}\tag{14.23}\]

      where \(\mathbf{R}\) is the received signal covariance matrix and \(\mathbf{a}(\theta_0)\) is the array steering vector.

    4. Software-defined beam shapes without hardware reconfiguration

    5. On-board massive MIMO processing for multi-user spatial multiplexing

    The SES Quantum satellite (Airbus Defence & Space, launched 2021) employs a fully software-defined payload with digital beamforming, allowing the beam shapes, pointing, and frequency allocation to be reprogrammed in orbit via ground command—a paradigm shift from the fixed hardware of conventional GEO satellites.

    14.12 Antenna Gain Measurement¶

    14.12.1 Far-Field Test Range¶

    Classical antenna gain measurement requires the transmitting antenna to be in the far field of the antenna under test (AUT). The far-field distance (Fraunhofer distance) is:

    \[R_{\mathrm{ff}} = \frac{2D^2}{\lambda}\tag{14.24}\]

    For a 3 m antenna at 14 GHz (\(\lambda = 21.4\) mm): \(R_{\mathrm{ff}} = 2(3)^2/0.0214 = 840\) m. This requires either an outdoor range (susceptible to multipath and weather) or an impractically large anechoic chamber.

    14.12.2 Compact Antenna Test Range (CATR)¶

    A CATR uses a precision-machined parabolic or dual-reflector system to convert the spherical wave from a nearby feed into a collimated plane wave at the AUT location, effectively simulating the far field at a distance of only a few meters. Typical CATR specifications:

    • Quiet zone diameter: 0.5–3 m

    • Amplitude taper in quiet zone: \(< 0.5\) dB

    • Phase variation in quiet zone: \(< 5\degree\)

    • Frequency range: 1–110 GHz

    • Required measurement room length: approximately \(R_{\mathrm{ff}}/10\) to \(R_{\mathrm{ff}}/50\)

    14.12.3 Near-Field Measurement¶

    Near-field measurement systems scan a probe antenna across a planar, cylindrical, or spherical surface close to the AUT and transform the measured near-field data to the far field via the Fourier transform relationship (planar near field) or wave expansion coefficients (spherical near field). Spherical near-field scanning is the gold standard for satellite antenna characterization, providing the full 4\(\pi\) steradian pattern.

    14.13 Feed Network Design¶

    The feed network distributes RF power from the transmitter to the array elements (transmit) or combines signals from elements to the receiver (receive). The choice of feed network technology depends on frequency, loss budget, and complexity:

    • Waveguide: Lowest loss (0.01–0.05 dB/m at Ku-band), used for satellite transmit feeds and high-power applications; mass penalty is significant.

    • Microstrip: Compact, low cost, easily integrated with MMIC; higher loss (0.1–1.0 dB/m at Ku-band); suitable for element-level distribution in compact arrays.

    • Butler matrix: An \(N \times N\) passive beamforming network producing \(N\) orthogonal beams from \(N\) ports, using only 90hybrid couplers and fixed phase shifters. A \(4\times 4\) Butler matrix uses 4 hybrids and 2 crossovers, producing 4 beams at fixed angles \(\theta = \pm\arcsin(1/(2N)) = \pm 14.5\degree\) and \(\pm 48.6\degree\) for \(N=4\), \(d=\lambda/2\).

    • Blass matrix: A general multiple-beam forming network using directional couplers along transmission line buses; allows arbitrary beam positions but is lossy and complex.

    • Rotman lens: A true-time-delay beamforming lens for broadband (squint-free) operation; achieves equal path lengths for multiple beam ports; naturally suited to wideband waveforms and UWB radar.

    14.14 On-Board Array Calibration¶

    Active phased arrays require periodic amplitude and phase calibration to correct for thermal drift, component aging, and manufacturing variations. Satellite-based calibration approaches include:

    1. Mutual coupling injection: A signal injected at one element couples to adjacent elements via the mutual coupling of the array and can be used to measure relative amplitude/phase.

    2. Pilot signal injection: Known calibration tones are injected at dedicated calibration ports and measured at the receive port after passing through the array manifold.

    3. Over-the-air calibration: Ground-based reference stations transmit known signals that are received by all elements; comparison with expected phase fronts yields element-level calibration corrections.

    4. Self-calibration using natural sources: Radio stars (Cassiopeia A, Cygnus A) serve as known amplitude/phase reference sources for large aperture arrays.

    Calibration accuracy requirements are driven by the sidelobe level target: to achieve \(-30\) dBc sidelobes, amplitude calibration must be within \(\pm 0.5\) dB RMS and phase calibration within \(\pm 5\degree\) RMS across the array.

    14.15 Ground Terminal Antenna Systems¶

    14.15.1 VSAT Dish Antennas¶

    Very Small Aperture Terminal (VSAT) dishes range from 0.6 m (Ku-band receive) to 2.4 m (enterprise hub stations). Mechanical construction uses stamped aluminum or fiberglass panels assembled to a parabolic contour, achieving surface accuracy of 0.5–1.0 mm RMS for Ku-band operation. The pointing alignment is typically performed manually using a signal strength meter, with \(\pm 0.2\degree\) accuracy achievable by a trained installer.

    14.15.2 Maritime and Aeronautical Stabilized Platforms¶

    Maritime and aeronautical satellite terminals must maintain pointing to the satellite despite vehicle motion. A gyro-stabilized two-axis mount (azimuth + elevation) or three-axis mount (azimuth + elevation + cross-elevation) decouples the antenna from platform motion. Platform rate sensors (fiber-optic or ring laser gyroscopes) measure angular rate, and servo drives counteract motion to maintain pointing within \(< 0.1\degree\) in elevation and azimuth. For Ku-band with HPBW \(\approx 3\degree\) (1.2 m dish), this keeps the antenna within \(<3\%\) of peak gain at all times.

    14.16 Summary¶

    Satellite antenna engineering encompasses an extraordinary range of technologies, from simple horn feeds to electronically steered arrays with thousands of active elements. The fundamental tradeoff—high gain requires large aperture, which requires either a large mechanical structure or many active elements—drives the technology choices for each application.

    Key design relationships:

    • Gain: \(G = \eta_a (\pi D/\lambda)^2\) — doubles with aperture area, increases \(\times 4\) with frequency doubling at same aperture

    • HPBW: \(\theta \approx 70\lambda/D\) — narrows proportionally with aperture at fixed wavelength

    • Phased array element spacing: \(d \leq \lambda/2\) to suppress grating lobes

    • Far-field distance: \(R_{\mathrm{ff}} = 2D^2/\lambda\) — limits antenna range measurement

    • Scan loss for planar array: \(\propto \cos\theta\) per element, totaling \(\sim\)3 dB at 60scan

    Emerging trends include mass-produced SiGe flat-panel arrays for LEO broadband user terminals, software-defined beams using on-board DBF, and deployable mesh reflectors at Ka-band with sub-millimeter surface accuracy.

    Summary of Key Antenna Relationships:

    \[\begin{align*} G &= \eta_a \left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2 && \text{(aperture gain)} \\ \theta_{\mathrm{HPBW}} &\approx 70\lambda/D \quad [\mathrm{deg}] && \text{(circular aperture)} \\ R_{\mathrm{ff}} &= 2D^2/\lambda && \text{(far-field distance)} \\ d &\leq \lambda/2 && \text{(no grating lobes, full scan)} \\ G(\theta_0) &= G_0 \cos^q\theta_0 && \text{(phased array scan loss)} \end{align*}\]

    Problems¶

    1. A parabolic reflector antenna has diameter \(D = 2.4\) m and operates at 14 GHz. Calculate: (a) the aperture area, (b) the theoretical maximum gain (\(\eta_a = 1\)), (c) the actual gain with \(\eta_a = 0.63\), and (d) the half-power beamwidth.

    2. An offset-fed parabolic reflector has a parent parabola with \(f/D_{\mathrm{parent}} = 0.45\). Explain why the offset configuration eliminates aperture blockage and describe the two main performance benefits. What new challenge does the offset geometry introduce regarding polarization?

    3. A linear phased array has 64 elements at \(d = \lambda/2\) spacing operating at 12 GHz. The beam is scanned to \(\theta_0 = 45\degree\). Calculate: (a) the required progressive phase shift between adjacent elements, (b) the grating lobe location (confirm there is no grating lobe within real space), and (c) the scan loss assuming \(\cos^1\theta\) element pattern.

    4. An AstroMesh deployable reflector has a diameter of 12 m and operates at 1.6 GHz. Using the Ruze equation, calculate the maximum permissible RMS surface error to maintain \(\eta_{\mathrm{surface}} \geq 0.90\). Compare this to the RMS surface error required at 20 GHz for the same efficiency.

    5. Calculate the total dissipated DC power in a 1024-element Ku-band AESA user terminal where each PA element delivers 50 mW RF output with 28% power added efficiency. Include the LNA DC power (5 mW per element) and phase shifter power (1 mW per element) and estimate the required thermal management area if the panel temperature must remain below \(70\degree\)C in a \(+40\degree\)C ambient with a heat flux limit of 0.5 W/cm\(^2\).
