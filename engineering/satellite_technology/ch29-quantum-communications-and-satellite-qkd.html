    Chapter 29: Quantum Communications and Satellite QKD¶

    “Anyone who is not shocked by quantum theory has not understood it.”
    — Niels Bohr

    The security of modern digital civilization rests on mathematical hardness assumptions—that factoring large integers or computing discrete logarithms is computationally infeasible. Quantum computers, leveraging Shor’s algorithm, threaten to invalidate these assumptions within the coming decades, rendering the public-key infrastructure protecting everything from financial transactions to military communications vulnerable. Quantum communications offers an alternative security paradigm grounded not in mathematical difficulty but in the fundamental laws of physics. Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), the most mature quantum communication technology, enables two parties to establish a shared secret key whose security is guaranteed by the no-cloning theorem and the postulates of quantum mechanics—any eavesdropper necessarily disturbs the quantum channel in a detectable manner.

    Satellite-based QKD is the enabling technology for global quantum-secure communications. Ground-based fiber QKD networks are limited by absorption losses: even at the optimal 1550 nm telecom wavelength, fiber attenuation of 0.2 dB/km means that photon survival probability falls to \(10^{-20}\) over 1000 km, making direct fiber QKD at intercontinental distances impossible without quantum repeaters. Free-space satellite links exploit a fundamentally different loss mechanism: diffraction spreading over vacuum, with the bulk of atmospheric absorption confined to the lowest 10 km. The Micius satellite, launched by China in 2016, demonstrated satellite-to-ground (downlink) QKD over slant ranges up to 1200 km, satellite-to-ground entanglement distribution over 1203 km, and an intercontinental quantum-secured link relayed through the satellite—milestones that validated the satellite QKD approach and triggered a global race to deploy quantum communication satellites (Liao et al. 2017).

    This chapter develops the quantum mechanical foundations necessary to understand QKD, describes the major QKD protocols in mathematical detail, examines the physical implementation challenges in free-space and satellite environments, surveys current and planned satellite QKD missions, and addresses the longer-term vision of quantum repeater networks and the relationship between QKD and post-quantum cryptography.

    29.1 Introduction to Quantum Communications¶

    Quantum communications encompasses a family of technologies that exploit quantum mechanical phenomena—superposition, entanglement, and measurement—to perform communication tasks that are impossible or insecure classically. The field divides broadly into three areas: quantum key distribution (QKD), quantum teleportation, and quantum networks (including quantum repeaters and a future quantum internet).

    QKD is the most technologically mature application. In QKD, the quantum channel transmits not data directly but random key material whose security is guaranteed by physics. Classical cryptographic algorithms (AES, ChaCha20) then encrypt the actual data using this key in a one-time-pad or cipher mode. The quantum channel need not carry high bandwidth; the security benefit is entirely in the key generation process. QKD is complementary to, not a replacement for, classical encryption algorithms; it addresses the key distribution problem specifically.

    Quantum teleportation transfers an unknown quantum state from one party to another using a pre-shared entangled pair and a classical communication channel. It does not teleport matter or information faster than light; it consumes one shared entangled pair and two classical bits per qubit teleported. Teleportation is the fundamental primitive of quantum repeaters.

    Quantum networks aim to interconnect quantum processors, sensors, and communication nodes via entanglement distribution, enabling distributed quantum computing and fundamentally new sensing and clock-synchronization capabilities. The term “quantum internet” describes a global network providing these services as infrastructure.

    Core Principle of QKD Security: Any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept or copy quantum states being transmitted necessarily perturbs those states, by the no-cloning theorem and measurement collapse. The resulting errors are detectable during classical post-processing, allowing Alice and Bob to bound the information acquired by any eavesdropper and amplify the remaining key privacy to an arbitrarily small information leakage. Security is guaranteed by physics, not by computational hardness.

    The satellite link budget for QKD differs from classical satellite communications in one critical respect: the signal consists of individual photons. While classical links require \(E_b/N_0\) ratios of 5–15 dB for reliable demodulation, QKD links operate at the single-photon level. The relevant figures of merit are photon detection rate (key sifted bits per second) and quantum bit error rate (QBER). A QBER below approximately 11% (for BB84) is required for positive secret key extraction; satellite QKD experiments have reported QBER of a few percent during night-time passes.

    29.2 Quantum Mechanics Fundamentals for Communications¶

    29.2.1 Qubits and Superposition¶

    The quantum bit, or qubit, is the fundamental unit of quantum information. Unlike a classical bit that takes value 0 or 1, a qubit can exist in any superposition of its two basis states. The general state of a qubit is:

    \[|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \quad \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{C}, \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\tag{29.1}\]

    where \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) are the computational basis (Z-basis) states, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are complex probability amplitudes, and the normalization condition \(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1\) ensures probabilities sum to unity. Upon measurement in the Z-basis, the qubit collapses irreversibly to \(|0\rangle\) with probability \(|\alpha|^2\) or to \(|1\rangle\) with probability \(|\beta|^2\).

    Bloch Sphere Representation: Any pure qubit state can be parameterized on the surface of a unit sphere (the Bloch sphere) as:

    \[|\psi\rangle = \cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|1\rangle\tag{29.2}\]

    with polar angle \(\theta \in [0,\pi]\) and azimuthal angle \(\phi \in [0,2\pi)\). The north pole represents \(|0\rangle\); the south pole represents \(|1\rangle\). The equatorial plane contains equal-superposition states including \(|{+}\rangle = (|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) (along \(+x\)) and \(|{-}\rangle = (|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\) (along \(-x\)).

    Photon Polarization Encoding: For satellite QKD, qubits are encoded in the polarization of individual photons. The four polarization states used in BB84 are:

    \[\begin{align*} |H\rangle &= |0\rangle \quad \text{(horizontal, Z-basis)} \\ |V\rangle &= |1\rangle \quad \text{(vertical, Z-basis)} \\ |D\rangle &= \frac{|0\rangle + |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \quad (\text{diagonal } +45^\circ,\ \text{X-basis}) \\ |A\rangle &= \frac{|0\rangle - |1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \quad (\text{anti-diagonal } -45^\circ,\ \text{X-basis}) \end{align*}\]

    The Z-basis \(\{|H\rangle, |V\rangle\}\) and X-basis \(\{|D\rangle, |A\rangle\}\) are mutually unbiased: measuring a Z-basis state in the X-basis gives a uniformly random result with no information about the original state. This mutual unbiasedness is the foundation of BB84’s security against intercept-resend attacks.

    29.2.2 Quantum Entanglement and Bell States¶

    A two-qubit system is entangled if its state cannot be written as a product of single-qubit states: \(|\Psi\rangle \neq |\psi_A\rangle \otimes |\psi_B\rangle\). The four maximally entangled two-qubit Bell states form a complete orthonormal basis for the two-qubit Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}_2 \otimes \mathcal{H}_2\):

    \[\begin{align*} |\Phi^+\rangle &= \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \\ |\Phi^-\rangle &= \frac{|00\rangle - |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \\ |\Psi^+\rangle &= \frac{|01\rangle + |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \\ |\Psi^-\rangle &= \frac{|01\rangle - |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \end{align*}\]

    In the \(|\Phi^-\rangle\) state, measurement of particle A in any basis is perfectly anti-correlated with measurement of particle B in the same basis, regardless of their separation (Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen correlations). This non-local correlation cannot be explained by any local hidden variable theory, as demonstrated by violation of Bell inequalities. The E91 QKD protocol (Ekert 1991) exploits entanglement as the physical resource for key generation, providing security from Bell inequality violation rather than basis randomness.

    EPR Correlations and Relativistic Causality: The correlations in entangled pairs are instantaneous and independent of distance, seemingly violating relativity. However, measurement outcomes are fundamentally random and cannot be controlled by either party, so no information is transmitted faster than light. Entanglement enables coordination but not signaling, preserving relativistic causality. The non-local correlations are detectable only through classical post-processing of both parties’ results.

    29.2.3 No-Cloning Theorem¶

    The no-cloning theorem is the fundamental quantum mechanical result that prohibits any device from creating a perfect copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. This theorem is the ultimate security guarantee of QKD, preventing eavesdroppers from copying transmitted qubits.

    Proof by contradiction: Assume that a unitary operation \(U\) exists such that for any state \(|\psi\rangle\):

    \[U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle\tag{29.11}\]

    Apply this to two orthogonal states:

    \[\begin{align*} U|0\rangle|0\rangle &= |0\rangle|0\rangle \\ U|1\rangle|0\rangle &= |1\rangle|1\rangle \end{align*}\]

    Now consider the superposition \(|\psi\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\). By linearity of quantum mechanics:

    \[U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = \frac{U|0\rangle|0\rangle + U|1\rangle|0\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{|00\rangle + |11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} = |\Phi^+\rangle\]

    But the assumed cloning operation requires \(U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle = |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle\), which evaluates to:

    \[|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle = \frac{(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)}{2} = \frac{|00\rangle + |01\rangle + |10\rangle + |11\rangle}{2}\]

    Since \(|\Phi^+\rangle \neq |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle\), we have a contradiction. Therefore no such unitary \(U\) exists, and perfect cloning of arbitrary unknown quantum states is impossible. \(\square\)

    The no-cloning theorem means that an eavesdropper Eve cannot intercept a qubit, copy it, and forward the original. Any measurement she performs collapses the quantum state, introducing detectable errors in the QKD protocol.

    29.2.4 Quantum Teleportation¶

    Quantum teleportation transfers an unknown qubit state \(|\chi\rangle = a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle\) from Alice to Bob using a pre-shared entangled pair \(|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}\) and two classical bits. The four-step protocol:

    1. Alice holds qubit \(|\chi\rangle\) plus her entangled qubit. The joint state is \(|\chi\rangle_C \otimes |\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}\).

    2. Alice performs a Bell state measurement on \(C\) and \(A\), obtaining one of four outcomes with equal probability \(1/4\), and projecting Bob’s qubit \(B\) into a state related to \(|\chi\rangle\) by a Pauli rotation.

    3. Alice sends the 2-bit measurement result to Bob via a classical channel (two classical bits).

    4. Bob applies the appropriate Pauli correction (\(I\), \(X\), \(Z\), or \(XZ\)) based on Alice’s classical message, recovering \(|\chi\rangle\) exactly.

    The original qubit at Alice is destroyed in the Bell measurement, consistent with no-cloning. Teleportation requires classical communication and does not violate relativistic causality. It is the operational primitive enabling quantum repeater networks: entanglement swapping is teleportation of one entangled particle to a distant location.

    29.3 QKD Protocols¶

    29.3.1 BB84 Protocol¶

    The BB84 protocol (Bennett and Brassard 1984), proposed by Bennett and Brassard in 1984, is the first and most widely deployed QKD protocol. Its security against arbitrary quantum attacks (including coherent attacks by an eavesdropper holding a quantum computer) has been rigorously proven in the finite-key regime.

    Protocol Steps:

    1. Qubit transmission: Alice randomly selects a bit value \(b \in \{0,1\}\) and a basis \(\beta \in \{Z,X\}\), prepares the corresponding state (\(|H\rangle\), \(|V\rangle\), \(|D\rangle\), or \(|A\rangle\)), and transmits it to Bob.

    2. Measurement: Bob randomly selects a measurement basis \(\beta' \in \{Z,X\}\) and records the outcome.

    3. Sifting: Alice and Bob communicate their basis choices (not outcomes) over a public authenticated classical channel. They retain only the approximately 50% of rounds where \(\beta = \beta'\). This forms the sifted key.

    4. Error estimation: Alice and Bob publicly compare a random subset (5–10%) of sifted key bits to estimate the QBER \(e\). Estimated bits are discarded.

    5. Error correction: An information-reconciliation protocol corrects discrepancies. Cascade is interactive and achieves \(f \approx 1.16\) efficiency; LDPC codes approach \(f \rightarrow 1\) with one-way communication.

    6. Privacy amplification: A universal hash function (Toeplitz matrix) compresses the error-corrected key by the estimated information leakage, producing a final key with arbitrarily small information available to any eavesdropper.

    Secret Key Rate: The asymptotic secret key rate (bits per transmitted pulse) against general (coherent) attacks for weak coherent pulse (WCP) sources with decoy states is:

    \[R \geq Q_\mu \left[1 - h_2(e_1)\right] - Q_\mu \cdot f \cdot h_2(E_\mu)\tag{29.16}\]

    where the binary entropy function \(h_2\) is:

    \[h_2(e) = -e \log_2 e - (1-e)\log_2(1-e)\tag{29.17}\]

    Here \(Q_\mu\) is the detection rate (counts per pulse sent), \(E_\mu\) is the observed QBER, \(e_1\) is the estimated phase error rate for single-photon pulses, and \(f \approx 1.16\) is the error correction efficiency factor. For a channel transmission efficiency \(\eta_{ch}\), detector efficiency \(\eta_d\), and mean photon number \(\mu\):

    \[Q_\mu \approx \eta_{ch} \eta_d \mu \quad \text{(for small }\eta_{ch}\eta_d\mu\text{)}\tag{29.18}\]

    QBER Security Threshold: For BB84 with one-way error correction, the maximum tolerable QBER for positive key rate is \(e_{\max} = 11.0\%\) (solution of \(1 - 2h_2(e) = 0\)). Typical satellite QKD achieves QBER of 3–6%, well below threshold. Higher QBER, e.g., from strong atmospheric turbulence or eavesdropping, signals abort.

    29.3.2 Decoy-State BB84¶

    Practical QKD sources are weak coherent pulse (WCP) lasers with Poisson photon-number statistics: \(P(n|\mu) = e^{-\mu}\mu^n/n!\) for mean photon number \(\mu \approx 0.1\). Multi-photon pulses (\(n \geq 2\)) are vulnerable to the photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack: Eve intercepts the pulse, retains one photon in a quantum memory, and forwards one photon to Bob. She later measures when Alice reveals the basis, gaining full information on multi-photon pulses without introducing QBER.

    The decoy-state method (Lo–Ma–Chen 2005) counters PNS attacks. Alice randomly interleaves:

    • Signal pulses: \(\mu \approx 0.1\) photons/pulse (key generation)

    • Weak decoy: \(\mu' \approx 0.5\) photons/pulse

    • Vacuum decoy: \(\mu_0 = 0\) (measures dark counts and stray light)

    Differences in detection statistics between intensity levels allow tight estimation of the gain \(Q_1\) and error rate \(e_1\) of the single-photon component, enabling the key rate formula:

    \[R \geq Q_1[1-h_2(e_1)] - Q_\mu \cdot f \cdot h_2(E_\mu)\tag{29.19}\]

    In its 2017 QKD experiment the Micius satellite transmitted decoy-state BB84 signals from orbit to a ground station (a satellite-to-ground downlink), obtaining key rates of the order of 1 kbps at a slant range of 1200 km and roughly an order of magnitude more at the shortest ranges of a pass (Liao et al. 2017).

    29.3.3 E91 Protocol¶

    The Ekert (E91) protocol (Ekert 1991) replaces Alice’s photon preparation with a source of entangled photon pairs, distributing one photon to Alice and one to Bob. The source may reside on the satellite (satellite-to-two-ground-stations entanglement distribution) or at a ground station. Security is verified by measuring correlations that violate the CHSH inequality.

    The CHSH (Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt) inequality for measurement settings \(A_i\) (Alice) and \(B_j\) (Bob) is:

    \[S = \left|E(A_1,B_1) - E(A_1,B_2) + E(A_2,B_1) + E(A_2,B_2)\right| \leq 2 \quad \text{(local hidden variable theories)}\tag{29.20}\]

    where \(E(A_i,B_j) = \langle A_i B_j \rangle\) is the correlation coefficient with outcomes \(\pm 1\). For the \(|\Phi^-\rangle\) Bell state with optimal measurement angles (Alice: \(0^\circ, 45^\circ\); Bob: \(22.5^\circ, 67.5^\circ\)):

    \[S_{\text{QM}} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.828 \quad \text{(Tsirelson bound, maximum quantum value)}\tag{29.21}\]

    The Micius satellite demonstrated \(S = 2.37 \pm 0.09\) across 1200 km separation, clearly exceeding \(S = 2\) and confirming that entanglement survives the free-space satellite channel. If an eavesdropper intercepts or measures the entangled pairs, entanglement degrades (the state becomes mixed), reducing \(S\) toward the classical bound and revealing the attack.

    29.3.4 Continuous-Variable QKD¶

    Continuous-variable (CV) QKD encodes key information in the quadrature amplitudes \((\hat{X}, \hat{P})\) of coherent laser states, rather than single-photon polarization. The GG02 protocol (Grosshans–Grangier 2002) operates as follows:

    1. Alice draws random displacements \((x_A, p_A) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, V_A N_0)\) from a Gaussian distribution with variance \(V_A\) in shot-noise units \(N_0\).

    2. Alice prepares coherent states \(|\alpha\rangle\) where \(\alpha = (x_A + ip_A)/2\) and transmits them to Bob through the quantum channel.

    3. Bob measures either \(\hat{X}\) or \(\hat{P}\) quadrature using homodyne detection, or both using heterodyne detection.

    4. Post-processing: parameter estimation of channel transmissivity and excess noise, reverse reconciliation (Bob’s data is the reference), privacy amplification.

    The asymptotic secret key rate against Gaussian collective attacks is:

    \[R \geq \beta I(A:B) - \chi(E:B)\tag{29.22}\]

    where \(I(A:B) = \log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\) is the classical mutual information, \(\chi(E:B)\) is the Holevo bound on Eve’s information about Bob’s key (computed from the channel covariance matrix), and \(\beta \leq 1\) is the reconciliation efficiency. CV-QKD offers the practical advantage of using standard telecom components (no cryogenic single-photon detectors), with demonstrations reaching 100 km fiber at 100 kbps (NTT, 2021). For satellite applications, atmospheric fading channels require modified security analysis.

    29.3.5 Measurement-Device-Independent QKD¶

    Practical QKD implementations face detector side-channel attacks: detector blinding (bright illumination forces InGaAs APDs into linear mode, making them responsive only to bright trigger pulses), time-shift attacks, and after-gate attacks exploit imperfections in real detectors. All such attacks are eliminated by measurement-device-independent QKD (MDI-QKD).

    In MDI-QKD, both Alice and Bob send independently prepared BB84 states to an untrusted relay node Charles, which may be controlled by the eavesdropper. Charles performs a Bell state measurement (BSM) and announces the result. The security proof shows that regardless of Charles’s actions, Alice-Bob correlations from successful BSM events satisfy the security requirements. The key rate with decoy states:

    \[R \geq Q_{11}^{ZZ}\!\left[1 - h_2(e_{11}^X)\right] - Q_{\mu\nu}^{ZZ} \cdot f \cdot h_2(E_{\mu\nu}^{ZZ})\tag{29.23}\]

    where \(Q_{11}^{ZZ}\) is the two-photon BSM gain for Z-basis transmissions, and \(e_{11}^X\) is the X-basis phase error rate estimated from decoy statistics. MDI-QKD requires Hong–Ou–Mandel (HOM) interference at Charles between photons from Alice and Bob, demanding sub-picosecond timing synchronization and sub-picometer wavelength matching—challenging but achievable with clock distribution over fiber.

    29.3.6 Twin-Field QKD¶

    The secret key capacity of any point-to-point quantum channel with transmissivity \(\eta\) is bounded by the Pirandola–Laurenza–Ottaviani–Banchi (PLOB) bound:

    \[C \leq -\log_2(1-\eta) \approx \frac{\eta}{\ln 2} \quad (\eta \ll 1)\tag{29.24}\]

    This linear scaling with \(\eta\) limits fiber QKD to impractical rates beyond \(\sim\)500 km (where \(\eta \approx 10^{-10}\)). Twin-field (TF) QKD surpasses the PLOB bound by using single-photon interference (not coincidence) at a central relay. Schematically, Alice and Bob each prepare phase-randomized WCP states and send them to a central station. The relay detects single photons by interference, and parity-check communication allows Alice and Bob to identify correlated outcomes. The key rate scales as \(\sqrt{\eta}\):

    \[R_{\text{TF}} \propto \sqrt{\eta} \quad \text{vs.} \quad R_{\text{MDI}} \propto \eta\tag{29.25}\]

    This square-root advantage enables TF-QKD over 500 km of fiber with positive key rate. In 2021, TF-QKD was demonstrated at 508 km fiber with \(R > 0\), using field-deployed fiber between two buildings in Shanghai with active phase stabilization. TF-QKD is the leading architecture for future ultra-long-distance fiber quantum backbone links.

    29.4 Physical Implementations¶

    29.4.1 Single-Photon Detectors¶

    Single-photon detector (SPD) performance determines the achievable key rate and maximum distance. Key metrics are detection efficiency \(\eta_d\), dark count rate (DCR) in counts per second, timing jitter \(\Delta t\) (affecting pulse resolution), and maximum count rate. Table 29.1 compares the three major SPD technologies.

    Table 29.1. Single-Photon Detector Technology Comparison for QKD Applications
    Parameter Si SPAD (800 nm) InGaAs SPAD (1550 nm) SNSPD (1550 nm)
    Detection efficiency 70% 25% \(>\)93%
    Dark count rate 100 cps 10,000 cps \(<\)1 cps
    Timing jitter 350 ps 200 ps \(<\)50 ps
    Max count rate 20 Mcps 10 Mcps 100 Mcps
    Operating temperature 300 K (TEC) 240 K (TEC) 0.8 K (cryocooler)
    Dead time 50 ns 10 \(\mu\)s (gated) 10 ns
    Wavelength range 400–1000 nm 1000–1700 nm 400–2000 nm
    Volume/complexity Small, low Moderate Large, cryocooler

    Superconducting Nanowire SPD (SNSPD): The SNSPD operates by maintaining a superconducting nanowire (NbN or WSi, width 50–100 nm, thickness \(\sim\)5 nm) at \(\sim\)0.8 K, biased just below the superconducting critical current. An absorbed photon locally destroys superconductivity via a hotspot mechanism, producing a voltage pulse detectable with a room-temperature RF amplifier. SNSPDs achieve detection efficiencies exceeding 93% at 1550 nm with \(<\)50 ps timing jitter and \(<\)1 cps dark count rate. The requirement for millikelvin operation (Gifford–McMahon or pulse-tube cryocoolers) is the primary integration challenge for satellite payloads. Future missions targeting 1550 nm operation will require miniaturized space-qualified cryocooler systems; progress in Joule–Thomson and Stirling-cycle miniaturization is relevant.

    InGaAs/InP SPAD: InGaAs APDs in Geiger mode are the workhorse of terrestrial telecom-wavelength QKD. Gated operation (100 ps–1 ns gates at repetition rates up to 1 GHz) suppresses afterpulsing, limiting effective detection rates. Detection efficiency of 25% and DCR of \(10^4\) cps are adequate for metropolitan fiber QKD but are inferior to SNSPDs for high-performance ground station receivers.

    Si SPAD: Silicon APDs at 850 nm were used in the Micius satellite ground stations. High efficiency (70%) and moderate DCR make them suitable for 850 nm satellite QKD despite limited telecom-band response.

    29.4.2 Entangled Photon Sources¶

    Spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) is the dominant method for generating entangled photon pairs. A pump photon (\(\omega_p\)) is converted in a second-order nonlinear crystal (\(\chi^{(2)}\) medium) into two lower-energy signal and idler photons satisfying:

    \[\begin{align*} \omega_s + \omega_i &= \omega_p \quad \text{(energy conservation)} \\ \mathbf{k}_s + \mathbf{k}_i &= \mathbf{k}_p \quad \text{(phase matching / momentum conservation)} \end{align*}\]

    Type-II phase matching in beta barium borate (BBO) or periodically poled potassium titanyl phosphate (PPKTP) crystals produces polarization-entangled pairs in the \(|\Phi^+\rangle\) or \(|\Psi^-\rangle\) Bell state with fidelity exceeding 99% in optimized laboratory sources.

    Source performance metrics:

    • Spectral brightness \(\mathcal{B}\) (pairs/s/mW pump/nm bandwidth): characterizes SPDC efficiency. Bulk BBO achieves \(\sim 10^4\) pairs/s/mW; fiber-coupled waveguide PPKTP sources achieve \(>10^6\) pairs/s/mW.

    • Pair generation rate \(R_{\text{pair}}\): total emitted pairs per second. The Micius SPDC source was reported to generate about \(5.9 \times 10^6\) pairs/s.

    • Entanglement fidelity: fraction of generated pairs in the target Bell state. for Micius the fidelity after the satellite-to-ground channel was high enough to violate the CHSH inequality (Section 29.3.3).

    • Multi-pair probability: heralded two-photon state purity limited by multi-pair events \(\propto \mu^2/2\); must keep pump power low enough that \(\mu \ll 1\).

    Space qualification: The SpooQySats program (National University of Singapore) demonstrated a miniaturized polarization-entangled photon-pair source on a 2U CubeSat (SpooQy-1) deployed from the ISS in 2019, showing that an SPDC source can operate in the space environment, including thermal cycling and radiation.

    29.4.3 Free-Space Optical Channel and Link Efficiency¶

    Total link efficiency for a satellite quantum channel is the product of all loss mechanisms:

    \[\eta_{\text{link}} = \eta_{\text{atm}} \cdot \eta_{\text{pointing}} \cdot \eta_{\text{optical}} \cdot \eta_{\text{detector}}\tag{29.28}\]

    Diffraction loss: For a Gaussian beam with transmitter aperture diameter \(D_t\) and receiver aperture diameter \(D_r\) at link range \(d\) and wavelength \(\lambda\), in the far-field regime (\(d \gg D_t^2/\lambda\)):

    \[\eta_{\text{diff}} = \left(\frac{\pi D_t D_r}{4\lambda d}\right)^2\tag{29.29}\]

    At 850 nm with \(D_t = 0.3\) m, \(D_r = 1.0\) m, \(d = 1200\) km this ideal expression gives \(\eta_{\text{diff}} \approx 5 \times 10^{-2}\) (about 13 dB). Real Gaussian beams, finite pointing and the practical divergence of the transmitter make the diffraction loss larger; a value of about 22 dB at 1200 km has been reported for Micius. Together with atmospheric, pointing and optical losses the total link loss is several tens of dB, which necessitates single-photon detection and explains why satellite QKD key rates are of the order of kbps rather than Gbps.

    Pointing loss: For a Gaussian beam with far-field divergence half-angle \(\theta_{\text{div}} = 2\lambda/(\pi D_t)\) and pointing error standard deviation \(\sigma_\theta\):

    \[\eta_{\text{pointing}} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{8\sigma_\theta^2}{\theta_{\text{div}}^2}\right)\tag{29.30}\]

    Micius achieved sub-microradian to microradian pointing accuracy, small compared with its beam divergence, with pointing loss reported to be below 3 dB. Such accuracy requires a two-stage pointing system: a coarse gimbal followed by a fast fine-steering mirror.

    Atmospheric transmission: Transmission through the turbulent atmosphere, dominated by the lowest \(\sim\)30 km, follows Beer–Lambert attenuation:

    \[\eta_{\text{atm}} = \exp\!\left(-\int_0^{\infty} \left[\alpha_{\text{abs}}(h) + \alpha_{\text{scat}}(h)\right] dh / \cos\theta_{\text{zen}}\right)\tag{29.31}\]

    Zenith transmission at 850 nm is 0.7–0.8 in clear conditions; at 45\(^\circ\) elevation, \(\cos\theta_{\text{zen}} = 0.707\) increases the effective path by \(\sqrt{2}\). Nighttime operation mitigates sky background, which at daytime would saturate SPDs with \(\sim 10^6\) photons/s in a 1 nm filter bandwidth.

    29.4.4 Polarization Control and Reference Frame Alignment¶

    Polarization-encoded QKD requires aligned reference frames between transmitter and receiver. During a satellite pass, orbital motion, attitude variations, and satellite rotation cause the polarization reference frame to rotate continuously. Two approaches address this:

    Active polarization compensation: A beacon laser at a different wavelength co-propagates with the QKD photons. The receiver monitors beacon polarization and feeds back corrections to a Pockels cell electro-optic modulator (EOM) or motorized waveplate actuator. Space systems also use a beacon for pointing and for tracking the polarization reference during the pass.

    Reference-frame-independent (RFI) QKD: The protocol is redesigned so that security does not require shared reference frames. Alice and Bob measure in three bases; two share a common alignment (Z), while a third (X-Y plane) is frame-independent. An additional parameter \(C = \langle A_x B_x \rangle^2 + \langle A_x B_y \rangle^2 + \langle A_y B_x \rangle^2 + \langle A_y B_y \rangle^2\) replaces CHSH violation for security verification. RFI-QKD relaxes the need for active polarization control at the cost of a somewhat reduced key rate.

    29.5 Satellite QKD Demonstrations¶

    29.5.1 Micius/QUESS Satellite¶

    The Micius satellite (Quantum Experiments at Space Scale, QUESS), launched 16 August 2016 into a roughly 500 km sun-synchronous orbit, is generally regarded as the first dedicated quantum communication satellite (Liao et al. 2017). Its mass was roughly 630 kg and its orbital period about 95 min. Key payload elements:

    • Spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) source of polarization-entangled photon pairs at 810–850 nm

    • Decoy-state QKD transmitter at 850 nm

    • Optical transmitter telescope with a two-stage acquisition, tracking and pointing system

    • Beacon laser and timing synchronization with the ground station

    Ground stations used in the experiments include Xinglong (near Beijing), Delingha (Qinghai), Lijiang (Yunnan), Nanshan (Urumqi), Ngari (Tibet, at high altitude, which reduces the atmospheric path) and, for the intercontinental link, the observatory at Graz in Austria.

    Landmark Achievements:

    • Satellite-to-ground QKD (Nature 549, 2017) (Liao et al. 2017): Decoy-state BB84 from Micius to the Xinglong ground station over 645–1200 km slant range, with key rates of the order of 1 kbps at the longest range. Demonstrates the feasibility of satellite QKD (a downlink: the quantum signal travels from satellite to ground).

    • Entanglement distribution (Science 356, 2017): Entangled photon pairs were sent simultaneously from the satellite to two ground stations, Delingha and Lijiang, separated by 1203 km (Yin et al., Science 356, 2017). Bell inequality \(S = 2.37 \pm 0.09 > 2\): the first distribution of entanglement over more than 1000 km.

    • Quantum teleportation from ground to satellite (Nature 549, 2017): Independent photon prepared at Ngari ground station teleported to Micius satellite via entanglement shared between Ngari and the satellite, over \(\leq 1400\) km slant range.

    • Intercontinental QKD (Phys. Rev. Lett. 120, 2018): Keys shared between Xinglong (China) and Graz (Austria) via Micius used to protect a video conference between the two countries over a ground distance of about 7600 km, with Micius acting as a trusted relay.

    Trusted Node Limitation: The Micius intercontinental relay operates as a “trusted node”: the satellite holds intermediate keys from both national networks and XORs them to relay the combined key. End-to-end information-theoretic security without trusting the satellite requires entanglement swapping with quantum repeaters, a longer-term technology goal.

    29.6 Quantum Repeaters and Long-Distance QKD¶

    29.6.1 Fundamental Limits of Direct QKD¶

    The PLOB bound \(C \leq -\log_2(1-\eta)\) limits direct QKD to impractical rates at intercontinental distances. For fiber at 0.2 dB/km:

    \[\begin{align*} d = 100~\text{km}: &\quad \eta = 10^{-2}, \quad C \leq 0.014 \text{ bits/use} \\ d = 500~\text{km}: &\quad \eta = 10^{-10}, \quad C \leq 1.4\times10^{-10} \text{ bits/use} \\ d = 1000~\text{km}: &\quad \eta = 10^{-20}, \quad C \leq 1.4\times10^{-20} \text{ bits/use} \end{align*}\]

    Even free-space satellite links are limited: at 10,000 km with 300 mm and 1 m apertures at 850 nm, ideal diffraction loss alone gives \(\eta \approx 8\times10^{-4}\) (about 31 dB), and atmospheric turbulence, pointing and optical losses add further loss. Multi-hop trusted node relaying (as in Micius) circumvents this at the cost of trusting each relay.

    29.6.2 Quantum Repeater Architecture¶

    The BDCZ (Briegel–Dür–Cirac–Zoller) quantum repeater divides a long link into \(N\) elementary segments, creating entanglement across each segment and then extending via entanglement swapping:

    1. Entanglement generation: Create Bell pairs across each elementary link of length \(L_0 = L/N\), storing photons in quantum memories at repeater nodes. Success probability per attempt: \(p_0 \approx \eta_{\text{elem}} = e^{-L_0/L_{\text{att}}}\).

    2. Entanglement swapping: Bell state measurement on two stored qubits at a repeater node creates entanglement across \(2L_0\) without requiring those photons to travel the full distance.

    3. Entanglement purification: Multiple lower-fidelity pairs are distilled to fewer higher-fidelity pairs via BBPSSW protocol: two pairs with fidelity \(F\) yield one pair with \(F' = F^2/(F^2 + (1-F)^2) > F\) for \(F > 1/2\).

    The achievable key rate with ideal quantum memories scales as \(\eta_0^{1/N}\) rather than \(\eta_0\), enabling exponential improvement. First-generation repeaters (in development as of 2025) require quantum memories and linear optical BSMs; second-generation repeaters additionally need quantum error correction; third-generation (“all-photonic”) repeaters use photonic cluster states.

    29.6.4 Experimental Quantum Networks¶

    QuTech (Delft University of Technology) demonstrated a three-node quantum network of NV-center qubits in the laboratory in 2021 (Pompili et al., Science 372, 259). The nodes, called Alice, Bob and Charlie, were linked by short optical fiber, with entanglement heralded by photons emitted at 637 nm. The experiment demonstrated:

    • Heralded entanglement generation between non-adjacent nodes (Alice–Bob) via entanglement swapping at Charlie

    • Quantum state storage in \(^{13}\)C nuclear spin memories (\(\tau_s \approx 1\) s)

    • Classical post-processing for distillation and key extraction

    This was a proof-of-principle of a multi-node quantum network with entanglement swapping between non-adjacent nodes. Later work has extended heralded entanglement between solid-state nodes to deployed fiber between cities.

    29.7 Post-Quantum Cryptography¶

    29.7.1 The Quantum Threat to Classical Cryptography¶

    Shor’s algorithm solves integer factorization (RSA) and discrete logarithm (DH, ECC) in polynomial time \(O(n^3)\) on a quantum computer, where \(n\) is the bit length of the modulus. A cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) capable of breaking 2048-bit RSA would need thousands of error-corrected logical qubits. Resource estimates by Gidney and Ekerå (2021) were about 20 million noisy physical qubits and 8 hours of running time with surface-code error correction at a physical error rate of \(10^{-3}\); later estimates are lower but still far beyond today’s machines. As of 2025, IBM Condor (1121 physical qubits, 2023) and Google Willow (105 qubits, 2024, error-correction demonstration) are examples of the state of the art.

    The “harvest-now-decrypt-later” (HNDL) threat is widely discussed: an adversary able to collect encrypted traffic today could store it until a future CRQC enables decryption. Data that must stay secret for 10–20 years (state secrets, financial records, medical data) is therefore a concern. Standards bodies therefore recommend starting the migration to quantum-resistant algorithms for such data now.

    29.7.2 NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards 2024¶

    NIST finalized three post-quantum cryptographic standards in 2024:

    • FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber): Module lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism. Security based on Module Learning With Errors (MLWE) hardness. Key sizes: ML-KEM-768 has 1184-byte public key, 1088-byte ciphertext. Replaces RSA/ECDH for key exchange.

    • FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium): Module lattice-based digital signature algorithm. Security based on Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS) and MLWE. Signature size: 2420 bytes (ML-DSA-44) to 3309 bytes (ML-DSA-65). Replaces RSA-PSS and ECDSA.

    • FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, formerly SPHINCS+): Stateless hash-based signature scheme. Security based solely on hash function security (SHA-256/SHA3), with no algebraic structure to exploit. Conservative but larger signatures (roughly 8000 to 50,000 bytes).

    FALCON (NTRU lattice-based signature) was also selected by NIST for standardization (as FN-DSA, in draft as of 2025), offering smaller signatures (about 666 bytes for Falcon-512, with an 897-byte public key) for bandwidth-constrained applications.

    QKD vs. PQC: QKD: Information-theoretically secure; guaranteed by physics; requires physical quantum channel and specialized hardware; limited key rate (\(\sim\)kbps) and distance; vulnerable to implementation side-channel attacks. PQC: Computationally secure against known quantum algorithms; may be broken by future algorithms; software-only deployment on existing infrastructure; arbitrary throughput; standard implementation maturity. Recommended hybrid: session key \(= K_{\text{QKD}} \oplus K_{\text{PQC}}\) (secure if either is secure).

    For satellite systems, QKD provides quantum-secure key distribution over the satellite link (typically refreshing daily key material for ground network segments), while PQC algorithms protect routine command/telemetry channels and can be implemented in firmware updates without hardware changes. ETSI GS QKD 014 specifies the QKD application layer interface for integration with classical network security functions.

    Chapter Summary¶

    Quantum communications exploits the postulates of quantum mechanics—superposition, entanglement, and the no-cloning theorem—to establish unconditionally secure key distribution. BB84 and decoy-state variants provide practical QKD with proven security; E91 uses Bell inequality violation for device-independent-like security; CV-QKD uses telecom components; MDI-QKD removes detector vulnerabilities; TF-QKD surpasses the PLOB bound. The Micius satellite achieved satellite-to-ground QKD over up to 1200 km (key rates of order 1 kbps), entanglement distribution over 1203 km (\(S = 2.37\)), and an intercontinental key relay over about 7600 km, validating the satellite approach. Quantum repeaters based on entanglement swapping and quantum memories (demonstrated in 3-node QuTech network) are the path to intercontinental QKD without trusted nodes. NIST FIPS 203/204/205 provide immediately deployable post-quantum cryptography, with hybrid QKD+PQC the recommended architecture for critical satellite infrastructure.
