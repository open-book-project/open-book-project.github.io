    6.1 Introduction to Satellite Power Systems¶

    The Electrical Power System (EPS) is the lifeblood of any spacecraft. Without continuous, reliable electrical energy, no other subsystem can function. The EPS must generate power from the available energy source (primarily solar radiation near Earth), store energy for use during eclipse periods when the spacecraft is shadowed by the Earth, regulate and condition the raw power into stable voltages suitable for each spacecraft load, distribute power safely to every unit on the spacecraft, and protect the spacecraft against electrical faults such as short circuits and overcurrents.

    The design of the EPS is inextricably linked to the mission requirements. The payload power demand—which for a modern high-throughput satellite (HTS) in GEO can exceed 20–30 kW—directly drives the size and mass of the solar arrays and battery system, which in turn drive structural design, launch mass, and ultimately launch cost. For a 3U CubeSat, the EPS must provide only a few watts from body-mounted solar cells in a volume of less than one unit equivalent (\(100 \times 100 \times 113.5\) mm). Understanding the engineering principles that span this enormous range of scales is the subject of this chapter.

    6.2 The Photovoltaic Effect and Solar Cell Physics¶

    6.2.1 The p-n Junction¶

    A solar cell is fundamentally a large-area semiconductor p-n junction optimised for photon absorption and minority carrier collection. When a photon with energy \(E_{ph} = h\nu\) exceeding the semiconductor bandgap \(E_g\) is absorbed in the depletion region or within a minority carrier diffusion length of it, it creates an electron-hole pair. The built-in electric field of the p-n junction separates these carriers, driving electrons toward the n-contact and holes toward the p-contact, generating a photocurrent \(I_L\) proportional to the photon flux.

    The ideal diode current-voltage relationship for a solar cell under illumination is:

    \[I = I_L - I_0 \left[ \exp\left(\frac{qV}{nkT}\right) - 1 \right]\tag{6.1}\]

    where \(I_L\) is the light-generated photocurrent (A), \(I_0\) is the reverse saturation (dark) current (A), \(q\) is the elementary charge (\(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\) C), \(V\) is the terminal voltage (V), \(n\) is the ideality factor (1 for ideal diode, 1–2 in practice), \(k\) is Boltzmann’s constant (\(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\) J/K), and \(T\) is the junction temperature (K).

    6.2.2 Key Parameters: Voc, Isc, Pmax, and Fill Factor¶

    From Equation (6.1), the key cell parameters are:

    Short-circuit current \(I_{sc}\): The current when \(V = 0\). For an ideal cell, \(I_{sc} \approx I_L\), proportional to irradiance.

    Open-circuit voltage \(V_{oc}\): The voltage when \(I = 0\):

    \[V_{oc} = \frac{nkT}{q} \ln\left(\frac{I_L}{I_0} + 1\right) \approx \frac{nkT}{q} \ln\left(\frac{I_L}{I_0}\right)\tag{6.2}\]

    Maximum power point \((V_{mp}, I_{mp})\): The point on the I-V curve that maximises the power \(P = I \times V\).

    Fill Factor \(\mathrm{FF}\): A measure of the “squareness” of the I-V curve, defined as:

    \[\mathrm{FF} = \frac{P_{max}}{V_{oc} \cdot I_{sc}} = \frac{V_{mp} \cdot I_{mp}}{V_{oc} \cdot I_{sc}}\tag{6.3}\]

    For a high-quality III-V multi-junction cell, FF is typically 0.84–0.88. The efficiency is:

    \[\eta = \frac{P_{max}}{P_{in}} = \frac{\mathrm{FF} \cdot V_{oc} \cdot I_{sc}}{S \cdot A_{cell}}\tag{6.4}\]

    where \(S\) is the solar irradiance (W/m\(^2\)) and \(A_{cell}\) is the active cell area.

    6.3 Solar Cell Technologies¶

    6.3.1 Silicon Solar Cells¶

    The earliest spacecraft solar arrays, beginning with Vanguard 1 in 1958, used silicon (Si) solar cells. Silicon has a bandgap of \(E_g = 1.12\) eV at 300 K, which is reasonably well matched to the terrestrial solar spectrum but suboptimal for the extra-terrestrial Air Mass Zero (AM0) spectrum. Beginning-of-life efficiencies of silicon space cells are typically 14–16%, rising to 18% for back-surface field (BSF) designs. Silicon cells are relatively inexpensive but suffer higher radiation degradation than III-V cells and have lower efficiency. They remain in use on cost-sensitive small satellite missions.

    6.3.2 Single-Junction Gallium Arsenide¶

    GaAs has a direct bandgap of 1.42 eV, providing excellent spectral match to the solar AM0 spectrum and high efficiency. Single-junction GaAs on Ge substrate cells achieve 18–21% efficiency BOL. The InGaP/GaAs heteroface single junction was a significant advance in radiation tolerance and efficiency. However, single-junction GaAs cells were largely superseded by multi-junction cells.

    6.3.3 Triple-Junction III-V Cells¶

    The standard space solar cell since the late 1990s is the triple-junction (3J) cell on a germanium (Ge) substrate, with the structure InGaP (top junction, \(E_g \approx 1.87\) eV)/InGaAs (middle junction, \(E_g \approx 1.40\) eV)/Ge (bottom junction, \(E_g = 0.67\) eV). The three junctions are series-connected, each converting a different portion of the solar spectrum. AM0 efficiencies of 28–30% BOL and roughly 23–26% EOL (after 15 years in GEO) are routinely achieved. Key manufacturers include Spectrolab (XTJ Prime, IMM cells), Azur Space (3G30A), and SolAero (Z4J).

    6.3.4 Four- and Five-Junction Cells¶

    Research and production have moved toward 4-junction and higher cells and toward inverted metamorphic (IMM) structures, which have demonstrated AM0 efficiencies in the low-to-mid 30% range in production and above that in laboratory records (for example, 5- and 6-junction research cells). Commercial 3-junction and IMM space cells from manufacturers such as Spectrolab, Azur Space and SolAero are generally rated at roughly 30–32% BOL as of 2025; datasheets should be consulted for current values. These higher-efficiency cells reduce solar array area and mass, which is critical for high-power satellites and for platforms where array size is mechanically constrained.

    6.3.5 Concentrator Cells¶

    Concentrating photovoltaic (CPV) systems use optical elements (Fresnel lenses or reflective troughs) to focus sunlight onto small-area, high-efficiency cells. Efficiencies of 40%+ are achievable under concentration ratios of 500–1000\(\times\). The Stretched Lens Array (SLA) developed by NASA Glenn Research Center and Entech Solar demonstrated this approach for space. However, the mechanical complexity of concentrating optics and the pointing accuracy requirements have limited adoption compared to flat-plate arrays.

    6.4 Temperature Effects and Radiation Degradation¶

    6.4.1 Temperature Coefficient of Efficiency¶

    Solar cell efficiency decreases with increasing temperature due to the narrowing of the bandgap and increased recombination. The temperature coefficient is approximately:

    \[\frac{d\eta}{dT} \approx -0.05~\text{percentage points}/\text{\degree{}C}\tag{6.5}\]

    for triple-junction cells, equivalent to a relative output loss of roughly 0.15–0.25%/C (better than crystalline silicon at roughly \(-0.4\)%/C relative). In LEO, solar cells may operate at temperatures between \(-80\)C (eclipse cool-down) and \(+80\)C (sunlit operation), while in GEO the equilibrium temperature is typically \(+50\) to \(+80\)C depending on array orientation and surface properties. Power output must be calculated at the expected operating temperature.

    6.4.2 Radiation Degradation¶

    In the space radiation environment, energetic electrons and protons from the Van Allen belts and cosmic rays create displacement damage in the semiconductor crystal lattice, reducing minority carrier lifetime and degrading cell efficiency. The degradation is quantified through the Remaining Factor (RF) or End-of-Life factor:

    \[P_{\mathrm{EOL}} = P_{\mathrm{BOL}} \times \mathrm{RF}\tag{6.6}\]

    RF is computed from the total radiation fluence (equivalent 1 MeV electron fluence) using cell degradation curves provided by the manufacturer. The fluence is calculated using trapped-particle models such as AP9/AE9 (or AP8/AE8), ray-tracing through the spacecraft shielding model, and integrating over the mission lifetime. For a GEO satellite over 15 years, 1 MeV equivalent fluence on cells with 150 \(\mu\)m cerium-doped borosilicate coverglass is approximately \(10^{15}\) e/cm\(^2\), giving RF of about 0.82 for 3J cells.

    The coverglass provides a first layer of shielding against low-energy particles. Cerium-stabilised glass (Ce-doped borosilicate) is preferred over standard glass because it does not discolour under ultraviolet radiation, which would reduce optical transmission. Standard coverglass thicknesses are 150 \(\mu\)m for power-optimised designs and up to 500 \(\mu\)m for high-radiation orbits (MEO, highly elliptical). The adhesive bonding coverglass to the cell (typically DC-93-500 silicone) also contributes to transmission losses, and its degradation must be modelled.

    6.5 Solar Array Sizing¶

    6.5.1 Power Balance During Eclipse and Sunlight¶

    The fundamental sizing constraint for the solar array is that it must supply all spacecraft loads during sunlight and charge the battery to a state of charge sufficient to supply all eclipse loads during eclipse. Using the method of Patel, the required Beginning-of-Life (BOL) array output power is:

    \[P_{\mathrm{array, BOL}} = \frac{P_s \, t_s + P_e \, t_e / (\eta_c \eta_d)}{t_s \cdot \mathrm{RF} \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{pcu}}}\tag{6.7}\]

    where:

    • \(P_e\) = eclipse power load (W)

    • \(t_e\) = eclipse duration (s or min)

    • \(\eta_d\) = battery discharge efficiency (typically 0.90)

    • \(P_s\) = sunlight power load (W)

    • \(t_s\) = sunlight duration (s or min)

    • \(\eta_c\) = battery charge efficiency (typically 0.85)

    • RF = end-of-life remaining factor

    • \(\eta_{\mathrm{pcu}}\) = power conditioning unit efficiency (0.92–0.96)

    6.5.2 Eclipse Duration for Circular Orbits¶

    For a circular orbit of altitude \(h\) and inclination \(i\), the maximum eclipse duration (at worst-case beta angle \(\beta = 0\), i.e., orbital plane contains the Sun) is:

    \[t_e = \frac{T}{\pi} \arcsin\left(\frac{R_\oplus}{R_\oplus + h}\right)\tag{6.8}\]

    where \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{(R_\oplus + h)^3/\mu}\) is the orbital period, \(R_\oplus = 6371\) km is the Earth mean radius, and \(\mu = 398600\) km\(^3\)/s\(^2\). For a 550 km LEO orbit (\(T \approx 96\) min), the maximum eclipse duration is approximately 36 minutes, or about 37% of the orbit. A GEO spacecraft experiences eclipses only during equinox seasons (\(\pm 23\) days around equinoxes), with a maximum eclipse of 72 minutes.

    6.5.3 Required Array Area¶

    Once the required array power \(P_{\mathrm{array}}\) is determined, the physical area of the array is:

    \[A = \frac{P_{\mathrm{array}}}{S_0 \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{cell, EOL}} \cdot \cos\theta \cdot (1 - f_{\mathrm{shadow}}) \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{packing}}}\tag{6.9}\]

    where \(S_0 = 1361\) W/m\(^2\) is the solar constant (mean value; varies from 1322 to 1414 W/m\(^2\) due to Earth’s orbital eccentricity), \(\eta_{\mathrm{cell, EOL}}\) is the EOL cell efficiency, \(\cos\theta\) accounts for off-normal sun incidence, \(f_{\mathrm{shadow}}\) is the fraction of array area blocked by harness/structure, and \(\eta_{\mathrm{packing}}\) is the cell packing factor (active cell area / total panel area, typically 0.88–0.93).

    6.5.4 Solar Array Configurations¶

    Solar arrays are implemented in several configurations depending on the spacecraft power requirement and mechanical constraints:

    Body-mounted arrays: Cells bonded directly to the spacecraft structure. Simple and highly reliable (no deployment mechanisms), but power is limited by the spacecraft surface area and is strongly dependent on attitude. Used in small satellites and CubeSats where deployed arrays are impractical. Typical power density is 20–40 W per face of a 1U CubeSat.

    Fixed deployable panels: Rigid or semi-rigid panels that deploy once after launch and remain in a fixed orientation relative to the spacecraft body. Used in LEO Earth observation satellites where the orbit geometry constrains panel orientation, and where the panel size is manageable. SSTL-300 uses this approach.

    Single-axis tracking (SADA): Panels rotate about one axis (typically the pitch axis for a nadir-pointing spacecraft) to track the Sun. The rotation is driven by Solar Array Drive Assemblies (SADAs) and powered by Solar Array Drive Mechanisms (SADMs). Single-axis tracking recovers most of the available solar energy and is standard for GEO satellites.

    Dual-axis tracking: Provides precise Sun pointing in both axes, maximising power output. More mechanically complex and rarely used on spacecraft, but employed for some science missions requiring precise Sun tracking.

    6.6 Battery Systems¶

    6.6.1 Nickel-Hydrogen (NiH₂) Batteries¶

    NiH\(_2\) batteries dominated space applications from the 1980s through the early 2000s. They offer exceptional cycle life (over 50,000 cycles at 40% depth of discharge for LEO applications) and good tolerance for overcharge. The nominal cell voltage is 1.25 V. Key properties:

    • Specific energy: 35–55 Wh/kg (relatively low compared to lithium-ion)

    • Depth of discharge (DoD): 60–80% for GEO, 40–50% for LEO

    • Operating temperature: \(-5\) to \(+30\)C

    • Heritage: Used on Hubble Space Telescope, ISS, and the majority of GEO commercial satellites through 2005

    The hydrogen pressure vessel in each NiH\(_2\) cell serves as the state-of-charge indicator: pressure is proportional to DoD. This is a valuable feature for on-board health monitoring.

    6.6.2 Lithium-Ion (Li-ion) Batteries¶

    Li-ion batteries have replaced NiH\(_2\) in virtually all new spacecraft designs since the mid-2000s. The primary advantages are significantly higher specific energy (150–200 Wh/kg for space-qualified cells, versus 35–55 Wh/kg for NiH\(_2\)), higher allowable DoD (up to 80–90% for short-cycle LEO missions), and smaller volume. Key properties:

    • Specific energy: 150–200 Wh/kg (cell level); 100–140 Wh/kg at battery assembly level

    • DoD: Up to 80% for LEO (frequent cycle), 70–80% for GEO eclipses (infrequent cycle)

    • Cycle life: 30,000+ cycles at 30% DoD for space-qualified cells (Saft, EaglePicher, GS Yuasa)

    • Cell voltage: 3.6 V nominal, 4.2 V fully charged, 2.5–3.0 V cutoff

    • Operating temperature: \(-20\) to \(+40\)C; storage at \(-40\) to \(+60\)C

    • Caution: susceptibility to thermal runaway if overcharged or short-circuited; requires Battery Management System (BMS)

    Notable space-qualified Li-ion cells include the Saft VES-180 (18 Ah prismatic), Saft MPS176065 (pouch), and EaglePicher BCX-2 series.

    6.6.3 Battery Sizing¶

    The battery capacity (in Ampere-hours, Ah) required to supply the eclipse load is:

    \[C_{\mathrm{bat}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{eclipse}} \cdot t_{\mathrm{eclipse}}}{\mathrm{DoD} \cdot V_{\mathrm{bus}} \cdot \eta_d \cdot N_{\mathrm{bat}}}\tag{6.10}\]

    where \(P_{\mathrm{eclipse}}\) is the total power demand during eclipse (W), \(t_{\mathrm{eclipse}}\) is the eclipse duration (h), DoD is the maximum allowable depth of discharge (dimensionless fraction), \(V_{\mathrm{bus}}\) is the regulated bus voltage (V), \(\eta_d\) is the battery discharge efficiency (0.90–0.95), and \(N_{\mathrm{bat}}\) is the number of parallel battery strings. A design margin of at least 10% is added on top of this to account for battery capacity degradation over the mission life and for uncertainty in power estimates.

    Example: For a LEO satellite with \(P_{\mathrm{eclipse}} = 180\) W, \(t_{\mathrm{eclipse}} = 36\) min = 0.60 h, DoD = 0.80, \(V_{\mathrm{bus}} = 28\) V, \(\eta_d = 0.92\), \(N_{\mathrm{bat}} = 1\):

    \[C_{\mathrm{bat}} = \frac{180 \times 0.60}{0.80 \times 28 \times 0.92 \times 1} \approx 5.24 \; \mathrm{Ah}\]

    Adding 20% margin: \(C_{\mathrm{bat, design}} \approx 6.3\) Ah, rounded up to 6.5 Ah.

    6.7 Power Control and Distribution Unit¶

    The Power Control and Distribution Unit (PCDU) is the electronic heart of the EPS. It conditions the unregulated power from the solar arrays, manages battery charge and discharge, regulates the main power bus, and distributes power to all spacecraft loads through switchable outputs with overcurrent protection.

    6.7.1 Bus Architecture¶

    Three main bus architectures are used in space:

    Fully regulated bus: The bus voltage is maintained constant (e.g., 28 V \(\pm 2\%\)) at all times, in sunlight and eclipse, through active regulation. This simplifies the design of secondary power supplies for each unit (known as DC/DC converters). The PCDU complexity is higher, but the load interfaces are simpler.

    Battery-dominated bus (unregulated): The bus voltage is directly set by the battery state of charge. The voltage varies from approximately 24 V (discharged) to 34 V (fully charged) for a 28-V nominal bus using Li-ion cells in a 7S configuration (\(7 \times 4.2 = 29.4\) V max, \(7 \times 2.8 = 19.6\) V min). Less complex PCDU but higher bus voltage variation requires more robust secondary converters.

    Partially regulated bus: Regulated during sunlight, battery-dominated during eclipse. A compromise that is less common but used on some missions.

    Standard bus voltages are 28 V (lower power, heritage from military/early commercial), 42 V (medium power), and 100 V (high power, >10 kW, reduces harness mass due to lower currents). The 100 V bus is used on the International Space Station and on modern high-power GEO satellites.

    6.7.2 Sequential Switching Shunt Regulator (S3R)¶

    The Sequential Switching Shunt Regulator (S3R) is the dominant PCDU architecture for medium to large spacecraft in Europe and is widely used globally. The S3R architecture divides the solar array into multiple parallel sections. Each section is connected to the main bus through a shunt switch (typically a MOSFET) and a series blocking diode.

    The regulation principle operates as follows: a Main Error Amplifier (MEA) compares the bus voltage to a reference. When bus voltage is above the reference (excess power from array), additional shunt sections are sequentially activated to divert current away from the bus to a shunt resistor (dissipative shunt) or to ground. When bus voltage is below reference (insufficient power), shunt sections are released, allowing their array sections to feed the bus. Battery charge is managed by a separate Battery Charge Regulator (BCR), which limits charge current. Battery discharge through the bus is managed by a Battery Discharge Regulator (BDR), which provides a regulated boost to maintain bus voltage when array power is insufficient.

    The S4R (Series Sequential Switching Shunt Regulator) adds a series switch in addition to the shunt for improved efficiency at partial load, but is less common in heritage designs.

    6.7.3 Power Distribution and Fault Isolation¶

    Power is distributed from the main bus to individual loads through either:

    Fuses: One-time overcurrent protection. Simple, cheap, and highly reliable, but require harness replacement if a fault occurs. Used for protection of critical loads and cabling.

    Solid-State Power Controllers (SSPCs): Semiconductor-based switches (typically power MOSFETs) that provide resettable overcurrent protection, remote switching capability, and current monitoring telemetry. Modern spacecraft use SSPCs extensively because they allow load management from ground commands and autonomous load shedding in contingency situations. SSPCs also eliminate the need for conventional fuse replacement during AIT.

    Load line current limits are set to protect cabling and to isolate faults without propagating to adjacent loads or the main bus. Cable sizing follows derating rules (typically 50% of rated current capacity at ambient, more conservative in vacuum where convective cooling is absent).

    6.7.4 Grounding and EMC¶

    The power system grounding scheme defines how electrical references are connected across the spacecraft and directly affects EMC performance. Two principal approaches exist:

    Single-point grounding (SPG): All grounds return to a single reference point (the structural chassis at one designated location). This prevents ground loops but requires careful management of return current paths and may result in long return conductors.

    Distributed grounding: Ground is connected at multiple points on the structure. This reduces return conductor length and impedance but creates ground loops that can couple noise between subsystems.

    In practice, most spacecraft use a hybrid approach: a single-point reference at the PCDU chassis, with the structure used as a return path but with bonds carefully managed to control impedance. Shield grounds for signal cables are typically connected at one end only to prevent shield current coupling.

    6.8 Representative Power Budget¶

    6.8.1 LEO Earth Observation Satellite Power Budget¶

    Table 6.1 presents a representative power budget for a 100 kg LEO Earth observation satellite in a 550 km sun-synchronous orbit.

    Table 6.1. Power budget for a representative 100 kg LEO Earth Observation satellite (550 km SSO, about 15 orbits/day, 36 min maximum eclipse per orbit).
    Unit Quantity Unit Power (W) Sunlight (W) Eclipse (W)
    Imaging Payload 1 80 60 (30% duty) 0
    ADCS (wheels+sensors) 1 set 30 30 30
    OBDH / OBC 1 15 15 15
    TT&C Transponder 1 20 20 20
    X-band Downlink 1 50 20 (30% duty) 0
    Thermal (heaters) – – 5 20
    Propulsion (valve/electronics) 1 5 2 (10% duty) 0
    PCDU losses – – 10 8
    Subtotal 162 93
    Power Margin (20%) 32 19
    Total Load 194 112
    Required Array (BOL+RF) \(\sim\)350 W BOL

    For this mission, taking a 36 min eclipse and 60 min of sunlight, the required BOL array power is approximately 350 W (using Equation (6.7) with RF = 0.85 for a 5-year LEO mission, \(\eta_c = 0.85\), \(\eta_d = 0.90\), \(\eta_{\mathrm{pcu}} = 0.94\)), which corresponds to an EOL array output of about 300 W. The battery capacity in the table follows from the 112 W eclipse load, a depth of discharge of 0.8, a bus voltage of 28 V and 20% margin (a lower depth of discharge would normally be chosen for a long LEO life, increasing the capacity). At an EOL cell efficiency of 26% for triple-junction cells, the required array area is approximately:

    \[A = \frac{300}{1361 \times 0.26 \times 0.98 \times 0.92} \approx 0.94 \; \mathrm{m}^2\]

    6.8.2 GEO High-Throughput Satellite Power¶

    GEO commercial telecommunications satellites have seen dramatic increases in power levels driven by the demand for bandwidth. Examples of current-generation HTS power levels include:

    • ViaSat-3 class: each satellite is designed for a throughput of the order of 1 Tbps, which requires a power system well above the typical GEO communications satellite.

    • Typical GEO HTS: roughly 15–25 kW total power generation, of which a large fraction is available to the payload.

    At these power levels, solar array specific power (W/kg) becomes a critical design driver. Modern GaAs-on-Ge flexible blanket arrays achieve 150–200 W/kg at array assembly level. Circular deployable arrays such as Northrop Grumman’s UltraFlex have also been used on GEO and science spacecraft.

    6.9 Advanced Power Technologies¶

    6.9.1 Flexible and Rollable Solar Arrays¶

    Body-mounted flexible arrays use thin-film photovoltaics (amorphous silicon, CIGS, or organic PV) bonded to flexible substrates. These have lower efficiency (10–15%) than crystalline III-V cells but offer very low areal mass and the ability to conform to curved surfaces. They are used on CubeSats and some small satellite platforms.

    The Roll-Out Solar Array (ROSA) developed by Deployable Space Systems (DSS) and flown on the International Space Station in 2017 (and used on the ISS Roll-Out Solar Array, iROSA, upgrades from 2021) demonstrates a high-power-density deployable flexible blanket approach. The array rolls out from a compact stowed configuration, achieving high specific power and very compact stowage.

    6.9.2 Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTG)¶

    For deep-space missions beyond approximately 3–4 AU from the Sun (where solar irradiance falls below 100 W/m\(^2\), making solar arrays large and heavy, although Juno has shown that solar power is feasible even at Jupiter, about 5.2 AU), or for missions to permanently shadowed craters or high-radiation environments (Europa, Titan), Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs) provide the only practical primary power source.

    An RTG converts the heat generated by the radioactive decay of a radioisotope (typically plutonium-238, which decays with a half-life of 87.7 years primarily by alpha emission) directly into electricity through an array of thermoelectric couples (thermocouples). The conversion efficiency is low (roughly 6–7% for the thermoelectric couples flown to date), but the thermal power from Pu-238 is 0.567 W per gram of \(^{238}\)PuO\(_2\). The Multi-Mission RTG (MMRTG) used on the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers produces approximately 110 W(e) at beginning of mission from about 4.8 kg of plutonium dioxide fuel.

    Enhanced Multi-Mission RTG (eMMRTG) technology using advanced thermoelectric materials (skutterudites or telluride compounds) aims to improve efficiency and end-of-life power, extending mission duration or reducing the Pu-238 fuel requirement.

    6.9.3 Solar Thermal and Nuclear Fission Power¶

    Solar thermal systems concentrate sunlight to generate heat which drives a thermodynamic cycle (Stirling or Brayton) to produce electricity. Efficiencies of 20–30% are achievable, significantly better than thermoelectric conversion, but the mechanical complexity of a heat engine in space has limited deployment. NASA’s Kilopower project (KRUSTY experiment, 2018) demonstrated a 1 kW(e) fission power system using a uranium-235 reactor and Stirling convertors, intended for future lunar and Mars surface power applications.

    6.10 Summary¶

    The electrical power system is a critical enabling subsystem for all spacecraft operations. Starting from the fundamental photovoltaic physics of the p-n junction, through multi-junction III-V cell technologies achieving 30%+ efficiency, radiation degradation modelling using the RF approach, and the Patel method for eclipse-aware array sizing, this chapter has traced the complete EPS design chain. Battery selection between NiH\(_2\) and Li-ion, and the application of the S3R regulated bus architecture implemented in the PCDU, complete the power generation, storage, and conditioning picture. Power distribution using SSPCs and fuses, grounding for EMC, and the construction of a representative LEO power budget demonstrate the practical application of these principles. High-power GEO HTS platforms pushing 25–90 kW, flexible and roll-out array technologies, and RTGs for deep-space missions represent the advanced frontier. Chapter 7 addresses the attitude determination and control system, which interacts closely with the EPS through the reaction of the spacecraft attitude to solar array drive commands.
