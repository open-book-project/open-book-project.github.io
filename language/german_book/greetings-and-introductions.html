    Lesson 2: Greetings and Introductions¶

    Begrüßung und Vorstellung

    The first things you say in a new language are always the same: hello, my name is, where are you from, how are you, goodbye. German has two ways of saying all of them, a polite way for strangers and people you meet at work, and a familiar way for friends, family, children and other young people. Choosing between them, du or Sie, is one of the first decisions every learner makes, and this lesson shows you how. You also meet your first verbs, the personal pronouns, and the two kinds of question.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

    • greet people and say goodbye at any time of day, in Germany, Austria and Switzerland;
    • introduce yourself and others, and say where you come from, where you live and which languages you speak;
    • ask and answer Wie geht's?;
    • choose between du, ihr and Sie;
    • use the personal pronouns and the present of heißen, kommen, wohnen and sprechen;
    • ask questions with a question word (wie, wo, woher, was, wer) and yes-no questions.

    2.1 Dialogue: the first day of the course¶

    In class¶

    Frau Wagner, the teacher, meets her new students.

    • Frau Wagner: Guten Morgen! Ich bin Frau Wagner, Ihre Lehrerin. Wie heißen Sie? — Good morning! I'm Frau Wagner, your teacher. What's your name?
    • Emma: Guten Morgen, Frau Wagner. Ich heiße Emma Clarke. — Good morning, Frau Wagner. My name is Emma Clarke.
    • Frau Wagner: Woher kommen Sie, Frau Clarke? — Where do you come from, Ms Clarke?
    • Emma: Ich komme aus England, aus Manchester. — I come from England, from Manchester.
    • Frau Wagner: Und wo wohnen Sie jetzt? — And where do you live now?
    • Emma: Jetzt wohne ich hier in Leipzig. — Now I live here in Leipzig.
    • Frau Wagner: Sehr schön. Herzlich willkommen! — Very nice. A warm welcome!

    In the break¶

    Emma talks to another student, Ravi. Then her flatmate Jonas, who studies at the university next door, comes past.

    • Ravi: Hallo! Ich bin Ravi. Wie heißt du? — Hello! I'm Ravi. What's your name?
    • Emma: Hallo, Ravi! Ich heiße Emma. Woher kommst du? — Hello, Ravi! I'm Emma. Where are you from?
    • Ravi: Ich komme aus Indien, aus Pune. Und du? Kommst du aus Amerika? — I'm from India, from Pune. And you? Are you from America?
    • Emma: Nein, ich komme aus England. Sprichst du Hindi? — No, I'm from England. Do you speak Hindi?
    • Ravi: Ja, ich spreche Hindi, Marathi und Englisch. Und ein bisschen Deutsch! — Yes, I speak Hindi, Marathi and English. And a little German!
    • Jonas: Hallo, Emma! Wie geht's? — Hi, Emma! How are you?
    • Emma: Gut, danke. Und dir? — Fine, thanks. And you?
    • Jonas: Super, danke. — Great, thanks.
    • Emma: Jonas, das ist Ravi. Ravi, das ist Jonas. — Jonas, this is Ravi. Ravi, this is Jonas.
    • Jonas: Hallo, Ravi! Freut mich. — Hi, Ravi! Nice to meet you.
    • Ravi: Freut mich auch. Tschüss, bis morgen! — Nice to meet you too. Bye, see you tomorrow!

    Notice:

    1. Frau Wagner and Emma say Sie to each other and use surnames: Frau Wagner, Frau Clarke. The students say du and use first names at once.
    2. The verb changes with the person: ich heiße, du heißt, Sie heißen; ich komme, du kommst.
    3. In a question with a question word, the verb comes second: Woher kommst du? In a yes-no question, the verb comes first: Kommst du aus Amerika?
    4. In Jetzt wohne ich hier, the verb comes second as well, so ich moves after it. Lesson 7 explains this important rule.

    2.3 Grammar: personal pronouns, and du or Sie¶

    The personal pronouns¶

    The personal pronouns are the words for I, you, he and so on. German has three words for you.

    Singular Plural
    1st person ich I wir we
    2nd person, familiar du you (one friend) ihr you (several friends)
    3rd person er, sie, es he, she, it sie they
    polite you Sie you (one person) Sie you (several people)

    Table 2.6. The personal pronouns.

    Two things to notice:

    • sie appears three times: sie (she), sie (they) and Sie (polite you, always with a capital). The verb and the context tell them apart: sie heißt is "she is called", sie heißen is "they are called", Sie heißen is "you are called". Lesson 5 has a figure of the three.
    • er and sie are used not only for people but for things, according to the gender of the noun (Lesson 4): a table, der Tisch, is er, "he".

    Du, ihr or Sie?¶

    German divides the people you talk to into two groups. With one group you use du (to one person) and ihr (to two or more); with the other you use Sie (to one or more). English lost a similar distinction when thou disappeared.

    • Use du and ihr with family, friends, children and animals, with other students and young people of your own age, in many workplaces among colleagues, and in many online and sports settings.
    • Use Sie with adults you do not know, with people serving you in shops, offices and restaurants, with teachers, lecturers, doctors and officials, and with most people in a formal working relationship. With Sie, use the surname: Herr Becker, Frau Wagner.

    Moving from Sie to du is a small step of friendship. It is usually offered by the older person or the person of higher status, with a phrase such as Wollen wir uns duzen? ("Shall we say du?") or simply Ich bin Jonas. (giving the first name). The verb duzen means "to say du", siezen "to say Sie".

    When in doubt

    If you are not sure, use Sie with adults and wait for the other person to offer du. Saying Sie to someone who expects du sounds a little stiff; saying du to a stranger who expects Sie can sound rude. Among young people, students and on most social media, though, du is the normal form from the start.

    2.4 Grammar: the first verbs, and two kinds of question¶

    The present of heißen, kommen and wohnen¶

    A German verb is listed in the dictionary in the infinitive, which ends in -en: kommen (to come), wohnen (to live), heißen (to be called). Take off -en and you have the stem: komm-, wohn-, heiß-. To use the verb, add an ending for each person.

    Person kommen wohnen heißen
    ich komme wohne heiße
    du kommst wohnst heißt
    er, sie, es kommt wohnt heißt
    wir kommen wohnen heißen
    ihr kommt wohnt heißt
    sie, Sie kommen wohnen heißen

    Table 2.7. The present of three verbs. The endings are -e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en.

    The endings are the same for almost every verb in the language, so learning them now is a great investment. Three tips:

    • The wir form, the sie (they) form and the Sie form are always the same as the infinitive.
    • With heißen, the du form is du heißt, not heißst: when the stem already ends in an s sound (s, ß, z), the -st ending is reduced to -t.
    • The er form and the ihr form are the same for regular verbs: er kommt, ihr kommt.

    Sprechen: a verb that changes its vowel¶

    sprechen (to speak) has the regular endings, but the e of its stem becomes i in the du and er forms. Lesson 6 explains this group of verbs; for now, learn its forms.

    Person sprechen
    ich spreche
    du sprichst
    er, sie, es spricht
    wir sprechen
    ihr sprecht
    sie, Sie sprechen

    Table 2.8. The present of sprechen.

    A first look at sein, to be

    You have already met Ich bin Ravi and Das ist Jonas. These are forms of sein, "to be", the most irregular verb in German, as to be is in English. For now, learn ich bin (I am), du bist (you are), er ist (he is), Sie sind (you are, polite). Lesson 5 gives the whole verb.

    Questions with a question word¶

    A question that asks for information starts with a question word. All German question words start with w, as most English ones start with wh:

    Question word English Example
    wie how Wie heißt du?
    wo where Wo wohnen Sie?
    woher where from Woher kommst du?
    was what Was sprechen Sie?
    wer who Wer ist das?

    Table 2.9. The first question words. Careful: German wer is "who" and wo is "where".

    The order is: question word – verb – subject. The verb is always the second element:

    • Woher kommen Sie? — Where do you come from?
    • Wo wohnt Ravi? — Where does Ravi live?

    German has no word like English do in questions: you do not say where do you come from, but simply where come you from.

    Common mistake: wer and wo

    The German question words look like the wrong English ones. wer means "who", not "where"; wo means "where", not "who". In fact the words are related to English the other way round: wer is the German relative of who, and wo of where; only the sounds have moved apart. Drill Wer ist das? Wo ist das? until the pair is automatic.

    Yes-no questions¶

    A question that can be answered with ja or nein starts with the verb:

    • Kommst du aus England? — Do you come from England? Ja, ich komme aus England.
    • Sprechen Sie Englisch? — Do you speak English? Ja, ein bisschen.
    • Wohnt Jonas in Berlin? — Does Jonas live in Berlin? Nein, er wohnt in Leipzig.

    To answer no and give the right information, use nein and a new sentence. To say that something is not so, put nicht after the verb or at the end: Ich wohne nicht in Berlin. Ich verstehe nicht.

    Worked example: from information to questions

    Ravi's details: name Ravi Kulkarni; from India; lives in Leipzig; speaks Hindi, Marathi, English. Ask about each, first with du, then with Sie.

    1. Name: Wie heißt du? / Wie heißen Sie?
    2. Country: Woher kommst du? / Woher kommen Sie?
    3. Town, as a yes-no question: Wohnst du in Leipzig? / Wohnen Sie in Leipzig?
    4. Languages: Was sprichst du? / Was sprechen Sie?

    Now describe Ravi to someone else, with er: Das ist Ravi. Er kommt aus Indien und wohnt jetzt in Leipzig. Er spricht Hindi, Marathi und Englisch.

    A conversation¶

    Read the conversation aloud with a partner, or take both parts yourself. Then do it again with your own details.

    • Guten Tag! Ich heiße Sophie Huber. Und wie heißen Sie? — Hello! My name is Sophie Huber. And what's your name?
    • Guten Tag, Frau Huber. Ich heiße Daniel Murphy. — Hello, Ms Huber. My name is Daniel Murphy.
    • Kommen Sie aus England, Herr Murphy? — Do you come from England, Mr Murphy?
    • Nein, ich komme aus Irland, aus Dublin. Und Sie? — No, I come from Ireland, from Dublin. And you?
    • Ich komme aus Österreich. Ich wohne in Wien. — I come from Austria. I live in Vienna.
    • Ah, Wien! Sehr schön. — Ah, Vienna! Very nice.

    Say it

    Introduce yourself in five sentences: your name, where you come from, where you live, which languages you speak, and a goodbye. For example:

    Hallo! Ich heiße Ravi. Ich komme aus Indien. Jetzt wohne ich in Leipzig. Ich spreche Hindi, Englisch und ein bisschen Deutsch. Tschüss!

    2.6 Culture: greetings in Germany, Austria and Switzerland¶

    Shaking hands. In formal and business situations, people in all three countries greet and say goodbye with a firm handshake, looking the other person in the eye. Friends and family often hug, and in Austria and Switzerland a kiss on the cheek between friends is common (two in Austria, usually three in Switzerland). Among young people a simple Hallo! or Hi! with a wave is normal.

    Regional greetings. The standard Guten Tag is understood everywhere, but regional greetings are what you will actually hear:

    • Grüß Gott ("God greet you") is the normal polite greeting in Bavaria, in much of southern Germany and in Austria. It is used by everyone, religious or not, and you can use it too.
    • Servus, from a Latin word meaning "servant", is a friendly hello and goodbye in Bavaria and Austria.
    • In Switzerland you say Grüezi to one person you say Sie to, Grüezi mitenand to a group, and Hoi or Sali to friends. Goodbye is often Uf Wiederluege or Tschüss, and Merci, borrowed from French, is the usual thank you.
    • In northern Germany, Moin or Moin moin means hello at any time of day, not only in the morning.

    Herr and Frau. With Sie, people use Herr or Frau and the surname: Guten Tag, Herr Becker! Frau is used for every adult woman, married or not; the old word Fräulein ("Miss") is no longer used for adults and can sound condescending. Academic titles are taken seriously, especially in Austria: a doctor of any subject is addressed as Herr Doktor or Frau Doktor, and in Austria professional titles such as Frau Magister appear on letters and doorbells.

    Knocking and greeting. Germans often greet a room when they walk in: in a waiting room or a small shop, a general Guten Tag! or Hallo! on arrival and Auf Wiedersehen! or Tschüss! on leaving is polite and expected.

    2.7 Summary¶

    Summary

    • Greetings: Hallo, Guten Morgen, Guten Tag, Guten Abend; regional Grüß Gott, Servus, Grüezi, Moin. Goodbyes: Auf Wiedersehen, Tschüss, Bis morgen, Gute Nacht.
    • How are you? Wie geht es Ihnen? (polite), Wie geht's? (informal). Gut, danke. Und Ihnen? / Und dir?
    • Introductions: Ich heiße … Ich komme aus … Ich wohne in … Ich spreche … Das ist … Freut mich.
    • Pronouns: ich, du, er, sie, es, wir, ihr, sie, Sie. sie = she / they; Sie = polite you.
    • du / ihr for friends, family, children, young people; Sie for adults you do not know and formal relations. In doubt, Sie.
    • Verb endings: -e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en (komme, kommst, kommt, kommen, kommt, kommen). du heißt; du sprichst, er spricht.
    • Questions: W-word + verb + subject (Woher kommst du?); yes-no: verb first (Kommst du aus England?).
