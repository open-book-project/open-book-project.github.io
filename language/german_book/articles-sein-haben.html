    Lesson 5: Articles, the Nominative, sein and haben¶

    Artikel, Nominativ, sein und haben

    With nouns and their genders in place, you can now build real sentences about people and things. This lesson brings together four essentials. The first is the word for "a", which, like the word for "the", has a form for each gender: ein, eine, ein. The second is kein, "no, not a", German's way of saying that something is not there. The third is the idea of case, which explains why these words sometimes change; you meet the first case, the nominative, here. And the fourth is the pair of verbs you will use in almost every conversation: sein ("to be") and haben ("to have").

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

    • use the indefinite article ein, eine, ein and the negative article kein, keine, kein, keine;
    • use the possessives mein, dein and Ihr with nouns of every gender;
    • recognise the subject of a sentence and use the nominative case;
    • conjugate sein and haben, and use them where German and English differ (Ich habe Hunger, Ich bin zwanzig);
    • say what your job is, without an article: Ich bin Lehrerin;
    • choose between nicht and kein.

    5.1 Dialogue: the birthday party¶

    Emma and Ravi arrive at Jonas's birthday party.

    • Jonas: Emma, Ravi, hallo! Kommt rein! — Emma, Ravi, hi! Come in!
    • Emma: Alles Gute zum Geburtstag, Jonas! Hier, ein Geschenk für dich. — Happy birthday, Jonas! Here, a present for you.
    • Jonas: Oh, danke! Das ist ein Buch! Super. — Oh, thanks! It's a book! Great.
    • Ravi: Wer ist die Frau da? Ist das deine Freundin? — Who is the woman there? Is that your girlfriend?
    • Jonas: Nein, das ist keine Freundin, das ist meine Schwester Lena. Sie ist Ärztin. — No, that's not a girlfriend, that's my sister Lena. She's a doctor.
    • Ravi: Und der Mann? Ist er auch Arzt? — And the man? Is he a doctor too?
    • Jonas: Nein, das ist mein Bruder Tim. Er ist Koch. Habt ihr Hunger? — No, that's my brother Tim. He's a cook. Are you hungry?
    • Ravi: Ja, ich habe Hunger! Und Durst. — Yes, I'm hungry! And thirsty.
    • Jonas: Die Pizza ist in der Küche, und die Getränke auch. — The pizza is in the kitchen, and the drinks too.
    • Lena: Hallo! Ich bin Lena. Bist du Emma aus England? — Hi! I'm Lena. Are you Emma from England?
    • Emma: Ja, genau. Und du bist Ärztin, oder? — Yes, exactly. And you're a doctor, aren't you?
    • Lena: Ja. Und was bist du von Beruf? — Yes. And what do you do for a living?
    • Emma: Ich bin Lehrerin, aber jetzt bin ich Studentin. Ich habe ein Jahr frei. — I'm a teacher, but now I'm a student. I have a year off.
    • Lena: Toll! Dein Deutsch ist sehr gut. — Great! Your German is very good.
    • Emma: Danke, du bist sehr nett. Aber ich habe noch viele Probleme mit der Grammatik! — Thanks, you're very kind. But I still have lots of problems with grammar!

    Notice:

    1. "a" is ein or eine: ein Buch, ein Geschenk, ein Jahr. "Not a" is kein or keine: keine Freundin.
    2. "My" and "your" also change with the gender: mein Bruder, meine Schwester, deine Freundin, dein Deutsch.
    3. Jobs have no article: Sie ist Ärztin. Er ist Koch. ("She is a doctor. He is a cook.")
    4. German says "I have hunger": Ich habe Hunger.

    5.3 Grammar: articles and the nominative case¶

    What a case is¶

    In English, he and him are the same person in different roles: He sees the dog but The dog sees him. These roles are called cases. English shows them only in a few pronouns (I / me, he / him, she / her, we / us, they / them, who / whom). German shows them also in the articles and possessives before nouns. German has four cases:

    Case Main job Taught in
    nominative the subject: who or what does or is something this lesson
    accusative the direct object: who or what is affected Lesson 8
    dative the indirect object, and after many prepositions Lesson 11
    genitive possession ("of"), mostly in writing for recognition only

    Table 5.5. The four cases.

    The nominative is the case of the subject, the person or thing that does the action or that the sentence is about. It is also the case after sein ("to be") and heißen: in Das ist ein Buch, both das and ein Buch are in the nominative. All the forms you have learnt so far are nominative forms, and the nominative is the form in which nouns are listed in dictionaries.

    To find the subject, ask who? (wer?) or what? (was?) before the verb: Mein Bruder ist Koch. Who is a cook? Mein Bruder: the subject, nominative.

    The indefinite article: ein, eine, ein¶

    The word for "a" or "an" has a form for each gender. Masculine and neuter look the same; feminine adds -e. There is no plural, as in English: "a book", but "books".

    Masculine Feminine Neuter Plural
    definite: the der Tisch die Lampe das Buch die Bücher
    indefinite: a ein Tisch eine Lampe ein Buch Bücher
    negative: no, not a kein Tisch keine Lampe kein Buch keine Bücher
    possessive: my mein Tisch meine Lampe mein Buch meine Bücher

    Table 5.6. The articles and possessives in the nominative. Switch on Practice and fill them in.

    The rule to remember: ein, kein and the possessives add -e for feminine and plural, and nothing for masculine and neuter. Learn one line and you have them all.

    The negative article kein¶

    kein means "no", "not a" or "not any". It is the negative of ein and of nouns with no article, and it takes the same endings as ein, with a plural form keine:

    • Das ist ein Problem. → Das ist kein Problem. — That's no problem.
    • Das ist eine Freundin. → Das ist keine Freundin. — That's not a girlfriend.
    • Ich habe Zeit. → Ich habe keine Zeit. — I have no time.
    • Hier sind Stühle. → Hier sind keine Stühle. — There are no chairs here.

    The possessives mein, dein, Ihr¶

    "My", "your" (familiar) and "your" (polite) are mein, dein and Ihr, and they take the same endings as ein:

    • mein Bruder, meine Schwester, mein Kind, meine Eltern
    • dein Name, deine Adresse, dein Handy, deine Freunde
    • Ihr Name, Ihre Adresse, Ihr Handy, Ihre Freunde

    The ending depends on the noun that follows, not on the owner: Emma's brother is mein Bruder when she speaks, and Jonas's sister is meine Schwester when he speaks. Lesson 9 gives the possessives for every person (his, her, our, their).

    Jobs and nationalities without an article¶

    After sein and werden ("to become"), German uses no article before a job, a nationality or a religion:

    • Ich bin Lehrerin. — I'm a teacher.
    • Er ist Koch. — He's a cook.
    • Sie ist Engländerin. — She's English (an Englishwoman).

    To ask about someone's job, say Was sind Sie von Beruf? or Was bist du von Beruf? ("What are you by profession?"), or simply Was machst du beruflich?. The article comes back when you add an adjective: Er ist ein guter Koch. ("He's a good cook.")

    Common mistake: Ich bin ein Lehrer

    Ich bin ein Lehrer is a direct translation of I am a teacher, and it sounds wrong in German. Leave out the article: Ich bin Lehrer. And use the feminine form for a woman: Ich bin Lehrerin.

    5.4 Grammar: sein and haben¶

    The forms¶

    sein and haben are irregular, as to be and to have are in English. They are the two most common verbs in the language, and you will also need them to form the past tense in Lesson 14. Learn them by heart.

    Person sein (to be) haben (to have)
    ich bin habe
    du bist hast
    er, sie, es ist hat
    wir sind haben
    ihr seid habt
    sie, Sie sind haben

    Table 5.7. The present of sein and haben.

    Notice that ihr seid ("you are", plural) is spelt with a d, and that seit with a t is a different word ("since"). In haben, only the du and er forms are irregular: the b disappears, du hast, er hat.

    Sie, sie and sie¶

    You can now see the three meanings of sie from Lesson 2 at work. The verb tells "she" apart from the other two; only the capital letter tells "they" from "you".

    At the start of a sentence, where every word has a capital, only the context tells "they" from polite "you": Sie sind sehr nett. can mean "They are very nice" or "You are very nice".

    Using sein¶

    sein links the subject to a description, a job, a place, an age or a name:

    • Ich bin müde. — I'm tired.
    • Lena ist Ärztin. — Lena is a doctor.
    • Wir sind in Leipzig. — We are in Leipzig.
    • Das ist mein Bruder. — This is my brother.
    • Jonas ist dreiundzwanzig. — Jonas is twenty-three.

    The word after sein that describes the subject is in the nominative, like the subject itself: Der Mann ist mein Bruder.

    Using haben¶

    haben means "to have", for what you own, your family, your time, and in many fixed phrases:

    • Ich habe ein Auto. — I have a car.
    • Hast du eine Schwester? — Have you got a sister?
    • Wir haben zwei Kinder. — We have two children.
    • Habt ihr heute Zeit? — Are you free today?

    The thing you have is not the subject but the object of the sentence, and it is in a different case, the accusative. With feminine, neuter and plural nouns the accusative looks exactly like the nominative, so all the examples above are already correct. Only a masculine noun changes: ein becomes einen, kein becomes keinen, mein becomes meinen:

    • Ich habe einen Bruder. — I have a brother.
    • Er hat keinen Computer. — He has no computer.

    Use these phrases as they are for now; Lesson 8 explains the accusative fully.

    Where German and English differ¶

    Three everyday situations take haben or another construction in German where English uses "to be", or the other way round:

    English German Not
    I'm hungry, I'm thirsty Ich habe Hunger. Ich habe Durst. Ich bin hungrig (possible, but much less common)
    I'm twenty Ich bin zwanzig. Ich habe zwanzig Jahre (as in French or Spanish)
    I'm cold, I'm hot Mir ist kalt. Mir ist warm. Ich bin kalt (this means "I am a cold person")
    I'm lucky Ich habe Glück. Ich bin glücklich (this means "I'm happy")
    You're right Du hast recht. Du bist richtig

    Table 5.8. Differences between German and English. Learn Mir ist kalt as a fixed phrase: mir is "to me" (Lesson 11).

    Nicht or kein?¶

    German has two words for "not". Choose by looking at what you are negating:

    • Use kein before a noun that would otherwise have ein or no article: Ich habe kein Auto. Das ist keine gute Idee. Wir haben keine Zeit.
    • Use nicht for everything else: with verbs, adjectives, names, and nouns with der, die, das or a possessive: Ich bin nicht müde. Das ist nicht mein Buch. Er ist nicht Jonas. Ich verstehe das nicht.

    Worked example: negating sentences

    Make each sentence negative.

    1. Das ist ein Problem. The noun has ein → kein: Das ist kein Problem.
    2. Ich habe Geschwister. ("brothers and sisters", plural, no article) → keine: Ich habe keine Geschwister.
    3. Das ist meine Tasche. The noun has a possessive → nicht: Das ist nicht meine Tasche.
    4. Lena ist verheiratet. An adjective → nicht: Lena ist nicht verheiratet.
    5. Ich habe einen Bruder. Masculine, accusative einen → keinen: Ich habe keinen Bruder.

    Who is it?¶

    Read the description aloud, then describe two people you know in the same way.

    • Das ist meine Freundin Sophie. — This is my friend Sophie.
    • Sie ist sechsundzwanzig und wohnt in Wien. — She's twenty-six and lives in Vienna.
    • Sie ist Ingenieurin. — She's an engineer.
    • Sie hat einen Bruder, aber keine Schwester. — She has a brother, but no sister.
    • Sie ist nicht verheiratet. — She's not married.

    Say it

    Introduce yourself with sein and haben: your name, age, job, family, and how you feel today. For example:

    Ich bin Ravi. Ich bin zweiunddreißig Jahre alt. Ich bin Informatiker. Ich habe eine Schwester. Heute bin ich müde, und ich habe Hunger.

    5.6 Culture: work and job titles¶

    What do you do? Germans tend to introduce themselves with their job more readily than with their hobbies, and a training is valued as much as a degree. Many young people in Germany, Austria and Switzerland learn a trade or profession through a dual vocational training (die duale Ausbildung): for two to three and a half years they work in a company and attend a vocational school at the same time. Cooks, mechanics, bank clerks, nurses, electricians and hundreds of other professions are trained this way, and the system is widely admired. A person in training is an Azubi, short for Auszubildende or Auszubildender.

    Job titles for women and men. Almost every job title has a feminine form in -in, and it is normal to use it: a woman says Ich bin Ingenieurin, not Ingenieur. Job advertisements must be open to all and usually show this in the title, for example Verkäufer (m/w/d), for männlich, weiblich, divers (male, female, diverse).

    Talking about groups. When speaking of a mixed group, German traditionally used the masculine plural: die Lehrer for all teachers. Today many speakers, institutions and newspapers prefer forms that name both, and you will meet several of them: Lehrerinnen und Lehrer; neutral words such as die Lehrkräfte (teaching staff) or die Studierenden (those who study); and written forms with a sign in the middle, such as Lehrer*innen or Lehrer:innen. These forms are the subject of lively public debate, and usage differs between people and organisations. As a learner, you should recognise them; in your own German, Lehrerinnen und Lehrer is always correct and polite.

    5.7 Summary¶

    Summary

    • Cases: nominative (subject), accusative, dative, genitive. The nominative is used for the subject and after sein and heißen.
    • Indefinite article: ein (m), eine (f), ein (n); no plural.
    • Negative article: kein, keine, kein; plural keine. Possessives mein, dein, Ihr take the same endings.
    • sein: bin, bist, ist, sind, seid, sind. haben: habe, hast, hat, haben, habt, haben.
    • Jobs without article: Ich bin Lehrerin. Was sind Sie von Beruf?
    • German vs English: Ich habe Hunger / Durst. Ich bin zwanzig. Mir ist kalt. Du hast recht.
    • haben + masculine object: einen, keinen, meinen (Lesson 8).
    • kein for nouns with ein or no article; nicht for the rest.
