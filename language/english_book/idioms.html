    Chapter 41: Idioms¶

    Idioms — the ones a dictionary will not explain

    An idiom is a phrase whose words mean one thing, while the whole phrase means something completely different. It's not rocket science has no rocket and no science in it — it means "this is not a hard task". The real benefit of learning idioms is in understanding, not in speaking. Until you can recognise ten idioms properly, half of every meeting goes over your head. And for speaking? Five or six are enough — but used in the right place.

    In one line

    Learn twenty to recognise, five to speak. Someone who uses too many idioms does not sound like a native — they sound like they memorised a list.

    41.1 The first rule of idioms — they never change¶

    An idiom is a frozen mould. Change a single word inside it and it breaks — the listener will think you made a mistake, not that you found a new way to say it.

    Broken idioms

    • Wrong: It's not rocket technology.
    • Right: It's not rocket science.

    • Why: The words of an idiom are fixed — synonyms do not work.

    • Wrong: Let's play it from ear.

    • Right: Let's play it by ear.

    • Why: Even the small words are fixed. Change one preposition and the idiom is gone.

    • Wrong: He is on the same boat.

    • Right: He is in the same boat.
    • Why: People sit "inside" a boat, not "on top of" it — so it is in the same boat.

    41.2 Three signals — which idiom, where¶

    This is the most important part. Sort idioms into three boxes: safe everywhere, fine at the office but not in writing, and never in formal settings. In interviews, client calls and written emails, the third box is completely closed.

    41.3 Idioms for work and decisions¶

    Idiom Meaning Signal
    on the same page to understand things the same way Green — fine in email too
    in the long run over a long period of time Green
    play it by ear to decide as things develop Yellow
    back to the drawing board to start thinking again from the beginning Yellow
    the ball is in their court it is now their decision Yellow
    on the back burner set aside for now Yellow
    cut corners to take shortcuts and lower the quality Yellow
    call it a day to stop work for today Yellow
    bite the bullet to do a difficult thing bravely Red — too casual
    • Before we start, let's make sure we're all on the same page. = Before we begin, let's be sure we all understand the same thing.
    • We don't have the numbers yet, so let's play it by ear. = The figures are not here yet, so we will decide as we go.
    • I've sent the proposal — the ball is in their court now. = I have sent the proposal — now it is up to them.
    • It's seven already — shall we call it a day? = It is already seven o'clock — shall we stop for today?

    41.4 Idioms for people and relationships¶

    Idiom Meaning Signal
    see eye to eye to agree Green
    in the same boat to be in the same situation Yellow
    a safe pair of hands a person you can trust Green — good for praise
    rub someone the wrong way to irritate someone without meaning to Yellow
    pull someone's leg to tease someone as a joke Red — only among friends
    let someone off the hook to free someone from a duty or blame Yellow
    get cold feet to become nervous at the last moment Yellow
    • We don't always see eye to eye, but we work well together. = We do not always agree, but we work well together.
    • Put her on the project — she's a safe pair of hands. = Put her on the project — you can rely on her.
    • Everyone's waiting on the same approval — we're all in the same boat. = Everyone is waiting for the same approval — we are all in the same situation.

    41.5 Money, time and difficulty¶

    Idiom Meaning Signal
    in the pipeline being prepared, coming soon Green
    a long shot unlikely to succeed, but worth trying Yellow
    down to the wire lasting until the very last moment Yellow
    cost an arm and a leg very expensive Red — casual
    tighten one's belt to spend less money Yellow
    a drop in the ocean so small that it makes no difference Green
    round the clock all day and all night Green
    a piece of cake very easy Red — sounds childish
    • Two more features are in the pipeline for next quarter. = Two more features are being prepared for next quarter.
    • It's a long shot, but it costs us nothing to ask. = It probably will not work, but asking costs nothing.
    • The approval came through right down to the wire. = The approval arrived at the very last moment.

    41.6 Idioms you should not use in formal settings¶

    There are three different reasons — and each has a different cure.

    a) Too casual — they sound childish

    a piece of cake, hit the sack, spill the beans, under the weather, pull someone's leg, cost an arm and a leg.
    In an interview, "The test was a piece of cake" sounds both arrogant and childish. Say this instead: "The test was straightforward." In formal settings, drop the idiom and use the plain word — your point becomes clearer.

    b) Too worn out — clichés

    think outside the box, give 110 percent, at the end of the day (again and again), touch base, hit the ground running, low-hanging fruit.
    They are not wrong — they have just been used so often that the listener stops paying attention. In an interview, "I always give 110 percent" has zero effect. Give a real example instead.

    c) Not understood outside India

    Some phrases are completely normal in India, but on an international call they bring blank faces:
    "Do the needful" → "Please go ahead and process it."
    "Same to same" → "Exactly the same."
    "What is your good name?" → "May I have your name?"
    "Hats off to him" → fine, but "Full credit to him" is more common.
    Be careful with cricket idioms too — "He was on a sticky wicket" or "clean bowled" will not be understood by anyone in America. There is nothing wrong with these phrases — they are part of Indian English. The only question is whether the other person will understand.

    41.7 How to manage without idioms¶

    The most useful skill is this: say the plain meaning instead of the idiom. That way you will never get caught out.

    Casual idiom Formal version
    It was a piece of cake. It was straightforward.
    Let's play it by ear. Let's decide once we have more information.
    We're back to the drawing board. We're revisiting the approach.
    It cost an arm and a leg. It was extremely expensive.
    I'm under the weather. I'm not keeping well today.
    Let's call it a day. Let's stop here and continue tomorrow.
    The ball is in their court. We're waiting for their decision.
    It's on the back burner. It's been deprioritised for now.

    A trick that always works

    If you are unsure about using an idiom, say a clear line first, then add the idiom:
    "We're waiting on their decision — the ball's in their court." The meaning is already clear, so even if the idiom is not understood, the conversation does not stop.

    41.8 Mistakes people make with idioms¶

    Pay attention

    • Wrong: We are on the same page with this.
    • Right: We're on the same page about this.

    • Why: After this idiom comes "about": on the same page about the plan.

    • Wrong: Please do the needful and revert back.

    • Right: Please go ahead and let me know once it's done.

    • Why: This is completely normal in India, but both phrases sound foreign abroad — and in "revert back", the "back" is unnecessary.

    • Wrong: In the interview I said the test was a piece of cake.

    • Right: I said the test was straightforward.

    • Why: In an interview, a casual idiom sounds arrogant. The plain word sounds more confident.

    • Wrong: I will touch base you tomorrow.

    • Right: I'll touch base with you tomorrow.

    • Why: "With" is needed. Besides, this idiom is worn out — plain "I'll check in with you" is better.

    • Wrong: Every point he made was same to same.

    • Right: Every point he made was exactly the same.

    • Why: "Same to same" is not understood abroad. It works in India, not on an international call.

    • Wrong: She spilled the beans in the client presentation.

    • Right: She shared the details early in the client presentation.
    • Why: The idiom is fine, but the place is wrong. In front of a client, an idiom weakens the meaning.

    41.9 Practice — Idioms¶

    Exercise 1

    What is better to say about a test in an interview?

    1. The test was straightforward.
    2. The test was a piece of cake.
    3. The test was child's play.
    4. The test was nothing for me.

    Hint: A casual idiom sounds arrogant.

    Answer

    The test was straightforward.

    In formal settings, drop the idiom and use the plain word. 'A piece of cake' sounds both childish and arrogant.

    Exercise 2

    What does 'The ball is in their court' mean?

    1. It is now their decision
    2. They are playing a game
    3. It is their fault
    4. We have won
    Answer

    It is now their decision

    The next move belongs to the other side. It is a polite way to say you are waiting at the office.

    Exercise 3

    Which idiom is completely safe in emails and client calls too?

    1. in the long run
    2. hit the sack
    3. spill the beans
    4. pull my leg
    Answer

    in the long run

    'In the long run' is from the green box — it works in formal writing too. The other three are very casual.

    Exercise 4

    What should you say on an international call instead of 'Please do the needful'?

    1. Please go ahead and process it.
    2. Please do the necessary.
    3. Kindly do the needful immediately.
    4. Please needful it.
    Answer

    Please go ahead and process it.

    'Do the needful' is clearly understood in India but sounds foreign abroad. Say plainly what needs to be done.

    Exercise 5

    Let's make sure we're all on the same ___ before we start.

    Hint: Understanding things the same way.

    Answer

    page

    'On the same page' = understanding things the same way. After it comes 'about': on the same page about the plan.

    Exercise 6

    We don't have the numbers yet, so let's play it by ___.

    Hint: A part of the body.

    Answer

    ear

    'Play it by ear' = decide as things develop. Note: 'by ear', not 'from ear'.

    Exercise 7

    The idiom is broken — tap the wrong word.

    We are all on the same boat this month.

    Answer

    Wrong word: on.

    Corrected: We are all in the same boat this month.

    People sit inside a boat — 'in the same boat'. The small words of an idiom are fixed.

    Exercise 8

    One word is unnecessary — find it.

    Please do the needful and revert back to me.

    Answer

    Wrong word: back.

    Corrected: Please go ahead and let me know once it's done.

    'Revert' already means 'come back', so 'back' is unnecessary. And it is better to write the whole sentence plainly.

    Exercise 9

    Match each idiom with its meaning.

    AB
    call it a dayto become nervous at the last moment
    cut cornersa person you can trust
    get cold feetto stop work for today
    a safe pair of handsto take shortcuts and lower the quality
    Answer

    call it a day — to stop work for today; cut corners — to take shortcuts and lower the quality; get cold feet — to become nervous at the last moment; a safe pair of hands — a person you can trust

    You will hear all four at the office every day — recognising them is necessary, speaking them is optional.

    Exercise 10

    Match each casual idiom with its formal version.

    AB
    It was a piece of cakeI'm not keeping well
    It cost an arm and a legIt's been deprioritised
    I'm under the weatherIt was straightforward
    It's on the back burnerIt was extremely expensive
    Answer

    It was a piece of cake — It was straightforward; It cost an arm and a leg — It was extremely expensive; I'm under the weather — I'm not keeping well; It's on the back burner — It's been deprioritised

    This is the most useful skill: say the plain meaning instead of the idiom. That way you will never get caught out.

    Exercise 11

    How many idioms are enough for speaking?

    1. Five or six, but in the right place
    2. The more the better
    3. At least fifty
    4. None — idioms are wrong
    Answer

    Five or six, but in the right place

    Learn twenty to recognise, five to speak. Someone who uses too many idioms sounds like they memorised a list, not like a native.

    Exercise 12

    Put the words in the right order — a line for the office.

    Words: are / the / ball / court / in / decision / We / waiting / is / their / for / so / their

    Answer

    We are waiting for their decision so the ball is in their court

    This is the trick: plain meaning first, then the idiom. Even if the idiom is not understood, the point is complete.

    Exercise 13

    Say this line. In 'same page' both words get equal stress.

    Before we start let us make sure we are on the same page

    Answer

    Say: Before we start let us make sure we are on the same page

    This is the most common line for starting a meeting. You can write it in an email too — it is a green-box idiom.

    What to do next

    Draw three columns on a sheet of paper — green, yellow, red. Whenever you hear a new idiom, put it into one of the columns straight away. In two months you will have your own list, and it will be more useful than any list from a book — because it comes from your own surroundings.
