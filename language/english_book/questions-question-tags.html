    Chapter 18: Questions & question tags¶

    Asking questions — the order changes, not the words

    In Hindi, asking a question is very easy: "Tum ja rahe ho." and "Tum ja rahe ho?" — the words are the same, only the tone changes. This does not work in English. In English, to make a question you have to change the word order — the helping verb comes in front of the subject. That is why "Where you are going?" sounds wrong to the ear, and "Where are you going?" sounds just right.

    In one line

    In an English question, the helping verb comes before the subject. The helping verb (am/is/are/was/were/do/does/did/have/has/will/can…) moves in front of the subject. That is the whole rule.

    18.1 The question machine — in one picture¶

    Look below: there is a plain statement, and two kinds of questions made from it. Notice how the helping verb jumps in front of the subject.

    18.2 Yes/No questions — three situations¶

    (a) When the sentence already has a helping verb¶

    Just bring it to the front. Nothing needs to be added.

    Statement Question
    She is ready. Is she ready?
    They were late. Were they late?
    He has finished. Has he finished?
    You can swim. Can you swim?
    It will rain. Will it rain?

    (b) When there is no helping verb — bring in do / does / did¶

    In the Present Simple and Past Simple the helping verb is hidden. When you make a question, it comes out — and the main verb goes back to V1.

    Statement Question Notice
    You play cricket. Do you play cricket? I/you/we/they → do
    She plays cricket. Does she play cricket? the -s moved into "does"
    He went home. Did he go home? the past moved into "did", verb is V1

    The tense shows in only one place

    Does she plays? ✗ · Does she play? ✓
    Did you went? ✗ · Did you go? ✓ do/does/did has already done the tense job — the main verb stays plain.

    (c) "am / is / are" is a helping verb by itself¶

    • Are you free tomorrow? Are you free tomorrow? — you never say "Do you are free".
    • Is this seat taken? Is anyone sitting here already?

    18.3 WH-words — when "yes/no" is not enough¶

    The WH-word always comes first, and after it the order is the same as in a yes/no question: WH + helping + subject + verb.

    WH-word What it asks Example
    What a thing / an action What do you want?
    Who a person (subject) Who called you?
    Whom a person (object) Whom did you call?
    Whose whose it is Whose bag is this?
    Which which one from given options Which shirt do you like?
    When time When does the bus leave?
    Where place Where are you going?
    Why reason Why did she leave early?
    How manner / condition How did you come?
    How much how much (uncountable) How much sugar do we need?
    How many how many (countable) How many brothers do you have?
    How long how much time / how long How long have you worked here?
    How often how many times How often do you exercise?
    How far how much distance How far is the station?

    Questions about the subject — no do/does/did here

    If the WH-word itself is the subject (who did it? what happened?), there is no need to change the order — because the subject is already at the front.
    Who broke the window? "Who did break the window?" is wrong. Who broke the glass — "Who broke".
    What happened yesterday?

    18.4 Question tags — "right?"¶

    In Hindi we add "na?", "hai na?", "theek hai?" at the end of a sentence. English does the same, but the tag is the opposite of the sentence: a negative tag after a positive sentence, and a positive tag after a negative sentence.

    Sentence Tag Full form
    Positive negative You are tired, aren't you?
    Negative positive She doesn't smoke, does she?
    Positive, past negative He came late, didn't he?
    Positive, perfect negative They have left, haven't they?
    Positive, modal negative You can drive, can't you?

    Three steps to make a tag

    1. Pick up the helping verb (if there is none, bring in do/does/did).
    2. Flip it — positive → negative, negative → positive.
    3. Turn the subject into a pronoun — Ravi → he, my sisters → they, this book → it.

    Four special tags you must remember¶

    Sentence Tag Why
    I am late, … aren't I? "amn't" does not exist in English
    Let's go, … shall we? Let's = a suggestion
    Open the door, … will you? used with an order / request
    Nobody came, … did they? the pronoun for nobody / everybody is "they"
    There is a problem, … isn't there? "there" takes the place of the subject

    'isn't it?' does not work everywhere

    • Wrong: You are coming tomorrow, isn't it?
    • Right: You are coming tomorrow, aren't you?

    • Why: The helping verb and the pronoun of the tag must come from the sentence. "Isn't it" is only right when the subject is "it" — It is cold, isn't it?

    • Wrong: He finished the work, no?

    • Right: He finished the work, didn't he?
    • Why: Hindi "na?" does not turn directly into "no?" — you have to build the full tag.

    18.5 Polite questions — the order goes back to normal¶

    When a question sits inside another sentence (a polite or indirect question), the inner part is written like a normal statement. Many people get this wrong.

    Direct question Polite form
    Where is the station? Could you tell me where the station is?
    What time does it start? Do you know what time it starts?
    Why did he leave? I wonder why he left.
    Can I sit here? Would you mind if I sat here?
    • Could you please tell me where the ticket counter is? Notice: not "where is the ticket counter", but "where the ticket counter is".

    18.6 The mistakes we really make¶

    Top question mistakes

    • Wrong: Where you are going?
    • Right: Where are you going?

    • Why: The helping verb "are" must come before the subject "you". Copying the Hindi word order is the most common mistake here.

    • Wrong: Why you did not come yesterday?

    • Right: Why didn't you come yesterday?

    • Why: WH + helping + subject + verb. "Didn't" first, "you" after it.

    • Wrong: What is your good name?

    • Right: May I know your name? / What's your name?

    • Why: "Good name" is a word-for-word translation of the Hindi "shubh naam" — English speakers don't say it. To sound polite, "May I know your name?" is best.

    • Wrong: Does she knows the answer?

    • Right: Does she know the answer?

    • Why: "Does" has taken the -s, so the main verb stays without -s.

    • Wrong: Tell me where is the office.

    • Right: Tell me where the office is.

    • Why: Inside an indirect question the order stays normal — the helping verb goes to the back.

    • Wrong: He is your brother, isn't it?

    • Right: He is your brother, isn't he?

    • Why: Take the pronoun from the sentence. The subject is "he", so the tag also has "he".

    • Wrong: What you do?

    • Right: What do you do?
    • Why: This is the standard question for asking someone's job, and "do" appears twice in it — once as the helping verb, once as the main verb.

    18.7 A small map¶

    Type of question Order
    Yes / No HELPING + subject + verb + rest?
    WH (about the object) WH + HELPING + subject + verb?
    WH (about the subject) WH + verb + rest? (no do/does/did)
    Question tag Sentence + opposite helping verb + pronoun?
    Indirect / polite Polite opening + WH + subject + verb

    18.8 Practice — Questions and question tags¶

    Exercise 1

    Which one is correct?

    1. Where you are going?
    2. Where are you going?
    3. Where you going are?
    4. Where going you are?

    Hint: Where does the helping verb sit?

    Answer

    Where are you going?

    The order is WH + helping + subject + verb. 'Are' must come before 'you'.

    Exercise 2

    ___ she live in Delhi?

    1. Do
    2. Does
    3. Did
    4. Is

    Hint: Present Simple, subject 'she'.

    Answer

    Does

    With he/she/it we use 'Does'. And remember — the verb after it has no -s: 'Does she live', not 'lives'.

    Exercise 3

    You have finished the project, ___ ?

    1. isn't it
    2. haven't you
    3. have you
    4. didn't you

    Hint: The sentence is positive, so what will the tag be?

    Answer

    haven't you

    Positive sentence → negative tag. The helping verb is 'have' and the subject is 'you', so 'haven't you'.

    Exercise 4

    ___ broke this glass?

    1. Who did
    2. Whom did
    3. Who
    4. Who does

    Hint: Here the WH-word is itself the subject.

    Answer

    Who

    When the WH-word is the subject, there is no do/does/did — 'Who broke this glass?' The verb follows directly.

    Exercise 5

    ___ you like some tea?

    Hint: The polite word for offering.

    Answer

    Would / would

    'Would you like…?' is the polite way to offer something. It sounds more polite than 'Do you want'.

    Exercise 6

    How ___ brothers do you have?

    Hint: Can brothers be counted or not?

    Answer

    many

    Use 'how many' for countable things and 'how much' for uncountable ones. Brothers can be counted.

    Exercise 7

    Let's start the meeting, ___ we?

    Hint: This is a special tag.

    Answer

    shall

    'Let's' always takes the tag 'shall we?' — this one you have to remember.

    Exercise 8

    There is one mistake in this question — tap the wrong word.

    Does your sister knows my address?

    Answer

    Wrong word: knows.

    Corrected: Does your sister know my address?

    'Does' has taken the -s into itself, so the main verb stays plain — 'know', not 'knows'.

    Exercise 9

    Find the wrong word.

    Please tell me where is the railway station.

    Answer

    Wrong word: is.

    Corrected: Please tell me where the railway station is.

    Inside an indirect question the order stays normal. 'Is' has to move after the subject.

    Exercise 10

    There is a mistake in the tag — catch it.

    She writes poems, isn't she?

    Answer

    Wrong word: isn't.

    Corrected: She writes poems, doesn't she?

    The sentence is in the Present Simple, where the hidden helping verb is 'does' — so the tag is 'doesn't she'. 'Isn't' would be right only if the sentence had 'is'.

    Exercise 11

    Put the words in the correct order to make a question.

    Words: been / city / in / have / you / this / long / How / living

    Answer

    How long have you been living in this city

    How long + helping verbs (have been) + subject (you) + main verb (living). 'How long you are living' is the wrong order.

    Exercise 12

    Match each meaning with the correct WH-word.

    AB
    Asking about distanceWhose
    Asking how many timesWhich
    Asking who owns itHow much
    Choosing from given optionsHow far
    Asking the amount of sugarHow often
    Answer

    Asking about distance — How far; Asking how many times — How often; Asking who owns it — Whose; Choosing from given options — Which; Asking the amount of sugar — How much

    How far = distance, how often = number of times, whose = who owns it, which = choosing from options, how much = the amount of an uncountable thing.

    Exercise 13

    Now say this question aloud — your voice should rise at the end.

    Could you please tell me where the bus stop is

    Answer

    Say: Could you please tell me where the bus stop is

    This is one of the most useful sentences — at a station, at a shop, anywhere. Remember — 'where the bus stop is', not 'where is the bus stop', because this is an indirect question.

    What to do next

    From today, whenever you ask a question, pause for one second in your mind, find the helping verb and put it first. At the start this will feel slow — but this one habit makes your English clearer straight away.
