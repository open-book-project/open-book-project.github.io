    Chapter 32: Small talk & opinions¶

    Small talk and giving opinions — starting, keeping going, ending

    The office lift, the wedding buffet, the tea break at a conference — these two or three minutes of talk build relationships. Many of us go quiet here, because one question keeps going round in the mind: "What do I say?" The truth is that small talk needs nothing deep — it only needs a frame: how to start, how to keep it going, how to give your opinion, and how to leave gracefully. Every line has a 🔊. Listen, say it along, and try it with someone tomorrow.

    32.1 The small-talk map — four steps¶

    When the path is clear in your mind, half of the nervousness goes. Every good short conversation passes through these four steps — and the middle two steps go round again and again.

    Four steps

    Open → Follow up → Share → Close. The middle two steps (a question and your own thought) go round again and again — this is what keeps the conversation alive. The goal of small talk is not to collect information, it is to connect.

    Topics to avoid at a first meeting

    Safe: weather, traffic, the event you are at, food, work, travel, movies, cricket.
    Avoid: salary, "when are you getting married?", weight, religion, politics. Among us these questions are asked with affection, but in an English-speaking setting they feel very personal.

    32.2 Starting a conversation — weather, work, and where you both are¶

    The easiest opener is whatever is in front of both of you — the same place, the same weather, the same food. One short statement, then a question. That is all.

    Where English line Listen
    Wedding / party So, how do you know the bride and groom?
    Conference Is this your first time at this conference?
    Weather It's so hot today, isn't it?
    Rain Looks like the rain's finally here.
    Work What kind of work do you do?
    Office How's your week going so far?
    Food Have you tried the paneer? It's really good.
    A new face Hi, I don't think we've met. I'm Kavya.

    The opener formula

    Statement + question: "Great music tonight. Do you know the band?" The statement sets the scene; the question gives the other person a chance to speak. A statement alone ("Hot day.") or a question alone ("Your name?") feels unfinished.

    Mini-dialogue — the tea break at a conference

    Situation: it is the tea break at a conference. You are standing alone, and another person beside you is also alone. Say your lines first, then Meera's lines too.

    • You: Hi! Is this your first time at this conference? The opening — a question about the place you are both in.
    • Meera: No, second time, actually. Last year's was in Bengaluru. "actually" = in fact. She gave an extra detail — a good sign.
    • You: Oh, nice. I'm Rohan, by the way. I work in logistics. "by the way" lets the name slip in easily, in the middle.
    • Meera: Nice to meet you, Rohan. I'm Meera. I'm a data analyst. A name and a job — now the way is open for a follow-up.
    • You: So what brings you here this year? = Why did you come this year? A very natural follow-up.
    • Meera: Mostly the AI sessions. My team wants to try some new tools. Now you can ask: "Oh, which tools?" — and the talk is rolling.

    32.3 Keeping it going — follow-up questions¶

    Conversations often die right here: a question comes, a one-word answer goes back, then silence. There are two tools to avoid this — the ping-pong answer, and good follow-up questions.

    The ping-pong answer

    Answer + one extra detail + a question back. "Pune. I moved here for work last year. What about you?" A one-word answer drops the ball on the floor — then both people just stand there.

    • Oh, really? How did you get into that? = How did you start that work? The best question about anyone's job.
    • What's that like? = What is it like? Use it for a new city, a new job, anything.
    • That sounds interesting. What do you enjoy most about it? = What is the best part? People happily talk about what they like.
    • How long have you been doing that? = Since when have you done this?
    • And what about you? = And you? The shortest line for sending the ball back.

    Turn a closed question into an open one

    Closed: "Do you like Pune?" → the answer is only yes or no.
    Open: "What do you like about Pune?" → the answer brings a story. Questions that start with what, how or why keep the talk going longer.

    32.4 Giving your opinion — I think, In my view, To be honest¶

    An opinion brings life to a conversation. But when you translate straight from Hindi, an opinion often sounds too harsh. In English an opinion is usually kept a little soft — and how soft is something you choose with the phrase.

    Line When Listen
    I think the new metro has really helped. An everyday, normal opinion
    In my view, the second half of the film was better. Thoughtful, a little formal
    To be honest, I didn't enjoy the food much. A truth the other person may not like
    Personally, I prefer working from the office. Your own taste — not forcing it on others
    I might be wrong, but I think it's going to rain. Very soft, you are not sure
    If you ask me, cricket is more fun in the stadium. Casual, among friends

    Opinion + reason

    An opinion on its own sounds weak — add because: "I think remote work is great because I save two hours a day." And in place of "According to me", say "I think" or "In my view".

    32.5 Agreeing and disagreeing — without a fight¶

    Disagreeing is not bad; the way you do it can be. A plain "No, you are wrong" ends the talk right there. English speakers first acknowledge what the other person said, and then give their own view.

    The disagreeing formula

    Acknowledge + but + your view. "That's a fair point, but I think traffic is the bigger problem." The first part shows respect, the second gives your opinion. You need both.

    Mini-dialogue — a debate about working from home

    Situation: at lunch, your colleague Aman says working from home is better. You half agree and half do not.

    • Aman: I think working from home is so much better. An opinion — with "I think".
    • You: I know what you mean. No traffic, right? Acknowledge first — "I understand you".
    • Aman: Exactly! I save almost two hours a day. Opinion + reason.
    • You: That's true. But to be honest, I find it harder to focus at home. Acknowledge + but + your view. Disagreeing without a fight.
    • Aman: Fair enough. I guess it depends on the person. "Fair enough" = OK, you have a good point.
    • You: Yeah, I suppose it does. Both are happy — the argument is over, the friendship stays.

    32.6 Ending the conversation — gracefully¶

    A good conversation should have a good ending too. Turning away without a word, or suddenly saying "OK, bye", feels rude. Three small steps — and you leave a good memory behind.

    • Well, it was really nice talking to you. "Well…" — the shortest and clearest signal.
    • I should let you get back to your friends. = I will not keep you here. The reason is about them — a very polite way.
    • I'm going to grab some food before it's all gone. A light, casual reason — useful at parties.
    • I need to catch up with a colleague, but let's talk later. "catch up with" = to meet someone and hear their news.
    • Let's stay in touch. Are you on LinkedIn? Use it if you want the relationship to continue.
    • Enjoy the rest of the evening! The last warm line.

    The three-step ending

    Signal ("Well…") + reason ("I should go and say hi to my boss") + warm line ("It was lovely meeting you"). Keep the reason short and true. Disappearing without a word, or a flat "OK, bye" — both feel rude.

    32.7 A complete conversation — from start to finish¶

    Now everything together — opener, follow-up, ping-pong, opinion, agreement and ending. Say both roles; saying the other person's lines also gets your ear ready.

    Mini-dialogue — the wedding buffet

    Situation: a long buffet queue at the reception. Priya is standing in front of you, and you do not know her.

    • You: This queue is moving so slowly! Everything looks delicious, though. The opener — a statement about what is in front of you.
    • Priya: I know! Someone told me the biryani is the best thing here. She picked up the thread and added a detail.
    • You: Good to know. So, are you from the bride's side or the groom's? The easiest follow-up at a wedding.
    • Priya: The bride's. We went to college together. And you? Ping-pong: answer + detail + a question back.
    • You: I work with the groom. I'm Vikram, by the way. The name comes in with "by the way".
    • Priya: I'm Priya. Nice to meet you. What do you two work on? An open question — "What are you working on?"
    • You: We build apps for hospitals. It's busy, but I really enjoy it. An answer + a feeling. Now Priya has something to talk about.
    • Priya: That sounds interesting. I think technology in healthcare is the future. An opinion — with "I think".
    • You: I couldn't agree more. Well, I'll let you get your food. It was lovely talking to you. Agreement, then signal + reason + warm line.
    • Priya: You too, Vikram. Enjoy the biryani! A good ending — both smile and part.

    32.8 The mistakes we really make¶

    These are less grammar mistakes and more translation mistakes — they come from carrying a Hindi pattern into English. The listener understands, but it sounds odd.

    Common small-talk mistakes

    • Wrong: What is your good name?
    • Right: What's your name? / Sorry, I didn't catch your name.

    • Why: "Good name" is a word-for-word copy of "shubh naam". It carries respect for us, but sounds odd in English.

    • Wrong: Myself Rahul, from Pune.

    • Right: I'm Rahul. I'm from Pune.

    • Why: An introduction does not start with "Myself". Just "I'm…" — short and natural.

    • Wrong: I am agree with you.

    • Right: I agree with you.

    • Why: "Agree" is a verb by itself — it does not need "am". The same for the negative: "I don't agree", not "I am not agree".

    • Wrong: According to me, the film was boring.

    • Right: I thought the film was boring. / In my view, it was boring.

    • Why: "According to" is for other people's words — according to the news, according to my doctor. For your own opinion use "I think".

    • Wrong: So, how much do you earn? When are you getting married?

    • Right: So, what do you enjoy most about your job?
    • Why: Salary and marriage are seen as very personal at a first meeting. Ask about the work, not the earnings.

    32.9 Practice — Small talk¶

    Exercise 1

    You want to start talking to a stranger at a conference tea break. Which is the most natural opener?

    1. Is this your first time at this conference?
    2. What is your good name?
    3. How much salary do you get?
    4. Myself Rohan, tell me about you.
    Answer

    Is this your first time at this conference?

    A question about the place you both share — the safest and the most natural. The other three are either translations or too personal.

    Exercise 2

    What should you add to your opinion to make it stronger?

    1. a reason with 'because'
    2. the word 'definitely' twice
    3. 'according to me' at the start
    4. a louder voice
    Answer

    a reason with 'because'

    Opinion + reason = a strong opinion. 'I think X because Y' — the listener may disagree, but will not take you lightly.

    Exercise 3

    You disagree with a colleague. Which reply is the most polite?

    1. You are wrong.
    2. That's a fair point, but I see it differently.
    3. No, no, that is not correct.
    4. I am not agree.
    Answer

    That's a fair point, but I see it differently.

    The formula: acknowledge + but + your view. The first part shows respect. 'I am not agree' is also wrong grammar.

    Exercise 4

    I ___ with you. The traffic is terrible these days.

    Hint: You do not need 'am'.

    Answer

    agree / totally agree / completely agree

    'Agree' is a verb by itself — 'I agree', not 'I am agree'.

    Exercise 5

    Well, it was really nice ___ to you.

    Hint: A line for ending a talk — verb + ing.

    Answer

    talking / speaking

    'It was nice talking to you' — the warmest line for the end of a conversation.

    Exercise 6

    One word in this introduction sounds odd — tap it.

    Myself Anjali and I work in a bank.

    Answer

    Wrong word: Myself.

    Corrected: I'm Anjali and I work in a bank.

    An introduction starts with 'I'm…', not 'Myself…'.

    Exercise 7

    Put the words in the right order — make an open follow-up question.

    Words: do / What / most / job / you / your / about / enjoy

    Answer

    What do you enjoy most about your job

    Wh-word + do + you + verb — this is an open question, and its answer is not just yes or no.

    Exercise 8

    Match each line to its job.

    AB
    How did you get into that?disagreeing politely
    To be honest, I didn't like it.ending the conversation
    I see your point, but…follow-up question
    I'll let you get back to it.an honest opinion
    Answer

    How did you get into that? — follow-up question; To be honest, I didn't like it. — an honest opinion; I see your point, but… — disagreeing politely; I'll let you get back to it. — ending the conversation

    Every line has a job — a question, an opinion, a disagreement, an ending. These steps keep small talk moving.

    Exercise 9

    Say the line for leaving a party — warm and clear.

    It was really nice talking to you

    Answer

    Say: It was really nice talking to you

    Join 'talking to you' together — 'tawking-tu-yu'. The voice falls on the last 'you'.

    Exercise 10

    Now show how to disagree politely.

    That's a fair point, but I see it differently

    Answer

    Say: That's a fair point, but I see it differently

    Pause a little after 'but' — it shows you really listened to them.

    Exercise 11

    Say an opener — with the voice rising at the end of the question.

    Is this your first time here

    Answer

    Say: Is this your first time here

    In a yes/no question the voice rises on the last word — 'here?'

    What next?

    This week, have a four-step conversation with one new person — it could be the society guard or the new intern at the office. The first time will feel odd; by the third time it will be a habit.
