    Chapter 2: The sounds of English¶

    English sounds — the ones Hindi doesn't have

    English has 26 letters, but about 44 sounds. This is where all the trouble starts: we read the letters, and we assume that what is written is what we should say. In Hindi, that assumption works — you say what is written in Devanagari. In English, it doesn't. In this chapter we will work on the sounds that your tongue has never made until now — because Hindi never needed them.

    In one line

    Your pronunciation is not wrong because something is "missing" in you — it is wrong because your mouth has only learnt Hindi positions so far. Learn the new position, and the sound will come by itself. This is a skill, not luck. A muscle just needs to be taught.

    2.1 Written ≠ spoken¶

    First, listen to these three sets. The words look alike, but they sound completely different:

    Words Listen What people wrongly say What it really is
    put / but both "ut" put = "puut", but = "bat"
    come / home both "ome" come = "kum", home = "hoam"
    though / through / tough all three "ough" the same tho, throo, taf — three different sounds

    So, one rule

    When you see a new word, first listen, then speak. Don't guess from the spelling. That is why every line in this book has a 🔊.

    2.2 Eight sounds that Hindi doesn't have¶

    These are the eight that make or break your pronunciation. For each one, the mouth position is written down — read it and try it right away. It is better if you have a mirror in front of you.

    Sound Words Where to put your mouth Listen
    /θ/ TH without voice think, three, mouth Put the tip of your tongue just out between your upper and lower front teeth. Blow air out; no sound from the throat. It is not the Hindi "थ" — in that one the tongue stays behind the teeth.
    /ð/ TH with voice this, they, mother Same tongue position — between the teeth. But now add a buzz in the throat. Put a finger on your throat; you should feel it vibrate.
    /v/ very, van, love Put your upper teeth on your lower lip, and make a sound from the throat. The lips are not rounded. The teeth must touch.
    /f/ fan, phone, laugh Exactly the same position as /v/ — upper teeth on the lower lip — but no sound from the throat, only air. It is not the Hindi "फ"; that one uses both lips.
    /z/ zoo, buzz, is, was Make an /s/ — tongue behind the upper teeth, thin air — and add a buzz in the throat. Like the buzzing of a bee.
    /æ/ cat, man, bad Open your mouth wide, jaw down, as if showing your throat to a doctor. A sound between "अ" and "ऐ". It is not "कैट".
    /ə/ schwa about, doctor, sofa Leave your mouth completely relaxed and let out a light "uh". No effort at all. This is the most common sound in English — and the most ignored.
    /ʒ/ vision, measure, usual Make a "sh", then add a voice from the throat in the same position. "zh" — not like "झ", but a long, buzzing "sh".

    2.3 Where is the tongue — a map of three positions¶

    In Hindi, "त" puts the tongue behind the teeth, and "ट" touches the palate (the roof of the mouth). The English T is in between the two — on the raised ridge just behind the teeth. And TH goes out between the teeth. Look at this map:

    Try it now — 10 seconds

    Say "टाइम". Then move your tongue slightly back and say "time". You will hear the difference clearly.

    2.4 Short and long vowels — one mistake, a hundred words¶

    In Hindi we know the difference between "इ" and "ई". English has the same difference, but the spelling doesn't show it — so we ignore it. The result: "ship" and "sheep" sound the same. A long vowel is not just longer; the mouth shape is also a little different.

    • The ship is big. / The sheep is big. = A boat is big / an animal is big. One small slip, and the meaning changes.
    • My glass is full. / He is a fool. = My glass is not empty / he is a silly person. Stretching "full" can be dangerous!

    2.5 Schwa — the biggest secret of English¶

    The most common sound in English is not a, e, i, o or u — it is /ə/ schwa: a completely relaxed, effortless "uh". A syllable that is not stressed almost disappears in English and turns into a schwa. We Indian speakers say every syllable in full — that is why our English sounds flat and a bit robotic.

    Word What we say What it really is Listen
    doctor dok-TOR DOK-tuh (relaxed ending)
    about E-BAUT uh-BOWT (relaxed beginning)
    computer KOM-PYU-TAR kuhm-PYOO-tuh
    banana BA-NA-NA buh-NAA-nuh
    support SUP-PORT suh-PORT

    The schwa rule

    Don't say the unstressed part fully — swallow it. In a word only one part shines; the other parts stay in the background. There is a whole "Rhythm and stress" chapter on this — but start here.

    2.6 Hindi habits that English catches immediately¶

    Five habits, five cures

    • Wrong: "iskool", "istation", "ispeak"
    • Right: school, station, speak — straight from "s"

    • Why: Hindi words don't begin with two consonants, so we add an "i" in front. Cure: first say only "sssss", then add "kool" in the same breath — "sss-kool".

    • Wrong: "filim" (film), an extra sound inside "homewark"

    • Right: film — "l" and "m" together, nothing in between

    • Why: Between two consonants we slip in a small "i" without noticing. Say it slowly: "fi—lm".

    • Wrong: "wery" or "vindow" — mixing up V and W

    • Right: very (teeth on lip) / window (rounded lips)

    • Why: Hindi has one "व"; English has two different sounds. The full cure is in the next chapter.

    • Wrong: "is" with an "s" sound — buzz as "bus"

    • Right: is, was, buzz — with a buzz in the throat

    • Why: /z/ is not in Hindi, so we slip back to /s/. Put a finger on your throat and say "zzzz" — you should feel it vibrate.

    • Wrong: Giving every word equal stress — "I AM GO-ING TO MAR-KET"

    • Right: I'm GOing to the MARket — two words shine, the rest are relaxed
    • Why: English rhythm is uneven. Speaking in a flat, even way is the biggest "accent" that people notice.

    2.7 Silent letters — written but not spoken¶

    Silent letter Words Say Listen
    K (kn-) know, knife, knee no, naif, nee
    W (wr-) write, wrong, wrist rait, rong, rist
    B (-mb) comb, climb, thumb koam, klaim, tham
    L talk, walk, half, could tok, wok, haaf, kud
    T listen, castle, often lisun, kaasul, ofun
    H hour, honest, honour aawar, onist, onar

    A small, sure gain

    Just remembering these six lines will make an immediate difference to your speaking — because these words come up every day. In "I don't know" you don't say the K at all.

    2.8 The daily 5-minute drill¶

    Say these five lines once every day. All the new sounds come together in them:

    • I think these three things are true. The TH drill — "think/these/three/things" has two kinds of TH.
    • Very few of my friends have a van. V and F — in both, the upper teeth touch the lower lip.
    • The zoo was closed because of the rain. The /z/ drill — zoo, was, closed, because: all four have a buzz in the throat.
    • The school bus stops at the station. The "s" start drill — never "iskool", "istop", "istation".
    • The doctor asked about my computer. The schwa drill — DOK-tuh, uh-BOWT, kuhm-PYOO-tuh. Keep the unstressed parts relaxed.

    2.9 Practice — English Sounds¶

    Exercise 1

    How many sounds are there in English?

    1. About 44
    2. Exactly 26
    3. About 33
    4. Exactly 52

    Hint: There are 26 letters — the sounds are more than that.

    Answer

    About 44

    26 letters, but about 44 sounds. That is why guessing pronunciation from spelling is wrong.

    Exercise 2

    What happens in your mouth when you say /v/?

    1. Upper teeth on the lower lip, buzz in the throat
    2. Both lips rounded together
    3. Tongue between the teeth
    4. Tongue pressed to the palate

    Hint: Where are the teeth?

    Answer

    Upper teeth on the lower lip, buzz in the throat

    For /v/ the teeth must touch the lip. If you round your lips, it becomes /w/.

    Exercise 3

    What is the difference between the TH in 'think' and in 'this'?

    1. 'this' has a buzz in the throat, 'think' does not
    2. In 'this' the tongue stays behind
    3. 'think' uses both lips
    4. No difference, they are the same sound
    Answer

    'this' has a buzz in the throat, 'think' does not

    The tongue position is the same in both — between the teeth. The only difference is voice: think = only air, this = buzz.

    Exercise 4

    Which is the most common sound in English?

    1. Schwa /ə/ — a relaxed 'uh'
    2. The long /iː/ as in 'sheep'
    3. /t/
    4. /æ/ as in 'cat'

    Hint: The one that needs no effort from the mouth.

    Answer

    Schwa /ə/ — a relaxed 'uh'

    Unstressed parts turn into a schwa: DOK-tuh, kuhm-PYOO-tuh. Saying every letter in full is what creates a 'flat' accent.

    Exercise 5

    Why do we say 'iskool' for 'school'?

    1. Hindi words don't begin with two consonants
    2. 'sch' means 'isk'
    3. English speakers say it that way too
    4. 's' is a silent letter
    Answer

    Hindi words don't begin with two consonants

    The tongue adds a vowel in front for comfort. Cure: first stretch 'sss', then say 'kool' in the same breath.

    Exercise 6

    Remove the silent letter — which letter in 'know' is never spoken? ___

    Hint: The first letter.

    Answer

    k / K

    The K in kn- is always silent: know = no, knife = naif, knee = nee.

    Exercise 7

    Which letter is silent in 'listen'? ___

    Answer

    t / T

    listen = li-sun. In the same way, castle = kaa-sul, and the T in 'often' is almost silent too.

    Exercise 8

    Match each sound to its mouth position.

    AB
    /θ/ thinkboth lips rounded, teeth don't touch
    /v/ verythe /s/ position + a buzz in the throat
    /w/ wettongue between the teeth, only air
    /z/ zooupper teeth on the lower lip
    Answer

    /θ/ think — tongue between the teeth, only air; /v/ very — upper teeth on the lower lip; /w/ wet — both lips rounded, teeth don't touch; /z/ zoo — the /s/ position + a buzz in the throat

    Every sound is a position. Get the position right, and the sound comes right by itself.

    Exercise 9

    One word in this sentence is spelled wrongly — tap it.

    I go to iskool every morning.

    Answer

    Wrong word: iskool.

    Corrected: I go to school every morning.

    'school' — straight from s. The extra 'i' sound in front is a Hindi habit, not English.

    Exercise 10

    The TH drill — slowly, with the tongue between the teeth.

    I think these three things are true

    Answer

    Say: I think these three things are true

    think and three have only air; these has a buzz. Both kinds of TH in one sentence.

    Exercise 11

    The V and F drill. Your teeth should be on the lower lip.

    Very few of my friends have a van

    Answer

    Say: Very few of my friends have a van

    very, few, friends, have, van — the same teeth-and-lip position five times.

    Exercise 12

    Start with 's' — don't add 'i'.

    The school bus stops at the station

    Answer

    Say: The school bus stops at the station

    school, stops, station — all straight from s. Practise by stretching 'sss' first.

    Exercise 13

    The /z/ drill — a buzz in the throat.

    The zoo was closed because of the rain

    Answer

    Say: The zoo was closed because of the rain

    zoo, was, closed, because — /z/ in all four. Keep a finger on your throat; it should vibrate.

    Exercise 14

    The schwa drill — leave the unstressed parts relaxed.

    The doctor asked about my computer

    Answer

    Say: The doctor asked about my computer

    DOK-tuh, uh-BOWT, kuhm-PYOO-tuh. Don't try to say every letter in full.

    What next?

    This chapter is a foundation — don't try to "finish" it in one go. Come back twice a week and repeat the sounds in the tables. The next step: the Fix your pronunciation chapter, where you'll find separate drills and minimal pairs for each sound.
