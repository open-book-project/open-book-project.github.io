    About this book¶

    ¡Bienvenidos!

    This is a first course in Spanish for English speakers. It starts from nothing, not even the alphabet, and takes you in sixteen lessons to the point where you can introduce yourself, talk about your family, your town and your day, order a meal, ask the way, go shopping, make plans and tell a friend what you did on holiday. In the terms of the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, it covers level A1 completely and the first part of A2. Spanish is spoken as a first language by several hundred million people in more than twenty countries, and it is one of the most rewarding languages an English speaker can learn: much of its vocabulary is related to English, its spelling is regular, and its grammar, though different from English, is logical and learnable.

    Who this book is for¶

    The book is written for adult and teenage learners whose first or strongest language is English and who have studied little or no Spanish. You may be learning for travel, for work, for a first university course, to talk with family or friends, or simply for pleasure. No knowledge of grammar is assumed: every term is explained when it first appears, and the Glossary at the end collects them all. If you studied Spanish at school long ago, the first lessons will come back quickly, and the later ones will give you the structures most people never got to.

    You can work through the book alone, with a teacher, or with a partner. Everything you need is on the page: dialogues you can hear, explanations, practice, and answers.

    How the book is organised¶

    The sixteen lessons are grouped in four parts.

    Part Lessons What you learn
    I · First steps 1 to 4 Sounds, spelling and stress; greetings and introductions; numbers, dates and time; nouns, gender and articles
    II · The present 5 to 8 Ser and estar; regular verbs in the present; family and describing people; food and the café
    III · Everyday life 9 to 12 The city and directions; the key irregular verbs; daily routine and reflexive verbs; likes with gustar
    IV · Plans and memories 13 to 16 Shopping and clothes; travel plans with ir a; the preterite; a final review

    At the end of the book are the Verb Tables, with every verb pattern of the course; a Glossary of grammar terms and troublesome words; and a short list of Further Reading.

    How to use a lesson¶

    Every lesson has the same nine parts, so you always know where you are.

    1. Learning outcomes. What you will be able to do at the end. Read them first, and come back to them at the end.
    2. Dialogue. A short scene in Spanish, with an English translation, that uses the language of the lesson. Listen to it, read it, and read it aloud. Several characters appear again and again: Daniel, an English student on a course in Salamanca; Lucía, his Mexican classmate; their teacher, Señora Ruiz; and their friends.
    3. Vocabulary. The new words and phrases, in tables, with a sound guide for each.
    4. Grammar. Usually two sections, each explaining one point step by step, from the idea to the forms, with examples and a worked example.
    5. Practice. Tables to fill in from memory, sentences to say aloud, and a speaking task.
    6. Culture. A note on life and language in Spain and Latin America.
    7. Summary. The essentials of the lesson in a few lines. Reread it before you start the next lesson.
    8. Exercises. Questions with answers folded underneath. Try each one before you open the answer.
    9. Quiz. A set of questions of different kinds to check what you have learnt.

    Aim to spend several sessions on each lesson rather than one long one. Short, regular practice works far better than long, occasional sessions: twenty minutes a day will take you further than three hours on a Sunday. Say everything aloud, even when you are alone. Go back to earlier lessons often; each review makes the language more automatic.

    Learn phrases, not only words

    Learn whole phrases such as ¿Me pone un café, por favor? or Me gustaría ir as they are, before you understand every part of them. The grammar sections explain the parts later, and by then you will already be using them.

    Hearing and practising¶

    This book is meant to be heard as well as read. Every Spanish word, phrase and sentence has a listen button beside it: press it to hear the phrase, and press again to hear it slowly. A dialogue or a list of phrases also has a Play all button that reads it from start to finish.

    • Sound guides. New words have an approximate pronunciation in square brackets, using English spelling, with the stressed syllable in capitals: gracias [GRAH-syahs]. Lesson 1 explains how to read them. They help, but the recordings are the real model.
    • Practice tables. A table with a Practice switch hides its key forms and turns them into boxes for you to fill in; Check marks your answers.
    • Typed answers. Many exercises have a box in which to type your answer and check it before you open the model answer. Accents are checked too, but an answer typed without them is accepted with a reminder.
    • Quizzes. Each lesson ends with a quiz: multiple choice, gaps to fill, matching, words to put in order, a wrong word to find, sentences to listen to and write down, and phrases to say aloud. Your answers and best score are kept in your browser, so you can come back to a quiz later.
    • Speaking tasks. Boxes titled "Say it" ask you to speak. Where your browser can recognise speech, a microphone button lets you check your pronunciation.

    Which Spanish?¶

    Spanish is one language with many accents, like English. This book teaches a neutral Spanish that is understood everywhere, and points out the main differences between Spain and Latin America as they come up: in pronunciation (Lesson 1), in the words for "you" (Lesson 2), in everyday vocabulary (in the culture notes of every lesson), and in the use of the past tenses (Lesson 15).

    Two choices are worth knowing from the start:

    • Vosotros. Spain has a plural informal "you", vosotros, with its own verb forms. Latin America uses ustedes instead. The book shows the vosotros forms in every table so that you can understand Spaniards, but if your Spanish is for Latin America you may simply learn to recognise them.
    • The sound of z and c. Most speakers in Spain say gracias and cinco with a th sound; Latin Americans say them with an s. Both are correct. The sound guides show the Latin American [s] and mention the Spanish [th] where it helps.

    Whichever variety you choose, keep to it, and enjoy hearing the others. ¡Vamos a empezar! Let's begin.
