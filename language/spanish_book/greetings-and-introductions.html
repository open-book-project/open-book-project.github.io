    Lesson 2: Greetings and Introductions¶

    Saludos y presentaciones

    The first Spanish most people ever use is a greeting, and it is worth getting right: a well-placed buenos días or mucho gusto opens doors in every one of the twenty-odd countries where Spanish is spoken. In this lesson you learn to say hello and goodbye at any time of day, to ask how someone is, to give your name and where you are from, and to choose between the two words for "you" that Spanish keeps apart: the friendly tú and the polite usted.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

    • greet people and say goodbye at any time of day, formally and informally;
    • ask how someone is and answer;
    • say your name, ask someone's name and react when you meet them;
    • say where you are from and give your nationality, in the masculine and feminine;
    • use the subject pronouns, and choose between tú, usted, vosotros and ustedes.

    masculinefeminine

    2.1 Dialogue: on the first day of class¶

    It is the first morning of a Spanish course in Salamanca. Daniel, a student from Manchester, sits down next to Lucía, who is from Mexico. Then the teacher, Señora Ruiz, arrives. Listen to each line, then read it aloud yourself.

    • Daniel: ¡Hola! Buenos días. — Hi! Good morning.
    • Lucía: ¡Hola! ¿Qué tal? — Hi! How's it going?
    • Daniel: Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? — Fine, thanks. And you?
    • Lucía: Muy bien. Me llamo Lucía. ¿Cómo te llamas? — Very well. My name is Lucía. What's your name?
    • Daniel: Me llamo Daniel. Encantado. — My name is Daniel. Pleased to meet you.
    • Lucía: Encantada. ¿De dónde eres, Daniel? — Pleased to meet you. Where are you from, Daniel?
    • Daniel: Soy inglés, de Mánchester. ¿Y tú? — I'm English, from Manchester. And you?
    • Lucía: Yo soy mexicana, de Guadalajara. — I'm Mexican, from Guadalajara.
    • Señora Ruiz: Buenos días a todos. Soy la profesora Ruiz. — Good morning, everyone. I'm Señora Ruiz, the teacher.
    • Daniel: Buenos días. ¿Cómo está usted? — Good morning. How are you?
    • Señora Ruiz: Muy bien, gracias. ¿Y usted? ¿Cómo se llama? — Very well, thank you. And you? What is your name?
    • Daniel: Me llamo Daniel Brown. Mucho gusto. — My name is Daniel Brown. Nice to meet you.
    • Señora Ruiz: Mucho gusto, Daniel. Bienvenido a Salamanca. — Nice to meet you, Daniel. Welcome to Salamanca.

    Notice three things before we explain them:

    1. Daniel says ¿Y tú? to Lucía, a fellow student, but ¿Y usted? to the teacher. Spanish has a friendly "you" and a polite "you".
    2. Daniel says encantado, Lucía says encantada. The word changes with the speaker: -o for a man, -a for a woman. The same happens with mexicano and mexicana.
    3. Questions begin with an upside-down question mark ¿ and exclamations with ¡. They are part of the spelling, not decoration: they tell you, before you start reading aloud, that the sentence will rise or be exclaimed.

    2.2 Vocabulary: hello, goodbye and how are you¶

    Saying hello¶

    Spanish Sounds like English When
    hola [OH-lah] hello, hi any time; friendly but never rude
    buenos días [BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs] good morning until lunch (in Spain, lunch is about 2 p.m.)
    buenas tardes [BWEH-nahs TAR-dehs] good afternoon, good evening from lunch until it gets dark
    buenas noches [BWEH-nahs NOH-chehs] good evening, good night after dark; also when you go to bed
    buenas [BWEH-nahs] hi (short for all three) very common in shops and bars
    bienvenido / bienvenida [byehn-beh-NEE-doh / -dah] welcome to a man / to a woman

    Table 2.1. Greetings. The h of hola is silent.

    Asking how someone is¶

    Spanish Sounds like English
    ¿Qué tal? [keh TAHL] How's it going? How are things?
    ¿Cómo estás? [KOH-moh ehs-TAHS] How are you? (to a friend: tú)
    ¿Cómo está usted? [KOH-moh ehs-TAH oos-TEHD] How are you? (polite: usted)
    ¿Y tú? / ¿Y usted? [ee TOO / ee oos-TEHD] And you?
    muy bien [mwee BYEHN] very well
    bien, gracias [byehn GRAH-syahs] fine, thanks
    regular [rreh-goo-LAR] so-so
    más o menos [MAHS oh MEH-nohs] more or less, so-so
    mal [MAHL] bad, not well
    cansado / cansada [kahn-SAH-doh / -dah] tired (man / woman)

    Table 2.2. How are you? The th sound of Spain is explained in Lesson 1: in Spain gracias sounds like [GRAH-thyahs].

    Saying goodbye¶

    Spanish Sounds like English
    adiós [ah-DYOHS] goodbye
    hasta luego [AHS-tah LWEH-goh] see you later (the everyday goodbye)
    hasta mañana [AHS-tah mah-NYAH-nah] see you tomorrow
    hasta pronto [AHS-tah PROHN-toh] see you soon
    nos vemos [nohs BEH-mohs] see you
    chao [CHAH-oh] bye (informal; written chau in Argentina and Uruguay)
    que tengas un buen día [keh TEHN-gahs oon bwehn DEE-ah] have a good day

    Table 2.3. Goodbyes.

    Meeting people¶

    Spanish English
    ¿Cómo te llamas? What's your name? (tú)
    ¿Cómo se llama usted? What's your name? (usted)
    Me llamo Ana. My name is Ana. (literally: I call myself Ana)
    Soy Ana. I'm Ana.
    Mucho gusto. Nice to meet you. (the same for everyone)
    Encantado. / Encantada. Delighted. (said by a man / by a woman)
    Igualmente. Likewise.
    ¿De dónde eres? Where are you from? (tú)
    ¿De dónde es usted? Where are you from? (usted)
    Soy de Londres. I'm from London.
    Te presento a mi amigo Pablo. Let me introduce my friend Pablo.
    Por favor. / Gracias. / De nada. Please. / Thank you. / You're welcome.
    Perdón. / Lo siento. Excuse me, sorry. / I'm sorry.

    Table 2.4. Phrases for meeting people.

    Hola is never rude

    In English "hi" can sound too casual for a bank manager. In Spanish hola is fine with anyone. To be polite, add the time-of-day greeting: Hola, buenos días. That combination is what you will hear most at a shop counter.

    2.3 Grammar: the subject pronouns, and four ways to say "you"¶

    The subject pronouns are the words for I, you, he, she, we, they. Here they are, with the "you" forms highlighted.

    Person Spanish English
    1st singular yo I
    2nd singular, informal tú you (one friend, a child, a relative)
    2nd singular, formal usted you (one person, politely)
    3rd singular él / ella he / she
    1st plural nosotros / nosotras we (any group / an all-female group)
    2nd plural, informal vosotros / vosotras you all (friends; Spain only)
    2nd plural ustedes you all (formal in Spain; formal and informal in Latin America)
    3rd plural ellos / ellas they (any group / an all-female group)

    Table 2.5. The subject pronouns.

    Four details matter:

    • Accents that change meaning. tú with an accent is "you"; tu without is "your". él with an accent is "he"; el without is "the". The written accent makes no difference to the sound here, only to the meaning.
    • Masculine wins in mixed groups. nosotros, vosotros and ellos are used for any group with at least one male in it. The forms in -as are only for groups made up entirely of women.
    • Usted is grammatically "he/she". Historically usted comes from vuestra merced, "your grace", so it takes the same verb form as él and ella: ¿Cómo está usted? has the same verb as ¿Cómo está él? In writing it is often shortened to Ud. or Vd., and ustedes to Uds. or Vds.
    • Pronouns are usually left out. Spanish verb endings already show who is speaking, so Soy inglés means "I'm English" without yo. You add the pronoun for emphasis or contrast, as Lucía does in the dialogue: Yo soy mexicana, "I'm Mexican (and you're English)".

    Tú or usted?¶

    Informal Formal
    one person tú (vos in some countries) usted
    several people, Spain vosotros, vosotras ustedes
    several people, Latin America ustedes ustedes

    Table 2.6. The four words for "you", and where they are used.

    Use tú with friends, family, children, other students and people your own age in a relaxed setting. Use usted with older people you do not know, officials, and in formal situations: a job interview, a bank, a doctor's surgery. When a Spanish speaker says puedes tutearme ("you can call me tú") or simply starts using tú with you, you may switch.

    Common mistake: mixing the two

    Once you have chosen, keep the same "you" for the whole conversation. ¿Cómo está usted? ¿Y de dónde eres? mixes polite and friendly forms and sounds odd. Say either ¿Cómo está usted? ¿De dónde es? or ¿Cómo estás? ¿De dónde eres?

    Vos

    In Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and much of Central America, people say vos instead of tú, with verb forms of its own: vos sos ("you are") instead of tú eres, ¿Cómo te llamás? instead of ¿Cómo te llamas? You do not need to use vos yourself, and everybody understands tú, but you should recognise it when you hear it. This book teaches tú and notes vos where it matters.

    2.4 Grammar: giving your name and where you are from¶

    Me llamo …¶

    The everyday way to give your name is me llamo, literally "I call myself". The verb is llamarse, "to be called". Its first three forms are all you need at this stage:

    Person llamarse English
    yo me llamo my name is, I'm called
    tú te llamas your name is
    él, ella, usted se llama his / her / your name is
    nosotros, nosotras nos llamamos our names are
    vosotros, vosotras os llamáis your names are (Spain)
    ellos, ellas, ustedes se llaman their / your names are

    Table 2.7. The verb llamarse. Switch on Practice and fill in the endings.

    The small word in front (me, te, se) is a reflexive pronoun, "myself, yourself, himself". You will meet many verbs like this in Lesson 11; for now, learn the phrases whole.

    • ¿Cómo te llamas? — Me llamo Sofía. — What's your name? — I'm Sofía.
    • ¿Cómo se llama usted? — Me llamo Antonio García. — What's your name, sir? — Antonio García.
    • Mi amiga se llama Carmen. — My friend's name is Carmen.
    • Mis padres se llaman Luis y Elena. — My parents are called Luis and Elena.

    Two other ways are also correct: Soy Sofía ("I'm Sofía"), which is short and friendly, and Mi nombre es Sofía ("My name is Sofía"), which is fine but more formal and more common in writing.

    Common mistake

    Do not translate "my name is" word for word into me llamo es. Either me llamo Sofía or mi nombre es Sofía, never both verbs at once. And "what is your name?" is ¿Cómo te llamas? (literally "how do you call yourself?"), not ¿Qué te llamas?

    Soy de … : where you are from¶

    To say where you come from, use soy de + the place. The verb is ser, "to be", which Lesson 5 explains fully. Its first three forms are:

    Person ser Example
    yo soy Soy de Toronto. — I'm from Toronto.
    tú eres ¿De dónde eres? — Where are you from?
    él, ella, usted es Marta es de Bogotá. — Marta is from Bogotá.

    Table 2.8. Three forms of ser.

    The question ¿De dónde eres? is literally "From where are you?" Spanish never ends a sentence with a preposition, so the de moves to the front.

    Nationalities: masculine and feminine¶

    A nationality is an adjective, and Spanish adjectives change their ending to match the person (Lesson 7 has the full rules). There are three patterns:

    Country Masculine Feminine English
    México mexicano mexicana Mexican
    Colombia colombiano colombiana Colombian
    Argentina argentino argentina Argentinian
    Australia australiano australiana Australian
    la India indio india Indian
    España español española Spanish
    Inglaterra inglés inglesa English
    Irlanda irlandés irlandesa Irish
    Escocia escocés escocesa Scottish
    Alemania alemán alemana German
    Estados Unidos estadounidense estadounidense American (of the USA)
    Canadá canadiense canadiense Canadian

    Table 2.9. Nationalities. Practice hides the endings in bold.

    The patterns:

    1. -o becomes -a: mexicano → mexicana.
    2. A consonant adds -a: español → española. If the masculine has a written accent on its last syllable, the accent disappears in the feminine, because the stress no longer needs marking: inglés → inglesa, alemán → alemana. (Lesson 1 explains why.)
    3. -e and most other endings do not change: canadiense, estadounidense.

    No capital letters

    In Spanish, nationalities and languages are written with a small letter: Soy inglés. Hablo español. Only the names of countries and cities take a capital: Inglaterra, Madrid.

    Worked example: introducing a friend

    Daniel introduces his friend Priya, an Indian woman who lives in Leeds, to Lucía.

    1. Introduce: Lucía, te presento a mi amiga Priya.
    2. Her name: Se llama Priya. (third person: se llama)
    3. Nationality, feminine: Es india. (indio → india)
    4. Where she lives now: Vive en Leeds. ("she lives in Leeds")

    Lucía answers Encantada, Priya, and Priya answers Igualmente.

    2.5 Practice¶

    Formal or informal?¶

    For each situation, decide whether you would use tú or usted, then say the greeting aloud.

    Situation You say
    a classmate of your age Hola, ¿qué tal? ¿Cómo estás?
    an elderly neighbour Buenos días, señora. ¿Cómo está usted?
    a hotel receptionist, at night Buenas noches. ¿Cómo está?
    your host family's ten-year-old ¡Hola! ¿Cómo te llamas?
    two friends together, in Madrid ¡Hola, chicos! ¿Qué tal estáis?
    two friends together, in Lima ¡Hola, chicos! ¿Qué tal están?

    Table 2.10. Choosing the right "you". Señor, señora and señorita are Mr, Mrs and Miss; señorita is now used mostly for young women and in shops.

    A first conversation¶

    Read each part of this conversation aloud. Then cover the Spanish and say it from the English.

    • Buenas tardes. ¿Cómo se llama usted? — Good afternoon. What is your name?
    • Me llamo Elena Torres. ¿Y usted? — My name is Elena Torres. And yours?
    • Me llamo Robert Kim. Soy canadiense, de Vancouver. — My name is Robert Kim. I'm Canadian, from Vancouver.
    • Mucho gusto, señor Kim. — Nice to meet you, Mr Kim.
    • Igualmente. Hasta luego. — Likewise. See you later.

    Say it

    Introduce yourself in three sentences: a greeting, your name, and where you are from. For example:

    Hola, buenos días. Me llamo Sarah. Soy inglesa, de Bristol.

    Then ask your partner the same things, once with tú and once with usted.

    2.6 Culture: greetings in Spain and Latin America¶

    Kisses and handshakes. In Spain, friends and people being introduced in a relaxed setting usually greet with dos besos, two light kisses, cheek to cheek, starting with the right cheek (so you lean to your left). This happens between women and between a man and a woman; two men usually shake hands or, if they are close, hug. In most of Latin America the custom is a single kiss on the right cheek. In business settings everywhere, a handshake is the safe choice. Do not worry about getting it perfect: follow the other person's lead.

    Time of day. Spanish days are divided by meals, not by the clock. In Spain lunch is late (often between 2 and 3 p.m.), so buenos días is used until then, and buenas tardes can last until nine in the evening in summer, when it is still light. In much of Latin America the change to buenas tardes comes at noon.

    Tú and usted around the world. Spain uses tú very widely, even with strangers of similar age, shop assistants and, increasingly, teachers. In much of Latin America usted is used more, and in parts of Colombia, Costa Rica and Central America some people use usted even with friends and family. When in doubt, start with usted: no one is offended by politeness, and the other person will tell you if you can switch.

    Vosotros and ustedes. Only Spain uses vosotros for "you all" among friends. In the Canary Islands and all of Latin America, ustedes is the only plural "you". This book shows the vosotros forms so that you can understand Spaniards, but if you are learning for Latin America you may skip them in your own speech.

    Names. Most Spanish speakers have two surnames: the father's first surname followed by the mother's first surname. In Gabriel García Márquez, García comes from his father and Márquez from his mother; he is "García" for short, not "Márquez". Women do not traditionally change their surnames when they marry.

    Señor, señora, don, doña. Señor and señora go with surnames (señora Ruiz). Don and doña go with first names and show respect, often to older people: don Antonio, doña Carmen.

    2.7 Summary¶

    Summary

    • Greetings: hola (any time), buenos días (morning), buenas tardes (afternoon, early evening), buenas noches (night). Goodbyes: adiós, hasta luego, hasta mañana, nos vemos.
    • How are you? ¿Qué tal? ¿Cómo estás? (tú) ¿Cómo está usted? (usted). Answer muy bien, bien, regular, mal.
    • Names: ¿Cómo te llamas? — Me llamo … React with mucho gusto or encantado / encantada.
    • Origin: ¿De dónde eres? — Soy de … Nationalities agree with the person (inglés, inglesa) and take no capital letter.
    • Pronouns: yo, tú, usted, él, ella, nosotros, vosotros, ustedes, ellos, ellas. Usted takes the same verb form as él and ella. Pronouns are usually left out.
    • "You": tú (friendly), usted (polite); for several people vosotros (friendly, Spain only) and ustedes (everywhere else, and formal in Spain).

    2.8 Exercises¶

    Exercise 1

    It is 10 a.m. You walk into a bakery. What do you say to greet the baker? Give the two words.

    Answer

    Buenos días. Buenos días is used until lunchtime. Hola, buenos días is also very natural.

    Exercise 2

    Give the Spanish for "See you tomorrow".

    Answer

    Hasta mañana. Hasta mañana.

    Exercise 3

    A friend asks ¿Qué tal?. Answer "Very well, thanks. And you?"

    Answer

    Muy bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? Muy bien, gracias. ¿Y tú? Since the question was informal, you answer with tú.

    Exercise 4

    Ask an older man you have just met, politely, what his name is.

    Answer

    ¿Cómo se llama usted? ¿Cómo se llama usted? With usted the verb is the third person form, se llama.

    Exercise 5

    Give the feminine form of escocés.

    Answer

    escocesa. escocesa. A consonant adds -a, and the written accent disappears because the stress is now on the next-to-last syllable, where Spanish expects it.

    Exercise 6

    Which subject pronoun would you use for each? (a) your two brothers, talking about them; (b) your sister and her friend Ana; (c) yourself and your friend Marta (both women); (d) a group of friends you are speaking to, in Seville; (e) the same, in Buenos Aires.

    Answer

    (a) ellos; (b) ellas; (c) nosotras; (d) vosotros; (e) ustedes.

    Exercise 7

    Correct the mistake: Me llamo es Tom. Soy Inglés.

    Answer

    Me llamo Tom. Soy inglés. Do not add es after me llamo, and write nationalities with a small letter.

    Exercise 8

    Write a short introduction for Kenji, a Japanese man from Osaka. Use the third person. (Japanese: japonés.)

    Answer

    Se llama Kenji. Es japonés, de Osaka. For a woman it would be Es japonesa.
