    Chapter 3: The sounds of French¶

    Les sons du français

    French has about 36 sounds. Most of them you already make in English or Hindi. A handful are new — the u of tu, the nasal vowels of un bon vin blanc, the R made in the throat. This chapter shows you where to put your lips and tongue for each one, and gives you pairs of words to train your ear. Use your voice on every 🔊.

    masculinefeminineplural

    The golden rule of French sound

    Tense lips, pure vowels, even syllables, stress at the end. French vowels never slide (“no” is not “noh-oo”), and every syllable gets the same short beat.

    3.1 Four big differences from English¶

    English habit French habit
    Relaxed mouth Tense, precise mouth — lips spread or rounded much more.
    Vowels glide: “day” = [de-ee], “go” = [go-oo] Pure vowels: des [day] stops dead; beau [bo] does not move.
    Stress-timed: long and short syllables Syllable-timed: every syllable gets about the same beat.
    Stress on one syllable of each word Stress on the last syllable of a group of words.
    • beau, des, si — beautiful, some, if — hold each vowel still, like a photo [BO, DAY, SEE]
    • l’université — university — five even beats, the last a little longer [lew-nee-vehr-see-TAY]

    Hint French spelling Example

    3.2 The oral vowels: a map of the mouth¶

    Vowels differ in two ways: how high the tongue is (mouth closed or open) and where it is (front or back), plus whether the lips are rounded. French uses all the combinations — including front + rounded, which English does not have.

    Sound Spellings Examples
    [ee] i, î, y ici, la vie, le stylo
    [ay] é, -er, -ez, -et l’été, parler, le nez
    [eh] è, ê, ai, ei, e + 2 consonants très, la fête, le lait, belle
    [ah] a, à le chat, la table, ami
    [oh] closed o (end), ô, au, eau le mot, l’hôtel, chaud, l’eau
    [o] open o + pronounced consonant le bol, la porte, l’homme
    [oo] ou, où, oû rouge, le jour, le goût
    [ew] u, û la rue, la lune, sûr
    [ö] closed eu (end of word) deux, peu, bleu
    [ö] open eu, œu + consonant la peur, la fleur, jeune
    [uh] e (no accent) le, de, petit, demain

    3.3 The key pair: u versus ou¶

    This is the most important sound difference for a beginner, because it changes meaning all the time. English and Hindi have ou [oo] (as in “food”, “phool”) but not French u.

    The u trick

    Say a long “eeeee”, freeze your tongue, and slowly round your lips. The sound that comes out is French u. Check in a mirror: lips round, tongue tip still touching your lower teeth.

    Minimal pairs: u / ou¶

    Each pair differs by one sound only. Listen to the pair, then say it.

    • tu / tout — you / all [TEW / TOO]
    • vu / vous — seen / you [VEW / VOO]
    • la rue / la roue — the street / the wheel [la REW / la ROO]
    • dessus / dessous — on top / underneath [duh-SEW / duh-SOO]
    • pur / pour — pure / for [PEWR / POOR]
    • du / doux — some / soft [DEW / DOO]
    • lu / le loup — read (past) / the wolf [LEW / luh LOO]

    Minimal pairs: i / u¶

    • la vie / la vue — life / the view [la VEE / la VEW]
    • le riz / la rue — rice / the street [luh REE / la REW]
    • dit / du — said / some [DEE / DEW]

    In sentences¶

    • Tu es où ? — Where are you? (informal) [tew eh OO]
    • Vous avez vu la rue ? — Have you seen the street? [voo-za-vay VEW la REW]
    • Le livre est dessus, pas dessous. — The book is on top, not underneath.

    The glides: oui, huit, lui¶

    When ou or u comes right before another vowel, it becomes a quick glide. oui starts with a [w]; huit and lui start with the same glide made with u-lips.

    • oui / huit — yes / eight [WEE / WEET (u-lips)]
    • Louis / lui — Louis (name) / him [loo-EE / lew-EE, very fast]
    • Je suis là cette nuit. — I am here tonight. [zhuh swee la set NWEE]

    Careful

    • Wrong: Tu said as “too”.
    • Right: Tu [tew]: ee-tongue, rounded lips.
    • Why: “too” is tout (all). Saying “too” for tu changes the word.

    3.4 The e family: é, è, silent e — and eu¶

    é [ay] and è [eh]¶

    é is a smiling, closed [ay] with no glide at the end. è is more open, like “e” in “bed”. In everyday speech some French speakers blur them at the end of words — but keep them apart and you will always be understood.

    • les / le lait — the (plural) / the milk [LAY / luh LEH]
    • l’été / il était — summer / he was [lay-TAY / ee-lay-TEH]
    • le pré / près — the meadow / near [luh PRAY / PREH]

    Careful

    • Wrong: café said as “ka-FAY-ee”.
    • Right: café [ka-FAY] — the vowel stops dead.
    • Why: English “ay” slides into “ee”. French é is one frozen sound — hold your jaw still.

    The silent e (e muet) [uh]¶

    An e without an accent is a short, relaxed [uh] with slightly rounded lips — or it disappears completely (you will see when in Silent letters & liaison). The tiny difference between [uh] and [ay] separates singular from plural:

    • le livre / les livres — the book / the books — the final s is silent; only the vowel of le/les tells you [luh LEEVR / lay LEEVR]
    • de / des — of / some (plural) [duh / DAY]
    • ce / ces — this / these [suh / SAY]
    • je / j’ai — I / I have [zhuh / ZHAY]

    le [uh] vs les [ay]

    Plural -s is silent, so the article carries the number: le [luh] = one, les [lay] = many. Get this vowel right and people hear your plurals.

    eu and œu: closed and open [ö]¶

    Make an [ay] and round your lips: that is closed eu (deux). Make an [eh] and round your lips: that is open eu (peur). Rule of thumb: closed at the end of a word, open before a pronounced consonant.

    • deux, peu, bleu, le jeu — two, little, blue, the game — closed [DÖ, PÖ, BLÖ, luh ZHÖ]
    • la peur, la sœur, la fleur, neuf — fear, sister, flower, nine — open [PÖR, SÖR, FLÖR, NÖF]
    • de / deux — of / two — deux is tenser and more rounded [duh / DÖ]
    • Il est neuf heures, je suis heureux. — It's nine o'clock, I am happy. [ee-leh nö-VÖR, zhuh swee-zö-RÖ]

    3.5 The four nasal vowels¶

    In a nasal vowel, air goes out through the mouth and the nose at the same time. The n or m in the spelling is not pronounced — it only tells you the vowel is nasal.

    Sound Spellings Examples
    [ã] an, am, en, em l’enfant, la chambre, le temps
    [õ] on, om bon, le nom, l’ombre
    [ɛ̃] in, im, ain, aim, ein, yn le vin, simple, le pain, plein
    [ɛ̃] or [œ̃] un, um un, lundi, le parfum

    Most speakers in France now say un exactly like in. Many in Belgium, Switzerland and the south keep a rounder [œ̃]. Both are correct.

    Hindi already has these

    हाँ (haan) ≈ French [ã]; the end of क्यों (kyon) ≈ [õ]; मैं (main) ≈ [ɛ̃] — it sounds almost exactly like French main (hand)!

    When is a vowel + n/m nasal?¶

    The nasal rule

    Vowel + n/m + consonant or end of word = nasal (bon, bonbon). Vowel + n/m + vowel, or a double nn/mm = not nasal (une, fine, bonne, l’homme).

    This rule is why masculine and feminine often sound different:

    • bon / bonne — good (m) / good (f) [BÕ / BON]
    • un / une — a (m) / a (f) [Ɛ̃ / EWN]
    • indien / indienne — Indian (m) / Indian (f) [ɛ̃-DYƐ̃ / ɛ̃-DYEN]
    • plein / pleine — full (m) / full (f) [PLƐ̃ / PLEN]
    • un an / un âne — a year / a donkey [ɛ̃-NÃ / ɛ̃-NAN]

    The three-way test: ã, õ, ɛ̃¶

    [ã] is the most open (jaw low, like “ah” through the nose); [õ] has rounded lips; [ɛ̃] is flatter, like “eh” through the nose.

    • le vent / ils vont / le vin — the wind / they go / the wine [VÃ / VÕ / VƐ̃]
    • sans / son / saint — without / his, sound / saint [SÃ / SÕ / SƐ̃]
    • le banc / bon / le bain — the bench / good / the bath [BÃ / BÕ / BƐ̃]
    • lent / long / le lin — slow / long / linen [LÃ / LÕ / LƐ̃]
    • blanc / blond — white / blond [BLÃ / BLÕ]
    • Un bon vin blanc. — A good white wine. — the famous four-nasal test [ɛ̃ bõ vɛ̃ BLÃ]
    • En France, on mange du pain. — In France, people eat bread. [ã FRÃSS, õ mãzh dew PƐ̃]

    Careful

    • Wrong: bon said as “bong” or “bonn”.
    • Right: bon [bõ]: the vowel goes nasal, the tongue never touches the roof of the mouth.
    • Why: An n or ng at the end turns bon into bonne — or into nothing French at all.

    3.6 The consonants that need attention¶

    The French R¶

    The French R is made at the back of the mouth, where you make [g] — not with the tongue tip. The tip stays down, behind your lower teeth.

    Step What to do
    1 Say “ga, ga, ga”. Feel the back of your tongue touch the soft palate.
    2 Now say “ga” but do not let it touch: leave a tiny gap so the air rubs through — a soft, gentle gargle.
    3 That rubbing sound is the French R. It is very close to Urdu/Hindi ग़ in ghazal, ghareeb.
    4 Keep the tongue tip pressed behind the lower teeth for every R.
    • la rue, rouge, rare — the street, red, rare — R at the start [la REW, ROOZH, RAR]
    • Paris, merci, Marie — R in the middle [pa-REE, mehr-SEE, ma-REE]
    • bonjour, la mer, la porte — hello, the sea, the door — R at the end: always pronounce it, softly
    • très, trois, un croissant — very, three, a croissant — after t or k the R is lighter, almost a whisper [TREH, TRWA, ɛ̃ krwa-SÃ]
    • Il fait très froid à Rouen. — It is very cold in Rouen. [eel feh treh FRWA a roo-Ã]

    Careful

    • Wrong: merci with a tapped Hindi र or a curled English r.
    • Right: merci with a soft throat R, tongue tip down.
    • Why: A rolled r is understood (some singers and older rural speakers use it), but the standard French R is in the throat.

    ch, j and g¶

    • Charles cherche son chat. — Charles is looking for his cat. — ch = [sh], always [SHARL SHEHRSH sõ SHA]
    • le chocolat, la Chine, dimanche — chocolate, China, Sunday [sho-ko-LA, la SHEEN, dee-MÃSH]
    • Je joue à la plage. — I play on the beach. — j and soft g = [zh], the sound in “measure” [zhuh ZHOO a la PLAZH]
    • le garage, rouge, la page — garage, red, the page [ga-RAZH, ROOZH, la PAZH]

    Careful

    • Wrong: chocolat as “tchocolat” (like Hindi च in chai).
    • Right: chocolat [sho-ko-LA], with श.
    • Why: French ch is श (sh). The “tch” sound only appears in borrowed words, spelled tch: tchèque, le Tchad.

    Careful

    • Wrong: je as “juh” with ज (a d at the start).
    • Right: je [zhuh]: a smooth buzz with no d.
    • Why: French j is the middle sound of “measure” or “television”, never the j of “jam” or jaana.

    gn [ny]¶

    • la montagne, l’Espagne, la campagne — mountain, Spain, countryside — gn = “ny” in “canyon” [mõ-TANY, les-PANY, kã-PANY]
    • gagner, le champagne — to win, champagne [ga-NYAY, shã-PANY]

    Careful

    • Wrong: montagne as “mon-tag-nuh”.
    • Right: montagne [mõ-TANY] — one sound, no g.
    • Why: gn is a single sound, made with the middle of the tongue against the palate.

    p, t, k, d, l and s¶

    French p, t, k have no puff of air — exactly like Hindi प, त, क (not फ, थ, ख). French t, d touch the teeth, like Hindi त, द. French l is always clear, like Hindi ल, even at the end of a word — never the “dark” l of English “ball”.

    • Papa, tu es là ? Le taxi est là. — Dad, are you there? The taxi is here. — no puffs of air
    • Elle est belle, la ville. — The town is beautiful. — a clear l every time, even at the end [el eh BEL la VEEL]

    A single s between two vowels sounds [z]; ss stays [s]. This changes meaning:

    • le poisson / le poison — the fish / the poison [pwa-SÕ / pwa-ZÕ]
    • le dessert / le désert — dessert / the desert [day-SEHR / day-ZEHR]
    • Le poisson n’est pas du poison ! — Fish is not poison!

    3.7 The letter h: muet and aspiré¶

    In French, h is never pronounced. l’hôtel = [lo-TEL], le héros = [luh ay-RO]. But there are two kinds of h, and they behave differently with the word before them:

    Type Behaves like Examples
    h muet (most words) a vowel: l’, liaison l’homme, l’hôtel, les‿hôtels
    h aspiré (a list to learn) a consonant: no l’, no liaison le héros, les haricots, la honte

    Dictionaries mark h aspiré with an asterisk or an apostrophe (*héros). The rules for linking words are in the next chapter, Silent letters & liaison.

    Careful

    • Wrong: l’hôtel as “la HO-tel”, with an English h.
    • Right: l’hôtel [lo-TEL].
    • Why: The h is silent, and the stress goes on the last syllable.

    3.8 Rhythm, stress and melody¶

    French sounds “smooth” because every syllable is about the same length and words run into each other. The only stress is a slight lengthening of the last syllable of each group of words — not of each word.

    • Bonjour. / Bonjour, Madame. — The stress moves to the end of the group: bon-JOUR / bonjour ma-DAME.
    • Je voudrais | un café | avec du lait. — I'd like a coffee with milk. — three groups, three small peaks: -DRAIS, -FÉ, LAIT

    Cognates: move the stress to the end¶

    English words borrowed from French stress the start. French stresses the end. Clap the syllables evenly and lengthen the last one.

    English stress French stress
    RES-tau-rant res-tau-RANT [res-to-RÃ]
    CHO-co-late cho-co-LAT [sho-ko-LA]
    CA-na-da Ca-na-DA [ka-na-DA]
    im-POR-tant im-por-TANT [ɛ̃-por-TÃ]
    pho-TO-gra-phy pho-to-gra-PHIE [fo-to-gra-FEE]

    Careful

    • Wrong: restaurant as “RES-tuh-ront”.
    • Right: restaurant [res-to-RÃ]: three even beats, nasal at the end, no t.
    • Why: Do not swallow the middle syllables as English does — French gives each one its full vowel.

    Melody: the voice rises for questions¶

    In everyday French, the same words can be a statement or a question. Only the melody changes: falling at the end for a statement, rising for a yes/no question.

    • Tu viens. ↘ / Tu viens ? ↗ — You're coming. / Are you coming? [tew VYƐ̃]
    • Ça va. ↘ / Ça va ? ↗ — I'm fine. / How are you? [sa VA]
    • Vous parlez anglais ? ↗ — Do you speak English? [voo par-lay ã-GLEH]

    Train your ear daily

    Pick two minimal pairs a day (tu/tout, vent/vin) and say each ten times. For mic drills with scoring, see Pronunciation clinic in Part 4.

    À retenir

    • Tense lips, pure vowels, even syllables, stress at the end. French vowels never slide (“no” is not “noh-oo”), and every syllable gets the same short beat.
    • Say a long “eeeee”, freeze your tongue, and slowly round your lips. The sound that comes out is French u. Check in a mirror: lips round, tongue tip still touching your lower teeth.
    • Plural -s is silent, so the article carries the number: le [luh] = one, les [lay] = many. Get this vowel right and people hear your plurals.
    • हाँ (haan) ≈ French [ã]; the end of क्यों (kyon) ≈ [õ]; मैं (main) ≈ [ɛ̃] — it sounds almost exactly like French main (hand)!
    • Vowel + n/m + consonant or end of word = nasal (bon, bonbon). Vowel + n/m + vowel, or a double nn/mm = not nasal (une, fine, bonne, l’homme).
    • Pick two minimal pairs a day (tu/tout, vent/vin) and say each ten times. For mic drills with scoring, see Pronunciation clinic in Part 4.

    3.9 Practice — The sounds of French¶

    Exercise 1

    Which word has the French u sound [ew]?

    1. tout
    2. vous
    3. tu
    4. sous
    Answer

    tu

    tu = [tew]. The others all have ou = [oo].

    Exercise 2

    How do you make the French u?

    1. Say 'oo' and smile
    2. Say 'ee', then round your lips
    3. Say 'ah' through the nose
    4. Say 'uh' quickly
    Answer

    Say 'ee', then round your lips

    u = the tongue of i + the lips of ou.

    Exercise 3

    'dessous' means 'underneath'. What does 'dessus' mean?

    1. on top
    2. inside
    3. behind
    4. far
    Answer

    on top

    dessus [duh-SEW] = on top; dessous [duh-SOO] = underneath.

    Exercise 4

    Match the nasal words to their meanings.

    AB
    le ventthey go
    ils vontthe wine
    le vinthe wind
    Answer

    le vent — the wind; ils vont — they go; le vin — the wine

    [ã] vent, [õ] vont, [ɛ̃] vin — three different nasal vowels.

    Exercise 5

    Which word does NOT have a nasal vowel?

    1. bon
    2. bonne
    3. vin
    4. un
    Answer

    bonne

    In bonne, nn + vowel: not nasal [bon]. bon, vin and un end in n: nasal.

    Exercise 6

    Un bon vin ___ (white).

    Hint: rhymes with 'an'

    Answer

    blanc

    Un bon vin blanc — all four nasal vowels in one phrase. The c is silent.

    Exercise 7

    French 'ch', as in 'chat', sounds like…

    1. English 'ch' in 'chair'
    2. English 'sh' in 'ship'
    3. English 'k'
    4. English 'j'
    Answer

    English 'sh' in 'ship'

    ch = [sh] (श). 'tch' is spelled tch in French.

    Exercise 8

    'gn' in 'montagne' sounds like…

    1. g + n
    2. 'ny' in 'canyon'
    3. 'ng' in 'sing'
    4. just n
    Answer

    'ny' in 'canyon'

    gn is one sound: [mõ-TANY].

    Exercise 9

    The French R is made at the ___ of the mouth, not with the tongue tip.

    Hint: front or back?

    Answer

    back

    Back of the tongue near the soft palate, like a soft ग़; the tip stays down.

    Exercise 10

    Tap the word that means something dangerous instead of 'fish'.

    Je mange du poison à midi.

    Answer

    Wrong word: poison.

    Corrected: Je mange du poisson à midi.

    poisson (ss = [s]) is fish; poison (single s = [z]) is poison!

    Exercise 11

    Where is the stress in French 'restaurant'?

    1. res-
    2. -tau-
    3. -rant (last)
    4. nowhere
    Answer

    -rant (last)

    French stresses the last syllable: res-to-RÃ.

    Exercise 12

    How is 'les' pronounced?

    1. [luh]
    2. [lay]
    3. [less]
    4. [lez]
    Answer

    [lay]

    les = [lay] (plural); le = [luh] (singular). The s is silent.

    Exercise 13

    In speech, what makes 'Tu viens ?' a question?

    1. A rising voice at the end
    2. A louder first word
    3. A longer 'tu'
    4. Nothing
    Answer

    A rising voice at the end

    Same words, rising melody = yes/no question.

    Exercise 14

    Which Hindi word ends in a sound close to French 'on' [õ]?

    1. क्यों (kyon)
    2. कल (kal)
    3. दिल (dil)
    4. राम (raam)
    Answer

    क्यों (kyon)

    The nasal o at the end of क्यों is very close to French [õ].

    Exercise 15

    Match the u / ou pairs.

    AB
    la rueyou
    la roueall
    tuthe street
    toutthe wheel
    Answer

    la rue — the street; la roue — the wheel; tu — you; tout — all

    u [ew] vs ou [oo] changes the word.

    Exercise 16

    Put the words in order: 'A good white wine.'

    Words: bon / vin / blanc / Un

    Answer

    Un bon vin blanc

    In French most adjectives follow the noun (vin blanc), but bon goes before.

    Exercise 17

    Say it aloud — four nasal vowels.

    Un bon vin blanc

    Answer

    Say: Un bon vin blanc

    [ɛ̃ bõ vɛ̃ blã] — no n or ng at the end of any word.

    Exercise 18

    Say it aloud — u then ou.

    Vous avez vu la rue

    Answer

    Say: Vous avez vu la rue

    vous [oo], vu [ew], rue [ew] — and link vous‿avez with a z.

    Exercise 19

    Say it aloud — ss and s.

    Le poisson n'est pas du poison

    Answer

    Say: Le poisson n'est pas du poison

    poisson [s], poison [z].
