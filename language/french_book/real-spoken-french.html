    Chapter 38: Real spoken French¶

    Le français parlé

    You studied Je ne sais pas. Then you arrive in France and hear "chais pas". This is not slang and it is not bad French — it is how almost everyone speaks, every day. The grammar in your book is the written code; this chapter is the spoken one. First learn to understand it, then use the parts that feel natural to you.

    Two Frenches, one language

    Written French keeps every little word. Everyday spoken French drops, squeezes and reorders them. Both are correct in their place — you need both.

    38.1 The disappearing ne¶

    In conversation, the ne of the negative is dropped almost all the time. The pas (or rien, jamais, personne, plus) carries the negative alone. The full ne… pas is still used in writing, speeches, news and careful speech.

    • Je sais pas. — I don’t know. (written: Je ne sais pas.)
    • C’est pas grave. — It doesn’t matter. / No worries.
    • J’ai rien compris. — I didn’t understand a thing.
    • Il y a personne. — There’s nobody here.
    • Je fume plus. — I don’t smoke any more. (here plus is said "plu", no s)
    • T’inquiète ! — Don’t worry! — even pas has gone: Ne t’inquiète pas → T’inquiète.

    Careful

    • Wrong: Je ne sais. (pas dropped)
    • Right: Je sais pas. / Je ne sais pas.
    • Why: Spoken French drops ne, never pas. Pas is the word that really means "not".

    38.2 On instead of nous¶

    In speech, on has almost replaced nous for "we". It takes the il/elle form of the verb — which is easier anyway. To insist, people say nous, on…

    Written Spoken
    Nous allons au cinéma. On va au ciné.
    Nous avons mangé. On a mangé.
    Nous partons ? On y va ?
    Nous, nous restons. Nous, on reste.
    Nous nous voyons demain. On se voit demain.

    Careful

    • Wrong: On allons au parc.
    • Right: On va au parc.
    • Why: On means "we" but conjugates like il: on va, on fait, on est. (Adjectives may still agree: On est fatigués.)

    on = we, people, someone

    On also means "people in general" (En France, on mange tard.) or "someone" (On m’a volé mon vélo.). Context tells you which.

    38.3 Squeezed words — elisions and reductions¶

    In fast speech, the short unstressed vowels vanish: the e of je, ne, le, de, que, the u of tu, the l of il. Consonants then crash into each other and change. You don’t write these forms (except in texts and comics), but you hear them all day.

    Full form You hear
    tu es / tu as t’es / t’as
    il y a y a
    il faut faut
    je suis j’suis / chuis
    il dit / elle dit i’ dit / è dit
    peut-être p’têt
    parce que pa’ce que
    qu’est-ce que qu’est-c’que [kes-kuh]
    quelque chose quèque chose
    puis pis

    The 🔊 voice is a careful reading voice, so it often says the fuller form. Real speakers squeeze more — use the buttons for the words, and real French videos for the speed.

    t’es ≠ tes, t’as ≠ ta

    T’es (you are) sounds like tes (your, plural), and t’as (you have) like ta (your). Meaning comes from what follows: T’as vu tes clés ? = Have you seen your keys?

    38.4 Questions, the spoken way¶

    In writing you meet three question forms (see Asking questions). In speech, one dominates: the plain statement with a rising voice. Question words like où, quoi, quand, combien often go to the end. Inversion (Où vas-tu ?) survives mostly in fixed phrases and formal talk.

    Formal / written Everyday spoken
    Où vas-tu ? Tu vas où ?
    Qu’est-ce que c’est ? C’est quoi ?
    Que fais-tu ce soir ? Tu fais quoi ce soir ?
    Quand arrive-t-il ? Il arrive quand ?
    Combien cela coûte-t-il ? Ça coûte combien ?
    Qui est-ce ? C’est qui ?
    Pourquoi pars-tu ? Pourquoi tu pars ?

    Quoi ? and Hein ? are blunt

    Friends say Quoi ? or Hein ? when they didn’t hear. With strangers or at work, use Pardon ? or Comment ? — otherwise you sound rude.

    38.5 Fillers and little words¶

    Fillers give the speaker time and colour the sentence. They are the first thing that makes a learner sound natural — and the first thing that makes them sound silly if overused. Learn to recognise all of these; pick two or three to use.

    Filler What it does Example
    euh… thinking (English "um") C’était, euh… mardi.
    ben / bah "well", obvious answer Ben oui !
    bon "right", moving on Bon, on y va ?
    du coup "so / as a result" Il pleuvait, du coup on est restés.
    en fait "actually", correcting En fait, c’est pas loin.
    genre "like" (young speakers) Il était genre super énervé.
    … quoi end tag: "you know" C’était nul, quoi.
    voilà "that’s it", closing a point Et voilà, c’est fini.
    bref "anyway", cutting short Bref, on a raté le train.
    tu vois / tu sais checking you follow Le petit café, tu vois ?
    enfin "I mean", self-correcting Lundi, enfin, mardi.

    Careful

    • Wrong: Du coup, j’ai, genre, du coup, réservé, quoi, du coup.
    • Right: Du coup, j’ai réservé.
    • Why: Du coup is so overused that even the French make fun of it. One filler per sentence, at most.

    Hinglish has the same thing

    Du coup works a lot like "toh" or "so basically", and quoi at the end like "na" or "yaar". You already know how fillers feel — you just need the French ones.

    38.6 Social codes: tu, ouais and friends¶

    Among young people, and increasingly in start-ups and creative jobs, tu is the default from the first second. With older people or in traditional settings, wait for them to offer it (see Hello, please, thank you). Everyday yes/no and greetings also have relaxed versions. (For true slang and verlan, see Registers & slang.)

    Careful Relaxed Meaning
    Oui / Non Ouais / Nan yeah / nah
    Bonjour Salut / Coucou hi / hiya (coucou: friends, family)
    Au revoir Salut / À plus / À toute bye / see you / see you later
    D’accord OK / Ça marche OK / sounds good
    Il n’y a pas de problème. Pas de souci ! no problem
    Vous voulez qu’on se tutoie ? On se tutoie ? shall we use tu?

    Ouais ouais… = I don’t believe you

    A single ouais is a relaxed yes. A slow, falling ouais, ouais… or mouais means doubt — like a sarcastic "yeah, right".

    38.7 Side by side: written vs spoken¶

    Here is the same content in both codes. Read the left column, then listen to the right one — this is what you will actually hear.

    Written / careful Everyday spoken
    Je ne sais pas où il est. Chais pas où il est.
    Est-ce que tu as vu ce film ? T’as vu ce film ?
    Il n’y a plus de pain. Y a plus de pain.
    Nous ne sommes pas prêts. On est pas prêts.
    Il faut que nous partions. Faut qu’on y aille.
    Cela ne fait rien. Ça fait rien.
    Ce n’est pas la peine. Pas la peine.
    Tu es très fatigué ? T’es crevé ?
    Je ne comprends rien. J’comprends rien.

    Mini-dialogue — two friends, real speed

    The gloss gives the careful French, then the English.

    • Inès : Salut ! T’es où, là ? — = Où es-tu en ce moment ? — Hi! Where are you right now?
    • Karim : Bah chuis encore au boulot. Y a eu un problème, du coup j’suis en retard. — = Eh bien, je suis encore au travail. Il y a eu un problème, donc je suis en retard. — Well, I’m still at work. There was a problem, so I’m late.
    • Inès : C’est pas grave. On se retrouve au ciné à huit heures ? — = Ce n’est pas grave. Nous nous retrouvons au cinéma… — No problem. Shall we meet at the cinema at eight?
    • Karim : Ouais, ça marche. T’as pris les places ? — = Oui, d’accord. As-tu pris les billets ? — Yeah, sounds good. Did you get the tickets?
    • Inès : Ben non, j’attendais de savoir si tu venais, quoi. — = Eh bien non, j’attendais de savoir si tu venais. — Well no, I was waiting to know if you were coming, you know.
    • Karim : OK, j’m’en occupe. Allez, à toute ! — = Je m’en occupe. À tout à l’heure ! — OK, I’ll take care of it. Right, see you later!

    38.8 Training your ear¶

    Spoken French is fast because the rhythm groups (see Pronunciation clinic) glue words together. You don’t hear words — you hear blocks. Train yourself to catch the blocks.

    • Chunks, not words: Qu’est-ce que t’en penses ? — You hear one block: "kes-kuh-tã-PÃS". Learn it as one unit — What do you think of it?
    • Catch the stressed end: Je crois qu’il est parti à la gare. — Listen for the long syllables: …par-TI… la GARE. — I think he’s left for the station.
    • Shadow it: Ah ouais, c’est clair, j’suis d’accord. — Play, then speak at the same time as the voice. Yeah, totally, I agree.

    Understand everything, imitate selectively

    Dropping ne, using on and asking Tu fais quoi ? are safe for you to use at once. Leave heavy reductions (chuis, chais pas) and fillers like genre until they come out by themselves.

    A daily listening routine

    Ten minutes a day: a French street-interview video or a podcast for learners. Watch French series with French subtitles, not English ones. Note one spoken form a day (y a, t’as, faut…) and listen for it until you catch it every time.

    À retenir

    • Written French keeps every little word. Everyday spoken French drops, squeezes and reorders them. Both are correct in their place — you need both.
    • On also means "people in general" (En France, on mange tard.) or "someone" (On m’a volé mon vélo.). Context tells you which.
    • Du coup works a lot like "toh" or "so basically", and quoi at the end like "na" or "yaar". You already know how fillers feel — you just need the French ones.
    • Dropping ne, using on and asking Tu fais quoi ? are safe for you to use at once. Leave heavy reductions (chuis, chais pas) and fillers like genre until they come out by themselves.
    • Ten minutes a day: a French street-interview video or a podcast for learners. Watch French series with French subtitles, not English ones. Note one spoken form a day (y a, t’as, faut…) and listen for it until you catch it…
    • Now switch codes. At work, French gets more careful again: Work, phone & interviews gives you phone calls, meetings and job interviews — with vous and the full ne.

    38.9 Practice — Real spoken French¶

    Exercise 1

    In everyday speech, 'Je ne sais pas' usually becomes…

    1. Je ne sais.
    2. Je sais pas.
    3. Ne sais pas.
    4. Je pas sais.
    Answer

    Je sais pas.

    Spoken French drops ne, never pas.

    Exercise 2

    'On va au ciné ?' — what does 'on' mean here?

    1. one (formal)
    2. we
    3. they
    4. you (plural)
    Answer

    we

    In speech, on almost always replaces nous for 'we'.

    Exercise 3

    'T'as vu ?' is a reduction of…

    1. Ta vue ?
    2. Tu as vu ?
    3. Tu es vu ?
    4. T'avais vu ?
    Answer

    Tu as vu ?

    tu as → t'as.

    Exercise 4

    You didn't hear your boss. Best word?

    1. Quoi ?
    2. Hein ?
    3. Pardon ?
    4. Bah ?
    Answer

    Pardon ?

    Quoi ? and Hein ? are fine with friends but blunt with strangers or at work.

    Exercise 5

    On ___ (aller) au parc cet après-midi.

    Hint: on conjugates like il.

    Answer

    va

    on va — the il/elle form.

    Exercise 6

    Spoken: 'Y ___ du monde.' (= Il y a du monde.)

    Answer

    a

    il y a → y a.

    Exercise 7

    Tap the wrong word.

    On allons au restaurant ce soir.

    Answer

    Wrong word: allons.

    Corrected: On va au restaurant ce soir.

    on takes the il form: on va.

    Exercise 8

    You are speaking to a shop assistant you don't know. Tap the word that sounds rude.

    Quoi ? Vous pouvez répéter, s'il vous plaît ?

    Answer

    Wrong word: Quoi.

    Corrected: Pardon ? Vous pouvez répéter, s'il vous plaît ?

    Quoi ? is fine with friends, but blunt with strangers. Use Pardon ? or Comment ?

    Exercise 9

    Match the filler to its job.

    AB
    du coupanyway, to cut it short
    en faityou know
    brefso, as a result
    quoi (at the end)actually
    Answer

    du coup — so, as a result; en fait — actually; bref — anyway, to cut it short; quoi (at the end) — you know

    Recognise them all; use two or three yourself.

    Exercise 10

    Match the spoken question to the formal one.

    AB
    Tu vas où ?Quand arrive-t-il ?
    C'est quoi ?Qui est-ce ?
    Il arrive quand ?Où vas-tu ?
    C'est qui ?Qu'est-ce que c'est ?
    Answer

    Tu vas où ? — Où vas-tu ?; C'est quoi ? — Qu'est-ce que c'est ?; Il arrive quand ? — Quand arrive-t-il ?; C'est qui ? — Qui est-ce ?

    In speech, question words often move to the end.

    Exercise 11

    Put the words in order (spoken style).

    Words: quoi / fais / Tu / ce / soir

    Answer

    Tu fais quoi ce soir

    Spoken style: statement order + question word at the end.

    Exercise 12

    Put the words in order.

    Words: du / Il / coup / restés / pleuvait / est / on

    Answer

    Il pleuvait du coup on est restés

    du coup = so, as a result.

    Exercise 13

    Say 'I don't know' the everyday way.

    Je sais pas

    Answer

    Say: Je sais pas

    No ne in normal speech.

    Exercise 14

    Ask where your friend is.

    T'es où

    Answer

    Say: T'es où

    tu es → t'es; question word at the end.

    Exercise 15

    Say 'It doesn't matter' (spoken).

    C'est pas grave

    Answer

    Say: C'est pas grave

    Ce n'est pas grave → C'est pas grave.

    Exercise 16

    Suggest leaving.

    On y va

    Answer

    Say: On y va

    on = we. On y va ? = Shall we go?

    Exercise 17

    Say 'There's no more bread' (spoken).

    Y a plus de pain

    Answer

    Say: Y a plus de pain

    Il n'y a plus → Y a plus.

    Exercise 18

    Say 'No problem!' the relaxed way.

    Pas de souci

    Answer

    Say: Pas de souci

    Pas de souci ! is everywhere in everyday French.
