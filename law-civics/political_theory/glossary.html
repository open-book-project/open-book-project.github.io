    Glossary¶

    Terms used in this book, with the chapter where each is explained

    A–C¶

    Ahiṃsā
    Non-violence, understood by Gandhi as active love that excludes hatred of the opponent; the basis of satyagraha. (Chapter 13)
    Alienation
    In Marx, the estrangement of workers under capitalism from the product and activity of their labour, from their human nature (species-being) and from one another. (Chapter 11)
    Anarchism
    The belief that the state is unnecessary and harmful and that society can be organised by voluntary cooperation. (Chapter 12)
    Arthaśāstra
    The Sanskrit treatise on statecraft attributed to Kauṭilya, describing administration, law, economy and foreign policy. (Chapter 13)
    Authority
    The recognised right to command and be obeyed; power accepted as rightful. Weber distinguished traditional, charismatic and legal-rational authority. (Chapter 3)
    Basic structure
    For Rawls, the main political, economic and social institutions of a society, which are the primary subject of justice. In Indian constitutional law, the essential features of the Constitution that amendment cannot destroy. (Chapters 6 and 10)
    Capability
    In Sen and Nussbaum, a person's real freedom to achieve valued functionings (beings and doings). (Chapters 5 and 6)
    Citizenship
    Full membership of a political community, combining legal status, rights and participation. (Chapter 9)
    Civic nationalism
    Nationalism that bases the nation on shared citizenship and political values, open to those who adopt them. (Chapter 9)
    Civil disobedience
    Public, non-violent, conscientious breach of law aimed at changing a law or policy, with acceptance of the penalty. (Chapter 3)
    Communitarianism
    The view that individuals are shaped by their communities and that the common good and shared values deserve more weight than liberal individualism allows. (Chapters 6 and 12)
    Condorcet's paradox
    The possibility that majority preferences over three or more options form a cycle, so that no option beats all others. (Chapter 8)
    Conservatism
    The ideology that values tradition, organic society, gradual change and authority, and is sceptical of abstract reason in politics. (Chapter 11)
    Cosmopolitanism
    The view that every human being has equal moral standing and that principles of justice apply to all people regardless of nationality. (Chapter 14)
    Cultural relativism
    The view that moral values are rooted in particular cultures and cannot be judged by a universal standard. (Chapter 14)

    D–H¶

    Daṇḍa
    Literally "rod": coercive authority and punishment in ancient Indian thought; also one of the seven limbs of the state (the army) and one of the four means (force). (Chapter 13)
    Deliberative democracy
    The view that democratic legitimacy comes from public reasoning among free and equal citizens, not only from counting votes. (Chapter 8)
    Democracy
    Rule by the people; procedural accounts stress free elections and rights, substantive accounts also equality and participation. (Chapter 8)
    Descriptive claim
    A claim about what is the case, true or false as a matter of fact. (Chapter 1)
    Difference principle
    Rawls's principle that social and economic inequalities must work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. (Chapter 6)
    Dharma
    Cosmic order, moral law and duty in Indian thought; government was expected to uphold it. (Chapter 13)
    Domination
    Being subject to the arbitrary will of another; republicans define freedom as its absence. (Chapter 4)
    Entitlement theory
    Nozick's theory that a distribution is just if it arises from just acquisition and just transfer, whatever its pattern. (Chapter 6)
    Equality of opportunity
    The principle that positions should be open to all; in its fair version, that people of similar talent and effort should have similar chances whatever their background. (Chapter 5)
    Essentially contested concept
    A concept, such as democracy or justice, that is evaluative, complex and open to rival interpretations, so that disputes about its use cannot be settled by definition. (Chapter 1)
    Ethnic nationalism
    Nationalism that bases the nation on shared descent, ethnicity, language or religion. (Chapter 9)
    Fascism
    A twentieth-century ideology of extreme nationalism, totalitarian rule, the leader principle and glorification of violence. (Chapter 12)
    Feminism
    The belief that women have been unjustly disadvantaged because of their sex and the commitment to ending that disadvantage. (Chapter 12)
    Functioning
    In the capability approach, an achieved state of being or doing, such as being nourished or literate. (Chapter 6)
    General will
    In Rousseau, the will of citizens directed at the common good, as distinct from the sum of private wills (the will of all). (Chapter 2)
    Hegemony
    In Gramsci, rule by consent through culture and ideas as well as by coercion. (Chapter 3)
    Historical materialism
    Marx's theory that the economic base (forces and relations of production) shapes the rest of society and drives historical change. (Chapter 11)

    I–M¶

    Ideology
    A more or less coherent set of ideas offering a description of society, a vision of a better one and a programme of action. (Chapter 11)
    Imagined community
    Anderson's description of the nation: a community whose members cannot know one another personally but share an image of their communion. (Chapter 9)
    Interest theory of rights
    The view that rights protect important interests of the right-holder. (Chapter 7)
    Intersectionality
    The way different forms of discrimination (by gender, race, class, caste and others) combine to shape distinctive experiences. (Chapter 12)
    Jus sanguinis
    Citizenship by descent from a citizen parent. (Chapter 9)
    Jus soli
    Citizenship by birth on a state's territory. (Chapter 9)
    Laïcité
    The French model of secularism, which keeps religion out of public institutions. (Chapter 10)
    Legitimacy
    The rightfulness of a political order; understood sociologically as belief in its rightfulness or normatively as its justification. (Chapter 3)
    Liberalism
    The ideology that puts the free individual first and values liberty, reason, equality, toleration and government by consent. (Chapter 11)
    Luck egalitarianism
    The view that inequalities resulting from unchosen circumstances (brute luck) are unjust, while those resulting from choices may stand. (Chapter 5)
    Maṇḍala theory
    Kauṭilya's account of a king's international environment as circles of enemies and allies. (Chapter 13)
    Maximin
    The decision rule of choosing the option whose worst outcome is best; used by Rawls to defend the difference principle. (Chapter 6)
    Multiculturalism
    The view that cultural diversity should be recognised and accommodated, sometimes through group-differentiated rights. (Chapters 9 and 12)

    N–R¶

    Nation
    A large group of people who see themselves as sharing a common identity and political destiny, usually seeking self-government. (Chapter 9)
    Negative liberty
    Freedom as the absence of deliberate interference by other people. (Chapter 4)
    Normative claim
    A claim about what ought to be the case, which needs an argument about values. (Chapter 1)
    Original position
    Rawls's hypothetical situation in which parties behind a veil of ignorance choose principles of justice. (Chapter 6)
    Paternalism
    Interference with a person's liberty for his or her own good; soft paternalism checks that choices are informed and voluntary, hard paternalism overrides them. (Chapter 4)
    Pluralism
    Several distinct ideas: the dispersal of authority among associations (pluralist theory of sovereignty), competition among groups (pluralist democracy), and the existence of many irreducible values (value pluralism). (Chapters 2, 4 and 8)
    Political obligation
    The moral duty, if any, to obey the law of one's state because it is the law. (Chapter 3)
    Positive liberty
    Freedom as self-mastery or self-government. (Chapter 4)
    Power
    The capacity to bring about outcomes, especially to get others to do what they would not otherwise do; Lukes described three dimensions. (Chapter 3)
    Primary goods
    In Rawls, goods any rational person wants whatever else they want: rights and liberties, opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect. (Chapter 6)
    Principled distance
    Bhargava's description of the Indian model of secularism: no established religion, with flexible engagement with religions guided by liberty and equality. (Chapter 10)
    Prioritarianism
    The view that benefits matter more the worse off the person who receives them. (Chapter 5)
    Reflective equilibrium
    The method of testing principles against considered judgements about cases and revising each until they cohere. (Chapter 1)
    Responsibility to protect
    The doctrine, endorsed by the UN in 2005, that states must protect their populations from mass atrocities and that the international community may act collectively when they manifestly fail. (Chapter 14)
    Right
    A justified entitlement that belongs to a holder, imposes duties on others and carries special weight. (Chapter 7)

    S–Z¶

    Saptāṅga
    The seven limbs of the state in the Arthaśāstra: ruler, ministers, territory and people, fort, treasury, army and ally. (Chapter 13)
    Sarvodaya
    "The welfare of all": Gandhi's ideal of a society serving the good of every person, including the least. (Chapter 13)
    Satyagraha
    "Truth-force": Gandhi's method of non-violent resistance that seeks to convert the opponent through willing self-suffering. (Chapter 13)
    Secularism
    The political doctrine that the state should not be governed by or identified with any religion, and that citizens' rights should not depend on religion. (Chapter 10)
    Self-determination
    The principle that a people has the right to decide its own political status. (Chapter 9)
    Social contract
    The idea that political authority is justified by the actual or hypothetical agreement of those subject to it. (Chapter 2)
    Socialism
    A family of ideologies valuing community, equality, need and, in many versions, common ownership. (Chapter 11)
    Sovereignty
    Supreme authority: internal (over everyone within the territory) and external (independence from other states); legal, political or popular. (Chapter 2)
    State
    In Weber's definition, a human community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory. (Chapter 2)
    Sufficientarianism
    The view that what matters is that everyone has enough, not that everyone has the same. (Chapter 5)
    Surplus value
    In Marx, the value produced by workers beyond the value of their wages, appropriated as profit. (Chapter 11)
    Swaraj
    Self-rule; for Gandhi both individual self-control and collective self-government. (Chapter 13)
    Tyranny of the majority
    The oppression of minorities or dissenters by a majority, through law or social pressure (Tocqueville, Mill). (Chapters 4 and 8)
    Universal Declaration of Human Rights
    The declaration of 30 articles adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948. (Chapter 14)
    Utilitarianism
    The theory that the right action or policy is the one that produces the greatest total happiness or well-being. (Chapter 6)
    Veil of ignorance
    In Rawls's original position, the restriction that prevents the parties from knowing their own place in society, talents or conception of the good. (Chapter 6)
    Will theory of rights
    The view that rights give their holders control over the duties of others. (Chapter 7)
