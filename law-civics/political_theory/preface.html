    Preface¶

    What political theory is for, and how to read this book

    About this book¶

    Every society has to answer the same few questions. Who should rule, and why should anyone obey them? How much may the state tell us what to do? What do we owe one another, and what may we keep for ourselves? Who belongs to the community, and on what terms? Political theory is the careful, reasoned study of such questions. It does not ask only how political life works; it also asks how it ought to work, and it tests the answers by argument.

    This book is an introduction to that study. It explains the central concepts of politics (the state, sovereignty, power, authority, liberty, equality, justice, rights, democracy, citizenship, nationalism and secularism), the great families of political ideas (liberalism, conservatism, socialism and Marxism, feminism, environmentalism and others), the political thought of modern India, and the questions of justice and human rights that cross national borders.

    The book tries hard to be fair. Political theory is a field of disagreement: thoughtful people reach opposite conclusions about liberty, equality or the proper role of religion in public life. For every major view the book sets out what it says, the reasons offered for it, and the main criticisms made of it. It does not tell you which view to hold. Its aim is to help you understand each position well enough to state it in a way its own defenders would accept, and then to judge it for yourself. The book makes no claims about present-day politicians or political parties; where it discusses events, they are historical.

    Who it is for¶

    The book is written for a reader meeting the subject for the first time:

    • a student in a first university course in political science, philosophy, law, sociology or public policy;
    • a candidate preparing for a competitive examination with a paper on political theory or political thought;
    • a teacher, a journalist or any citizen who wants to think more clearly about the arguments heard in public life.

    No background is needed beyond school. Technical words are explained when they first appear, and the glossary at the end collects them.

    How the book is organised¶

    The fourteen chapters are grouped in five parts.

    Part Chapters What it covers
    I. Foundations 1–3 What political theory is; the state and sovereignty; power, authority and legitimacy
    II. Core values 4–7 Liberty; equality; justice (including Rawls, Nozick and Sen); rights
    III. Democracy and belonging 8–10 Democracy; citizenship and nationalism; secularism
    IV. Ideologies and traditions 11–13 Liberalism, conservatism, socialism and Marxism; feminism, environmentalism and other ideologies; Indian political thought
    V. Beyond the state 14 Global justice and human rights

    Table P.1. The plan of the book.

    The order is deliberate. The first part explains the basic setting of politics: an organised community with a state that claims the right to rule. The second part examines the values by which that rule is judged. The third asks who governs and who belongs. The fourth shows how values are combined into larger outlooks, and how Indian thinkers worked on the same questions in their own circumstances. The last part asks whether the ideas of justice and rights stop at the borders of the state.

    How each chapter is built¶

    Every chapter has the same parts, so that you always know where you are.

    • Learning outcomes at the top say what you should be able to do when you have finished the chapter.
    • Numbered sections move from an everyday puzzle to a precise statement of the concept and the main theories about it.
    • Boxes pick out what matters. A key idea box states the one thing in a section not to forget. A note adds a detail, a piece of history or a link to another chapter. A caution marks a common confusion.
    • Worked examples take a concrete case (a ban on a protest march, a scholarship scheme, a dispute about a school uniform) and show how the theories in the chapter would analyse it.
    • Figures and tables show comparisons and structures that are hard to hold in words.
    • A summary collects the chapter in a few points.
    • Exercises come with answers folded under them. Try each one before you open the answer. Many exercises have no single right answer; the folded text then shows what a good answer would contain.
    • A short quiz at the end lets you check yourself; it remembers your answers in your browser.

    How to use it¶

    Read the chapters in order the first time. Later chapters lean on earlier ones: justice (Chapter 6) uses the ideas of liberty and equality from Chapters 4 and 5, and the ideologies of Chapters 11 and 12 combine almost every concept in the book.

    Four habits will help you more than any amount of memorising.

    1. Define before you argue. Many political quarrels are really disagreements about the meaning of a word. Before you ask whether a law restricts liberty, ask which idea of liberty you have in mind.
    2. Look for the reasons. A position in political theory is only as good as the arguments for it. When you meet a claim, ask what reasons support it and what would count against it.
    3. State the other side at its strongest. You do not understand a view until you can present it as well as its best defender would. Then criticise it.
    4. Test theories against cases. A principle that sounds attractive in general may give a strange answer in a particular case. Use the worked examples, and invent your own.

    A note on sources and quotations¶

    The thinkers discussed in this book wrote over more than two thousand years, in many languages. Their ideas are explained here in plain words. Short quotations are given only from works that are long out of copyright (such as those of Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Mill, Wollstonecraft and Tagore), from public records, or where a phrase has become a well-known part of the language of politics. Everything else is paraphrase. Dates given for books are the dates of first publication. The list of further reading at the end names standard editions and textbooks for going deeper; reading at least one primary text in full, such as Mill's On Liberty, is the best next step after this book.
