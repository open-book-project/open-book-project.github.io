    Chapter 12: Feminism, Environmentalism and Other Ideologies¶

    New questions for political theory: gender, nature, culture, authority and empire

    Learning outcomes

    When you have finished this chapter you should be able to:

    • explain the key concepts of feminism (patriarchy, sex and gender, the public–private divide) and its main waves and varieties, including intersectionality;
    • describe feminist thought in India from the nineteenth century;
    • explain the core ideas of environmentalism, including the distinction between shallow and deep ecology, sustainable development and environmental justice;
    • outline anarchism, multiculturalism and communitarianism, with their main criticisms;
    • describe fascism as a historical ideology and explain why it is condemned;
    • explain the main ideas of postcolonial thought.

    12.1 Beyond the classical ideologies¶

    The ideologies of Chapter 11 were shaped by the conflicts of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: monarchy against representative government, tradition against reason, capital against labour. In the twentieth century other questions forced their way onto the political agenda. Why had half of humanity been excluded from political life? Could the earth sustain unlimited economic growth? How should states treat cultural minorities? What did the end of empire mean for the ideas of the colonisers? This chapter surveys the ideologies that grew from these questions, together with older ideologies, anarchism and fascism, that sit outside the usual left–right line.

    12.2 Feminism¶

    Feminism is the belief that women have been, and are, unjustly disadvantaged because of their sex, and the commitment to ending that disadvantage. Feminists disagree about the causes of women's subordination and the remedies, but they share two claims: that the relations between the sexes are political (shaped by power and open to change) and that they have been unjust.

    Key concepts¶

    • Patriarchy. Literally "rule of the father", the term is used by feminists for a social system in which men as a group hold power over women, in the family, the economy, the state and culture.
    • Sex and gender. Many feminists distinguish sex (biological differences) from gender (the social roles, behaviour and expectations attached to being a man or a woman). Gender roles vary between societies and change over time, which shows they are not fixed by nature. Simone de Beauvoir, in The Second Sex (1949), expressed this in a famous phrase: one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.
    • The public–private divide. Liberal political theory traditionally separated a public sphere of politics and the market, where justice applies, from a private sphere of the family, regarded as natural and outside politics. Feminists argued that this division hid domestic violence, unequal division of labour and male authority from political scrutiny. The slogan of the 1960s and 1970s, "the personal is political", expressed the claim that private life is shaped by power.

    Waves of feminism¶

    Historians often describe feminism in waves, though the metaphor simplifies a continuous history.

    • First wave (mid-nineteenth to early twentieth century): demands for legal and political equality, above all the right to vote, and rights to property, education and the professions. Early landmarks include Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), which argued that women's apparent inferiority was the result of their lack of education, and J. S. Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869), written with ideas developed with Harriet Taylor Mill, which argued that the legal subordination of women was wrong in itself and a hindrance to human improvement. Women won the vote in New Zealand in 1893, in Britain on equal terms with men in 1928, and in many countries only after the Second World War.
    • Second wave (1960s to 1980s): with legal equality largely won in many Western countries, feminists turned to discrimination in work, sexuality, reproduction, the family and culture. Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) described the dissatisfaction of educated housewives; radical feminists analysed patriarchy as a pervasive system.
    • Third wave (from the 1990s): emphasis on diversity among women, on identity, and on the different experiences of women of different races, classes, sexualities and nations.
    • Some writers speak of a fourth wave from the 2010s, using online activism against sexual harassment and violence.

    Varieties of feminism¶

    Variety Cause of women's subordination Remedy
    Liberal feminism Unequal rights and opportunities; prejudice Equal legal and political rights; equal opportunity in education and work
    Socialist and Marxist feminism Capitalism and the economic dependence of women; unpaid domestic labour Transform economic structures as well as gender relations
    Radical feminism Patriarchy as the most basic form of oppression, present in all spheres including sexuality and the family Transform all social relations; sometimes separate women's organisation
    Black and intersectional feminism Interlocking systems of oppression by gender, race, class and others Address the specific experience of women facing several forms of oppression
    Postcolonial feminism Colonial and global inequalities, alongside local patriarchies Challenge Western feminism's claim to speak for all women

    Table 12.1. Varieties of feminism.

    Intersectionality, a term introduced by the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, describes how different forms of discrimination (by sex, race, class, caste, disability, sexuality) intersect, so that the experience of, say, a Black woman or a Dalit woman cannot be understood simply by adding the experience of race or caste to that of gender. The postcolonial scholar Chandra Talpade Mohanty, in the essay "Under Western Eyes" (1984), criticised Western feminist writing that portrayed "Third World women" as a single, passive, victimised group.

    Feminism in India¶

    Indian feminism has its own history. In the nineteenth century social reformers campaigned against practices such as sati and child marriage and for widow remarriage and women's education. Savitribai Phule and Jyotirao Phule opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848, challenging both caste and gender hierarchies. Tarabai Shinde's Stri Purush Tulana (A Comparison of Women and Men, 1882) is an early, sharp critique of the double standards applied to women. Pandita Ramabai campaigned for the education and welfare of widows and wrote The High-Caste Hindu Woman (1887). Women took part in large numbers in the national movement, and the Constitution of 1950 granted universal adult franchise, so that Indian women voted on equal terms with men from the first general election. Since independence, women's movements have campaigned on issues including dowry, violence against women, equal pay, representation and land rights. The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) reserved one-third of seats in panchayats and municipalities for women, and the 106th Amendment (2023) provides for reserving one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies for women, to come into effect after a fresh delimitation.

    Criticisms of feminism¶

    • Conservatives argue that differences between men's and women's roles reflect nature or valuable tradition, and that feminism undermines the family.
    • Some liberals worry that feminist proposals for regulating private life or speech conflict with individual liberty.
    • Within feminism, women of colour, working-class women and women from the global South have criticised mainstream feminism for generalising from the experience of educated, Western, middle-class women; intersectional and postcolonial feminism grew from this criticism.
    • There are continuing debates within feminism, for example about pornography, sex work and the relation between sex and gender identity.

    Key idea

    Feminism holds that relations between the sexes are political and have been unjust. Its varieties differ on the causes of women's subordination (unequal rights, capitalism, patriarchy, intersecting oppressions) and therefore on the remedies.

    12.3 Environmentalism¶

    Environmentalism (or ecologism) is the ideology that places the relation between human beings and the natural world at the centre of politics. It grew from the 1960s in response to pollution, the destruction of habitats and species, the depletion of resources and, later, climate change. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962), on the effects of pesticides, is often seen as a starting point of the modern movement.

    Core ideas¶

    • Ecology. All living things, including humans, are part of interdependent ecosystems. Damage in one place has effects elsewhere.
    • Limits to growth. Infinite economic growth on a finite planet is impossible. The report The Limits to Growth (1972), commissioned by the Club of Rome, used computer models to argue that continued growth in population and production would exhaust resources.
    • Sustainability. The report of the World Commission on Environment and Development, Our Common Future (1987, known as the Brundtland Report), defined sustainable development as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
    • Responsibility to future generations and to other species. Environmentalists extend moral concern beyond the present generation and, in some versions, beyond human beings.

    Shallow and deep ecology¶

    The Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess distinguished in 1973 between shallow and deep ecology.

    Shallow (light green) ecology Deep (dark green) ecology
    Moral centre Anthropocentric: nature matters because it matters to humans Ecocentric: nature and all living things have value in themselves
    Aim Protect the environment for human health and prosperity Respect the intrinsic value of the whole of nature
    Means Reform within existing economic and political systems: regulation, green technology, pricing pollution Fundamental change in values, lifestyles and economic systems
    Attitude to growth Sustainable or "green" growth is possible Sceptical of growth; may favour reduced consumption

    Table 12.2. Shallow and deep ecology.

    Varieties¶

    • Reformist environmentalism works within liberal democratic and market systems: environmental regulation, carbon taxes, emissions trading and renewable energy.
    • Eco-socialism argues that capitalism, with its drive for profit and growth, is the root cause of environmental destruction.
    • Eco-feminism connects the domination of nature with the domination of women; the Indian scholar and activist Vandana Shiva (Staying Alive, 1988) argued that development models had marginalised both women's knowledge and nature.
    • Eco-anarchism and social ecology (Murray Bookchin) favour small, decentralised, self-governing communities living in harmony with their environment.

    Environmental justice and India¶

    The environmental justice movement points out that environmental harms (pollution, toxic waste, displacement by dams and mines) fall disproportionately on the poor, on indigenous peoples and on marginalised communities, while the benefits of resource use go mostly to others. In India the Chipko movement, which began in the Himalayan region of what is now Uttarakhand in 1973, saw villagers, many of them women, embrace trees to prevent them from being felled by contractors. The historian Ramachandra Guha and others have described an "environmentalism of the poor" in which struggles over forests, water and land are struggles for livelihood as well as for nature. At the global level, debates about climate change raise questions of climate justice: how to share the burden of reducing emissions between rich countries that have emitted most in the past and poorer countries that need to develop (Chapter 14).

    Criticisms of environmentalism¶

    • Economic costs. Critics argue that strict environmental regulation slows growth and harms the poor, who most need development; environmentalists reply that environmental damage itself harms the poor most.
    • Anti-human? Deep ecology is criticised for valuing nature over human needs; some critics have called it misanthropic.
    • Authoritarianism. Some fear that the urgency of environmental crisis could justify restrictions on democratic freedoms.
    • Technological optimism. Others argue that innovation will solve environmental problems without radical changes in lifestyle.

    12.4 Anarchism¶

    Anarchism (from the Greek anarchos, without a ruler) is the belief that the state is unnecessary and harmful, and that society can and should be organised on the basis of voluntary cooperation without coercive authority. Anarchists do not favour chaos; they believe that order can arise from free association.

    • Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (1809–1865), the first to call himself an anarchist, wrote in What Is Property? (1840) that "property is theft", attacking property that allows some to live off the labour of others, and proposed mutualism, a society of small producers exchanging through cooperative banks.
    • Mikhail Bakunin (1814–1876) clashed with Marx in the First International, warning that a workers' state would become a new tyranny of a ruling elite.
    • Peter Kropotkin (1842–1921), in Mutual Aid (1902), argued against social Darwinism that cooperation, not only competition, is a factor in evolution, and envisaged anarcho-communism.
    • Individualist anarchism and anarcho-capitalism (associated with Murray Rothbard) reject the state from the opposite direction, favouring private property and markets for all services, including protection.

    Gandhi described himself at times as a philosophical anarchist, valuing a stateless society of self-governing villages as an ideal while accepting that some state was needed in practice (Chapter 13). Criticisms: anarchism is said to be utopian, underestimating human selfishness and the need for coordination and protection against aggression; anarcho-capitalism is criticised for leaving the weak at the mercy of the strong.

    12.5 Multiculturalism and communitarianism¶

    Multiculturalism¶

    Multiculturalism, as a political theory, holds that cultural diversity within a state should be recognised and accommodated, not merely tolerated, and that members of minority cultures may be entitled to special rights or protections. Its leading theorists include:

    • Charles Taylor, whose essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) argued that identity is shaped by recognition from others, and that misrecognition (being seen through a demeaning image) can inflict real harm; equal respect may require recognising distinct identities.
    • Will Kymlicka, who defended group-differentiated rights on liberal grounds (Chapters 7 and 9).
    • Bhikhu Parekh, in Rethinking Multiculturalism (2000), who argued that cultures should engage in an open dialogue in which no single culture, including liberalism, is taken as the standard.

    Criticisms come from several sides. Liberal egalitarians such as Brian Barry argue that it is fairer to apply the same laws to all and that group rights can entrench inequality. Feminists such as Susan Moller Okin, in "Is Multiculturalism Bad for Women?" (1997), argued that defending minority cultures can mean defending practices that subordinate women within them. Others worry that multicultural policies may weaken social cohesion; defenders reply that recognition strengthens minorities' attachment to the wider society.

    Communitarianism¶

    Communitarianism (Sandel, MacIntyre, Walzer, Taylor; Chapter 6) emphasises that individuals are shaped by their communities and that the common good, shared values and social responsibilities deserve more weight than liberal individualism allows. Political communitarians in the 1990s, such as Amitai Etzioni, called for balancing rights with responsibilities and strengthening families, neighbourhoods and civil society. Critics worry that communitarianism can be conservative, suppressing dissent and diversity within communities, and that "community" is often defined by its most powerful members.

    12.6 Fascism¶

    Fascism was a political ideology and movement of the twentieth century, which rose to power in Italy under Benito Mussolini (from 1922) and, in its Nazi form, in Germany under Adolf Hitler (from 1933). It is studied in political theory both as a historical phenomenon and as a warning.

    Main features¶

    Historians and political theorists have identified a cluster of features, though fascist movements differed among themselves:

    • extreme nationalism: the nation (or, in Nazism, a supposed race) is the highest value, and its glory and expansion the supreme goal;
    • rejection of liberal democracy, individualism and rights, and of the Enlightenment ideas of reason and equality;
    • anti-communism combined with hostility to liberal capitalism, while in practice cooperating with business;
    • totalitarianism: the state, controlled by a single party, seeks to control every area of life, including culture, education, youth and the economy;
    • the leader principle: a charismatic leader embodies the will of the nation;
    • glorification of violence, struggle and war;
    • scapegoating of minorities and, in Nazism, a racial ideology of antisemitism.

    Why it is condemned¶

    Fascist regimes destroyed democratic institutions, suppressed all opposition, launched wars of aggression and, in the case of Nazi Germany, carried out the Holocaust, the systematic murder of some six million Jews, together with the murder of Roma, disabled people, political opponents and many others. The defeat of fascism in the Second World War shaped the post-war order, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the commitment of many constitutions to human dignity (Chapter 14). Political theorists such as Hannah Arendt (The Origins of Totalitarianism, 1951) studied how such regimes arose and how they used terror and ideology to atomise and dominate whole societies.

    Caution: using the word 'fascist' carefully

    "Fascist" is often used loosely as a term of abuse for any opponent. In political theory the term refers to a specific historical ideology with the features listed above. Analysing whether a movement shares some of those features is a legitimate scholarly question, but it requires evidence and care, not labels.

    12.7 Postcolonialism¶

    Postcolonial theory studies the cultural, political and intellectual legacies of colonialism, and criticises the ways in which Western knowledge represented colonised peoples.

    • Frantz Fanon (1925–1961), a psychiatrist from Martinique who joined the Algerian struggle for independence, analysed in Black Skin, White Masks (1952) and The Wretched of the Earth (1961) the psychological damage of colonial racism and the dynamics of decolonisation. He also warned that a new national elite might simply replace the colonisers.
    • Edward Said (1935–2003), in Orientalism (1978), argued that Western scholarship, literature and art constructed an image of "the Orient" as exotic, backward, irrational and unchanging, in contrast to a rational, progressive West, and that this knowledge was bound up with colonial power.
    • The Subaltern Studies group of historians, founded in the early 1980s by Ranajit Guha and others, set out to write the history of colonial India from the perspective of peasants, workers and other subordinated groups, rather than of colonial rulers or nationalist elites. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) asked whether intellectuals can represent the voices of the most marginalised without silencing them.
    • Partha Chatterjee, in Nationalist Thought and the Colonial World (1986), examined how Indian nationalism both challenged and borrowed from the categories of colonial thought.

    Postcolonial thought has influenced political theory by questioning the universality of concepts developed in Europe, drawing attention to the colonial context of liberal thinkers, and encouraging comparative political theory. Critics argue that it can be obscure, that it sometimes treats "the West" as monolithic, and that it may neglect the agency of colonised peoples, or the role of local hierarchies, in favour of an exclusive focus on colonial discourse.

    Worked example 12.1: One issue, several ideologies

    Issue. A large dam is proposed that would generate electricity and irrigate farmland, but flood forests and displace several thousand villagers, many of them from tribal communities.

    How each ideology might frame it. A neo-liberal would ask whether the project is economically efficient and whether the displaced are properly compensated through market-based valuation. A social democrat would support development that reduces poverty, with fair compensation and rehabilitation. A shallow ecologist would weigh environmental costs against human benefits and look for less damaging alternatives. A deep ecologist would emphasise the intrinsic value of the forest and its species. An environmental justice perspective would ask why the costs fall on poor and tribal communities while benefits go elsewhere. A feminist would ask how displacement affects women's work, land rights and security. A multiculturalist would ask whether the project respects the distinct way of life and land rights of tribal communities. An anarchist would ask why the decision is made by the state rather than by the affected communities themselves.

    Summary

    • Feminism holds that gender relations are political and unjust; key concepts are patriarchy, sex and gender, and the public–private divide. Its waves run from Wollstonecraft and suffrage to second-wave, third-wave and fourth-wave feminism, and its varieties include liberal, socialist, radical, intersectional (Crenshaw) and postcolonial (Mohanty) feminism. Indian feminism includes Savitribai Phule, Tarabai Shinde and Pandita Ramabai.
    • Environmentalism stresses ecology, limits to growth and sustainability (Brundtland, 1987). Naess distinguished anthropocentric shallow ecology from ecocentric deep ecology; varieties include reformism, eco-socialism, eco-feminism and social ecology; Chipko exemplifies the environmentalism of the poor.
    • Anarchism (Proudhon, Bakunin, Kropotkin) rejects the state in favour of voluntary cooperation.
    • Multiculturalism (Taylor, Kymlicka, Parekh) seeks recognition and accommodation of cultural diversity; critics include Barry and Okin. Communitarianism stresses community and responsibilities.
    • Fascism was a twentieth-century ideology of extreme nationalism, totalitarianism, the leader principle and violence; Nazism added racial antisemitism and carried out the Holocaust.
    • Postcolonialism (Fanon, Said, Subaltern Studies, Spivak, Chatterjee) examines colonial legacies and questions the universality of Western categories.

    12.8 Exercises¶

    Exercise 1

    Explain the distinction between sex and gender and why it matters to feminists.

    Answer

    Sex refers to biological differences; gender to the social roles, behaviours and expectations attached to being a man or woman. The distinction matters because it shows that many differences between men's and women's lives are socially created and changeable, not fixed by nature, so they can be criticised as unjust and reformed.

    Exercise 2

    What does "the personal is political" mean? Give an example.

    Answer

    It means that relations in private life, especially in the family and sexuality, are shaped by power and are proper subjects of political debate and action. Example: the division of unpaid housework and childcare, often regarded as a private matter, affects women's opportunities in paid work and politics, so it is a question of justice.

    Exercise 3

    Distinguish liberal, radical and intersectional feminism.

    Answer

    Liberal feminism attributes women's disadvantage to unequal rights and opportunities and seeks equal legal and political rights. Radical feminism sees patriarchy as a pervasive system of male domination present in all areas of life, including the family and sexuality, requiring deep social transformation. Intersectional feminism (Crenshaw) holds that gender oppression intersects with race, class, caste and other forms of oppression, so that women's experiences differ and must be understood in their specific combinations.

    Exercise 4

    Compare shallow and deep ecology on (a) the value of nature and (b) attitudes to economic growth.

    Answer

    (a) Shallow ecology is anthropocentric: nature is valuable for its use to humans. Deep ecology is ecocentric: nature has intrinsic value. (b) Shallow ecology believes sustainable or green growth is possible through reform; deep ecology is sceptical of growth and favours changes in values and reduced consumption.

    Exercise 5

    What is environmental justice? Illustrate with the Chipko movement.

    Answer

    Environmental justice concerns the fair distribution of environmental harms and benefits; it notes that the poor and marginalised often bear the costs of pollution and resource extraction while others gain the benefits. In Chipko (from 1973), villagers in the Himalayan region, many of them women, embraced trees to stop commercial felling that threatened their forests and livelihoods: an "environmentalism of the poor" in which protecting nature meant protecting livelihoods.

    Exercise 6

    Summarise Okin's criticism of multiculturalism and one reply a multiculturalist might give.

    Answer

    Okin argued that group rights for minority cultures can protect practices that subordinate women within those cultures, since many cultures are patriarchal and the group's leaders are usually men. Reply: multiculturalists such as Kymlicka support external protections but not internal restrictions that limit members' basic liberties, so a liberal multiculturalism can protect women's rights while accommodating culture.

    Exercise 7

    What was Said's argument in Orientalism?

    Answer

    Said argued that Western scholarship, literature and art constructed "the Orient" as exotic, backward, irrational and unchanging, in contrast to a rational, progressive West, and that this body of knowledge was bound up with colonial power, helping to justify and administer domination.
