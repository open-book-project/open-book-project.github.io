    Chapter 8: Democracy¶

    Rule by the people: its meaning, its models, its forms and its critics

    Learning outcomes

    When you have finished this chapter you should be able to:

    • explain the meaning of democracy and the main arguments for and against it, from Plato to the present;
    • compare direct and representative democracy, and the main theories of representation;
    • describe the main models of democracy: protective, developmental, elitist, pluralist, participatory and deliberative;
    • explain the danger of the tyranny of the majority and the idea of liberal constitutional democracy;
    • compare majoritarian and consensus forms of democracy, and the main electoral systems;
    • analyse a voting cycle (Condorcet's paradox) and explain what it shows about majority rule.

    8.1 What is democracy?¶

    The word democracy comes from the Greek dēmos (the people) and kratos (power or rule): rule by the people. The most famous modern definition is Abraham Lincoln's, from his Gettysburg Address of 1863: government "of the people, by the people, for the people". Each phrase points to a different element: the people are the source of authority, they take part in governing, and government serves their interests.

    Today nearly every government claims to be democratic, which shows the prestige of the word but also how elastic it has become. For most of history the word was a term of abuse. Plato and Aristotle regarded democracy as rule by the ignorant poor; the framers of the American Constitution spoke of a republic rather than a democracy; and many nineteenth-century liberals feared that universal suffrage would threaten property and liberty. Only in the twentieth century, after long struggles by workers, women, colonised peoples and racial minorities, did universal adult suffrage become the norm.

    Two broad understandings of democracy can be distinguished.

    • Procedural (or minimal) democracy defines democracy by its procedures: free and fair elections, universal suffrage, competing parties, freedom of speech and association, and peaceful transfer of power.
    • Substantive democracy adds conditions about outcomes or social conditions: protection of rights, social and economic equality, real participation, and government that actually serves the people's interests.

    Dahl's criteria¶

    Robert Dahl, in On Democracy (1998), listed five criteria that an ideal democratic process would meet among the members of an association:

    1. effective participation: all members have equal and effective opportunities to make their views known;
    2. voting equality: every member has an equal and effective opportunity to vote, and all votes count as equal;
    3. enlightened understanding: each member has opportunities to learn about the alternatives and their likely consequences;
    4. control of the agenda: members decide which matters are placed on the agenda;
    5. inclusion of adults: all, or most, adult permanent residents have full rights of citizens.

    No real state meets these perfectly. Dahl used the term polyarchy for the actual regimes that approximate them, characterised by elected officials, free and fair elections, inclusive suffrage, the right to run for office, freedom of expression, alternative sources of information and freedom of association.

    8.2 Arguments for and against democracy¶

    Arguments for¶

    • Equality. Democracy expresses the equal worth of citizens: each counts for one in deciding the laws all must obey.
    • Freedom and self-government. Citizens who make their own laws are, in a sense, free (positive liberty; Chapter 4). Democracy also protects negative liberty, since rulers who can be removed have reason to respect citizens' rights.
    • Protection of interests. Each person is usually the best judge and guardian of his or her own interests. Those excluded from power tend to have their interests neglected, as J. S. Mill argued about the working class and women.
    • Peaceful change. Democracy allows governments to be removed without bloodshed. Karl Popper emphasised this as its central merit.
    • Better decisions. The "wisdom of crowds" argument holds that the judgements of many people, pooled together, are often better than those of a few. The Condorcet jury theorem (1785) shows mathematically that if each voter is more likely than not to be right on a yes-or-no question, and votes independently, the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty as the number of voters grows.
    • Development of citizens. Taking part in government educates citizens and widens their sympathies (Mill, Rousseau, participatory democrats).

    Arguments against¶

    • Incompetence. Plato compared the state to a ship: we would not choose the captain by a vote of the passengers, so why choose rulers that way? Governing well requires knowledge most citizens lack. A modern version, sometimes called epistocracy, proposes giving more weight to better-informed voters.
    • Tyranny of the majority. A majority may oppress minorities (section 8.5).
    • Instability and demagogy. Ancient critics feared that democracy leads to rule by flatterers and eventually to tyranny.
    • Elite rule in disguise. Elite theorists argued that every society is in fact ruled by a minority. Gaetano Mosca held that there is always a ruling class; Vilfredo Pareto spoke of the circulation of elites; Robert Michels, studying socialist parties, formulated an iron law of oligarchy (1911): any large organisation, however democratic in principle, comes to be controlled by a small leadership.
    • Marxist critique. In a capitalist society political equality coexists with economic inequality, so that democracy is formal and the wealthy dominate in practice.

    8.3 Direct and representative democracy¶

    Direct democracy¶

    In direct democracy citizens make decisions themselves. Athens in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE is the classic example: the assembly (ekklēsia) of adult male citizens met frequently to decide on laws, war, finance and the punishment of officials; many offices were filled by lot rather than election, on the view that selection by lot treats citizens equally. Modern forms include referendums, citizens' initiatives (in which a set number of citizens can force a vote on a proposal), recall of officials and local assemblies. In India the gram sabha, the assembly of all registered voters of a village panchayat area, gives a role for direct participation in local government.

    Representative democracy¶

    In representative democracy citizens elect representatives who make decisions on their behalf. It is the only practical form for large states, but its advocates also defended it on its own merits. James Madison, in Federalist No. 10 (1787), argued that representation would refine and enlarge public views by passing them through a chosen body of citizens. Rousseau, by contrast, held that sovereignty cannot be represented, and that the English were free only during elections and enslaved between them.

    Theories of representation¶

    What does it mean for one person to represent others?

    • Trustee model. The representative uses his or her own judgement on behalf of constituents. Edmund Burke, in his speech to the electors of Bristol (1774), argued that a representative owes the people his judgement, and betrays them if he sacrifices it to their opinion.
    • Delegate model. The representative follows the instructions or wishes of constituents.
    • Mandate model. A party that wins an election has a mandate to carry out the programme it presented to voters.
    • Descriptive (or mirror) representation. A representative body should resemble the population in its composition, by sex, caste, religion, region or class. This is one argument for reserved seats for women or for disadvantaged groups. Critics argue that resembling a group does not guarantee acting in its interests; defenders reply that some experiences are best understood by those who share them, and that presence matters symbolically.

    The political theorist Hanna Pitkin, in The Concept of Representation (1967), distinguished formal, descriptive, symbolic and substantive representation, and argued that the core idea is acting in the interest of the represented, in a manner responsive to them.

    8.4 Models of democracy¶

    Political theorists, notably David Held in Models of Democracy (1987), have identified several models that differ in why they value democracy and how much participation they expect.

    Model Key thinkers What democracy is for Role of citizens
    Classical (direct) Athens; Rousseau Collective self-government Active participation in decisions
    Protective Bentham, James Mill Protecting citizens against rulers and from each other Vote to hold rulers accountable
    Developmental J. S. Mill Developing citizens' capacities Participate and deliberate, with weight for the educated in Mill's own proposal
    Competitive elitist Schumpeter Choosing between competing leaders Vote, then leave governing to leaders
    Pluralist Dahl Balancing the interests of many groups Join groups that compete for influence
    Participatory Pateman, Macpherson Extending self-government to workplaces and communities Active participation in many spheres
    Deliberative Habermas, Joshua Cohen Reaching decisions through public reasoning Exchange reasons in public debate

    Table 8.1. Models of democracy.

    Protective democracy¶

    Jeremy Bentham and James Mill defended representative democracy on utilitarian grounds: rulers, like everyone else, pursue their own interests, so they must be made accountable to the governed through regular elections, a free press and the secret ballot, or they will use power for themselves.

    Developmental democracy¶

    J. S. Mill, in Considerations on Representative Government (1861), valued democracy not only as protection but as a school of citizenship that develops people's intellectual and moral capacities. He supported extending the vote, including to women, and proportional representation to give minorities a voice. But he also proposed plural voting (extra votes for the educated), fearing the rule of an ignorant majority, a proposal few democrats now accept.

    Competitive elitism¶

    The economist Joseph Schumpeter, in Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy (1942), rejected what he called the classical doctrine, which assumed a "common good" and a "will of the people" that democracy expresses. Ordinary citizens, he argued, know and care little about most political issues. Democracy is better understood as a method: an institutional arrangement for arriving at political decisions in which individuals acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for the people's vote. The voters' role is to choose and remove governments, not to govern. Critics argue that this reduces democracy to choosing masters, and that citizens' apathy may be a product of their exclusion rather than a fixed fact.

    Pluralism¶

    Pluralists such as Dahl saw democracy as a process in which many organised groups (business, labour, farmers, professions, religious and ethnic groups) compete and bargain, so that power is dispersed and no single group dominates. Critics, including Dahl himself in later work, noted that groups are unequal in resources and that business enjoys a privileged position (Chapter 2).

    Participatory democracy¶

    Carole Pateman (Participation and Democratic Theory, 1970) and C. B. Macpherson argued that democracy should extend beyond voting to active participation in decisions in the workplace, the neighbourhood and the community. Participation educates people and builds the confidence and skills that democracy needs. Examples include participatory budgeting, first developed in the Brazilian city of Porto Alegre in 1989, in which residents help decide how part of the municipal budget is spent.

    Deliberative democracy¶

    Deliberative democracy, developed from the 1980s by Jürgen Habermas, Joshua Cohen, Amy Gutmann, Dennis Thompson and others, holds that democratic legitimacy comes not simply from counting votes but from public reasoning: free and equal citizens exchanging arguments, listening to one another and seeking decisions that all could accept. Its supporters advocate institutions such as citizens' assemblies and deliberative polls, in which randomly selected citizens learn about an issue, deliberate and make recommendations. Ireland used a citizens' assembly in 2016–2018 to discuss questions including abortion before referendums were held. Critics argue that deliberation favours the articulate and educated, may silence those who express themselves differently, and is hard to scale up to whole societies.

    Key idea

    Democracy means rule by the people, but theories differ about what that requires: choosing leaders (Schumpeter), balancing group interests (Dahl), taking part directly (Pateman) or reasoning together in public (Habermas).

    8.5 Majority rule and its limits¶

    The tyranny of the majority¶

    The French writer Alexis de Tocqueville, in Democracy in America (1835 and 1840), warned of the tyranny of the majority: in a democracy the majority's opinion can become so dominant that it crushes dissent, not only through law but through social pressure. J. S. Mill took up the warning in On Liberty (Chapter 4). The danger is greatest where a society is divided into permanent groups, by religion, language, caste or ethnicity, so that a minority can never become part of a majority.

    Liberal constitutional democracy¶

    The common response is liberal democracy (or constitutional democracy): majority rule within limits set by a constitution that protects individual rights and minority interests, separates powers, provides for judicial review and can be changed only by special procedures. Some decisions, on this view, are taken off the table of ordinary majority politics.

    Critics of strong constitutional limits, such as the legal theorist Jeremy Waldron, argue that when citizens disagree in good faith about rights, it is more democratic to let elected legislatures decide than to give final authority to unelected judges. Defenders reply that democracy itself requires certain rights (free speech, the vote, equal citizenship), which courts protect, and that minorities need protection against majorities that might otherwise violate them.

    Condorcet's paradox¶

    A deeper puzzle about majority rule was discovered by the Marquis de Condorcet in 1785: majority preferences may be intransitive, so that there is no option a majority prefers to every other.

    Voter group (one-third of voters each) First choice Second choice Third choice
    Group 1 A B C
    Group 2 B C A
    Group 3 C A B

    Table 8.2. Preferences that produce a voting cycle.

    Worked example 8.1: A voting cycle

    Situation. A town council must choose one of three projects: A (a library), B (a park) or C (a road). Its members fall into three equal groups with the preferences in Table 8.2, and it votes on pairs of options.

    Analysis. Compare each pair by majority vote.

    • A against B: Groups 1 and 3 prefer A to B, so A beats B by two-thirds.
    • B against C: Groups 1 and 2 prefer B to C, so B beats C.
    • C against A: Groups 2 and 3 prefer C to A, so C beats A.

    The majority prefers A to B, B to C and C to A: a cycle. There is no option that beats all others (no Condorcet winner). The final outcome depends on the order in which pairs are voted on. If the council first votes A against B and then the winner against C, C wins; if it first votes B against C and then the winner against A, A wins. Whoever controls the agenda can determine the result.

    In 1951 the economist Kenneth Arrow proved a much more general result, Arrow's impossibility theorem: when there are at least three options, no method of turning individual rankings into a social ranking can satisfy a small set of reasonable conditions at once (including that the method works for any preferences, respects unanimous preferences, makes the ranking of two options depend only on how voters rank those two, and is not a dictatorship). Some theorists, such as William Riker in Liberalism against Populism (1982), concluded that there is no meaningful "will of the people" for democracy to express, and that democracy is valuable chiefly as a way of removing rulers. Others reply that cycles are less common in practice than in theory, that deliberation can shape preferences so that cycles disappear, and that the results show only that democratic procedures must be designed with care.

    8.6 Forms of democratic government¶

    Parliamentary and presidential systems¶

    Feature Parliamentary system Presidential system
    Executive Prime minister and cabinet, drawn from and responsible to the legislature President, separately elected for a fixed term
    Head of state Separate from the head of government (monarch or president) The president is both
    Removal Government falls if it loses the confidence of the legislature President can be removed only by impeachment for serious misconduct
    Relation of powers Fusion of executive and legislature Separation of powers
    Examples United Kingdom, India, Japan United States, Brazil, Mexico

    Table 8.3. Parliamentary and presidential systems.

    Defenders of parliamentary systems point to flexibility (an unpopular government can be replaced without waiting for an election) and to the absence of deadlock between executive and legislature. Defenders of presidential systems point to the stability of a fixed term, a clear choice of executive by voters and checks between separate branches. The political scientist Juan Linz argued that presidentialism carries risks of deadlock and winner-take-all politics; others dispute how far this is borne out.

    Majoritarian and consensus democracy¶

    Arend Lijphart, in Patterns of Democracy (1999), contrasted two ideal types. Majoritarian democracy concentrates power in the hands of the majority: single-party governments, a dominant executive, two main parties, first-past-the-post elections and a unitary, centralised state (the "Westminster model"). Consensus democracy disperses power: coalition governments, a balance between executive and legislature, multiple parties, proportional representation, federalism and strong judicial review. Lijphart argued that consensus democracy works better in deeply divided societies, where he recommended consociational arrangements such as power-sharing among groups.

    Electoral systems¶

    • First-past-the-post (plurality): the candidate with the most votes in each constituency wins. It is simple, produces clear constituency representatives and often single-party governments, but can give a party a majority of seats with a minority of votes and under-represent smaller parties. It is used for elections to the Lok Sabha.
    • Proportional representation: seats are allocated in proportion to votes, often through party lists. It is fairer to smaller parties and minorities but usually produces coalition governments and may weaken the link between a representative and a locality.
    • Mixed systems combine the two, as in Germany and New Zealand.
    • Preferential systems let voters rank candidates; the single transferable vote is used, for example, in the election of the President of India by an electoral college.

    Worked example 8.2: Votes and seats

    Situation. Three parties contest 10 single-member constituencies of equal size. In every constituency Party X wins 40 per cent of the vote, Party Y 35 per cent and Party Z 25 per cent.

    Analysis. Under first-past-the-post, X wins all 10 seats with 40 per cent of the vote; Y and Z win none. Under proportional representation with these national totals, X would win 4 seats, Y about 3 or 4 and Z 2 or 3, depending on the counting formula. The example is extreme (real votes vary between constituencies), but it shows how first-past-the-post can magnify the largest party's lead, while proportional systems mirror the distribution of votes.

    8.7 Democracy and its present challenges¶

    Political theorists today discuss several challenges to democratic systems, which this book states in general terms.

    • Participation. Declining party membership and turnout in some democracies raise questions about how engaged citizens are.
    • Money and power. Large inequalities of wealth may translate into unequal political influence, through campaign finance, lobbying and media ownership.
    • Information. The spread of misinformation and the fragmentation of public debate online may weaken the shared basis of facts that deliberation needs.
    • Populism. Political scientists use the term for movements that present politics as a struggle between a virtuous "people" and a corrupt "elite", and claim to speak for the people as a whole. Scholars disagree on whether such movements threaten liberal democracy by attacking checks and balances, or express legitimate grievances that established parties neglected.
    • Democratic backsliding. Researchers have studied cases in which elected governments gradually weaken courts, the press and electoral bodies while keeping elections.

    Summary

    • Democracy is rule by the people; procedural accounts focus on free elections and rights, substantive accounts add equality and real participation. Dahl's criteria define an ideal; real regimes are polyarchies.
    • Arguments for democracy rest on equality, freedom, protection of interests, peaceful change and better decisions; criticisms come from Plato, elite theorists (Mosca, Pareto, Michels) and Marxists.
    • Direct democracy (Athens, referendums, gram sabhas) and representative democracy coexist; representatives may act as trustees (Burke), delegates, mandate-holders or descriptive mirrors.
    • Models include protective, developmental, competitive elitist (Schumpeter), pluralist (Dahl), participatory (Pateman) and deliberative (Habermas) democracy.
    • Tocqueville and Mill warned of the tyranny of the majority; liberal constitutional democracy limits majorities. Condorcet's paradox and Arrow's theorem show that majority preferences can cycle and agenda control matters.
    • Democracies may be parliamentary or presidential, majoritarian or consensus (Lijphart), and use plurality, proportional, mixed or preferential electoral systems.

    8.8 Exercises¶

    Exercise 1

    Distinguish procedural from substantive democracy. Could a country be democratic in one sense but not the other?

    Answer

    Procedural democracy is defined by procedures: free and fair elections, universal suffrage, competing parties, civil liberties. Substantive democracy also requires outcomes or conditions such as protection of rights, real equality and government that serves people's interests. Yes: a country with regular free elections but deep social exclusion of some groups may be procedurally democratic but substantively weak.

    Exercise 2

    Explain Schumpeter's definition of democracy and one criticism of it.

    Answer

    Schumpeter defined democracy as a method in which individuals acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for the people's vote; citizens choose and remove leaders but do not govern. Criticism: it reduces democracy to choosing between elites and treats citizen apathy as natural, when it may result from exclusion; participatory democrats argue that democracy should involve citizens much more actively.

    Exercise 3

    Contrast Burke's trustee model of representation with the delegate model. Which better describes a representative who votes against a popular measure because she thinks it unwise?

    Answer

    A trustee uses independent judgement for constituents' good; a delegate follows their instructions or wishes. A representative who votes against a popular measure because she judges it unwise acts as a trustee, as Burke recommended in his speech to the electors of Bristol (1774).

    Exercise 4

    Five voters rank three options: two voters rank A > B > C, two rank B > C > A, and one ranks C > A > B. Is there a Condorcet winner?

    Answer

    Compare pairs. A vs B: A is preferred by 2 + 1 = 3 voters, B by 2: A beats B. B vs C: B by 2 + 2 = 4, C by 1: B beats C. A vs C: A by 2, C by 2 + 1 = 3: C beats A. The majority prefers A to B, B to C and C to A, a cycle: there is no Condorcet winner.

    Exercise 5

    What is deliberative democracy, and what is one practical institution its supporters recommend? Give one criticism.

    Answer

    Deliberative democracy holds that legitimacy comes from public reasoning among free and equal citizens seeking decisions all could accept, not just from counting votes. Supporters recommend citizens' assemblies or deliberative polls of randomly selected citizens. Criticism: deliberation may favour the educated and articulate, and is hard to extend to whole societies.

    Exercise 6

    Give two advantages and two disadvantages of first-past-the-post compared with proportional representation.

    Answer

    Advantages: (1) simplicity and a clear link between a representative and a constituency; (2) it often produces single-party governments that are stable and clearly accountable. Disadvantages: (1) disproportionality: a party can win a majority of seats with a minority of votes; (2) smaller parties and dispersed minorities are under-represented, and many votes are wasted.

    Exercise 7

    What is the tyranny of the majority, and how does liberal constitutional democracy respond to it?

    Answer

    The tyranny of the majority (Tocqueville, Mill) is the oppression of minorities or dissenters by a majority, through law or through social pressure. Liberal constitutional democracy responds by limiting majority rule: a constitution entrenches rights, separates powers, provides for judicial review and requires special procedures for amendment, so that some matters are protected from ordinary majorities.
