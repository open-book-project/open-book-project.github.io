    Chapter 13: Elections and Political Parties¶

    The franchise, electoral systems, the conduct of elections, parties and electoral reform

    Learning outcomes

    When you have finished this chapter you should be able to:

    • state the constitutional provisions on elections (Part XV) and the two Representation of the People Acts;
    • explain universal adult franchise and the system of electoral rolls;
    • compare the first-past-the-post system with proportional representation by single transferable vote, and work simple examples of each;
    • explain delimitation and reserved constituencies;
    • describe the stages of an election, the Model Code of Conduct, voting machines and NOTA, and how election disputes are settled;
    • explain the role of political parties, how they are registered and recognised, and how the party system has changed;
    • discuss the main electoral reforms and the issues still debated.

    13.1 Elections in a democracy¶

    In a representative democracy, elections are the means by which the people choose their representatives and hold them to account. An election is democratic only if certain conditions are met: every adult can vote, every vote counts equally, voters can choose freely among real alternatives, the poll is secret, the count is honest, and those who lose accept the result. The Indian Constitution tries to secure these conditions through Part XV (Articles 324 to 329) and through laws made by Parliament.

    Article Provision
    324 Superintendence, direction and control of elections vested in the Election Commission
    325 One general electoral roll for every constituency; no one excluded on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex
    326 Elections to the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage
    327 Parliament may make laws on elections to Parliament and State legislatures
    328 State legislatures may make such laws for their own elections, so far as Parliament has not done so
    329 Courts may not question laws on delimitation or the allotment of seats; elections may be questioned only by an election petition

    Table 13.1. Part XV of the Constitution.

    The main laws are the Representation of the People Act, 1950, which deals with the allocation of seats, delimitation, the qualifications of voters and the preparation of electoral rolls, and the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which deals with the conduct of elections, the qualifications and disqualifications of members, corrupt practices and election disputes.

    Article 325 deserves a special note. Under British rule, Muslims and some other groups had separate electorates: they voted only for candidates of their own community in seats reserved for it. The Constitution rejected this. There is one general roll for each constituency, and even in constituencies reserved for Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes, all voters vote together.

    13.2 The franchise and the electoral roll¶

    Universal adult franchise¶

    Article 326 gives the vote to every citizen who is at least 18 years old on the qualifying date fixed by law and is not otherwise disqualified (for example, for non-residence, unsoundness of mind, or crime or corrupt practices). The voting age was 21 until the 61st Amendment (1988) lowered it to 18. There are no qualifications of property, income, education or sex.

    The first general elections, held between October 1951 and February 1952 under the first Chief Election Commissioner, Sukumar Sen, were then the largest elections ever held. Most voters could not read, so each party was identified by a symbol, a practice that continues.

    Electoral rolls¶

    A citizen can vote only if his or her name is on the electoral roll of a constituency in which he or she is ordinarily resident. The rolls are prepared and revised by the Election Commission through Electoral Registration Officers, usually with an annual summary revision and continuous updating. Since 2022, the law has provided four qualifying dates in a year (1 January, 1 April, 1 July and 1 October), so that young people can register soon after turning eighteen. Voters are issued an Electors Photo Identity Card.

    Common confusion

    Article 326 guarantees adult suffrage, but the right to vote in India has been described by the Supreme Court as a statutory right, conferred and regulated by the Representation of the People Acts, rather than a Fundamental Right. The freedom of voting (the choice of whom to vote for, or not to vote for anyone) is, however, protected as part of freedom of expression under Article 19(1)(a).

    13.3 Electoral systems¶

    An electoral system is the method by which votes are turned into seats. India uses two.

    First past the post¶

    Elections to the Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assemblies use the first-past-the-post (FPTP) system, also called the simple plurality system. The country (or the State) is divided into single-member constituencies. Each voter has one vote. The candidate who receives more votes than any other candidate wins, even if he or she does not have a majority of the votes cast.

    The framers chose FPTP for its simplicity, which mattered in a country where most voters were voting for the first time, and because it tends to produce stable governments with a clear link between each constituency and its representative. Its main drawback is that the share of seats a party wins can differ greatly from its share of votes.

    Worked example 13.1: Votes and seats under FPTP

    Situation. A small State has three constituencies of 100,000 voters each, all of whom vote. Three parties, A, B and C, contest every seat. The results are:

    • Constituency 1: A 40,000; B 35,000; C 25,000.
    • Constituency 2: A 38,000; B 37,000; C 25,000.
    • Constituency 3: A 20,000; B 50,000; C 30,000.

    Who wins each seat, and how do vote shares compare with seat shares?

    Answer. A wins Constituencies 1 and 2 (with 40 and 38 per cent), and B wins Constituency 3. Overall votes: A \(= 98{,}000\) (32.7 per cent), B \(= 122{,}000\) (40.7 per cent), C \(= 80{,}000\) (26.7 per cent). Seats: A 2 (66.7 per cent), B 1 (33.3 per cent), C 0. Party B won the most votes but fewer seats than A, and C won more than a quarter of the votes but no seat at all. This is the typical distortion of FPTP: what matters is where votes are cast, not just how many.

    Proportional representation by single transferable vote¶

    Indirect elections use proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (STV). It is used for the elections of the President and the Vice-President (Chapter 5), of members of the Rajya Sabha by the State Legislative Assemblies, and of members of the State Legislative Councils.

    Where several seats are to be filled, as in a Rajya Sabha election, each voter ranks the candidates in order of preference. A candidate is elected on reaching a quota, calculated as

    \[ \text{quota} = \left\lfloor \frac{\text{number of valid votes}}{\text{number of seats} + 1} \right\rfloor + 1 . \]

    Votes in excess of the quota (the surplus) are transferred to the voters' next preferences, and if seats remain unfilled, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and his or her votes are transferred, until all seats are filled. (In practice, each vote is given a value of 100 to allow fractional transfers.)

    Worked example 13.2: A Rajya Sabha election

    Situation. A State Legislative Assembly has 120 elected members, all of whom vote. Four seats in the Rajya Sabha are to be filled. Party P has 70 members and Party Q has 50. Assuming every member votes for his or her own party's candidates, how many seats will each party win?

    Answer. Quota \(= \lfloor 120 / (4 + 1) \rfloor + 1 = 24 + 1 = 25\) votes. Party P, with 70 votes, can fill two quotas (\(2 \times 25 = 50\)) with 20 votes to spare; Party Q, with 50 votes, can fill exactly two quotas. So P wins 2 seats and Q wins 2, even though P has a clear majority in the Assembly. P's remaining 20 votes are not enough for a third quota. (Under FPTP-style voting for each seat, P would have won all four.) The STV system gives a minority party in the Assembly representation roughly proportional to its strength.

    13.4 Delimitation and reserved constituencies¶

    Delimitation¶

    Delimitation means drawing the boundaries of constituencies. Under Article 82 (and Article 170 for the States), after each census the allocation of seats and the division into constituencies are to be readjusted by an authority set up by Parliament. Parliament has set up Delimitation Commissions under laws of 1952, 1962, 1972 and 2002. A Delimitation Commission is an independent body, usually chaired by a retired Supreme Court judge, with the Chief Election Commissioner (or an Election Commissioner) and the State Election Commissioners as members. Its orders have the force of law and cannot be questioned in any court (Article 329(a)).

    As Chapter 6 explained, the number of seats allotted to each State has been frozen on the basis of the 1971 census until the first census after 2026. The last Commission, constituted under the 2002 law, redrew constituency boundaries within each State on the basis of the 2001 census without changing the number of seats per State. A new delimitation after the next census will affect both the allocation of seats among States and the coming into force of the reservation of seats for women under the 106th Amendment. It is expected to be a significant political question, since States differ greatly in population growth.

    Reserved constituencies¶

    Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha and the State Assemblies in proportion to their population (Articles 330 and 332), until 2030 (104th Amendment). The Delimitation Commission decides which constituencies are reserved, choosing for Scheduled Tribes those where their share of the population is highest, and for Scheduled Castes, constituencies spread across the State where their share is relatively high. In a reserved constituency only members of the group can stand as candidates, but all registered voters vote. There is no reservation of seats for any religious community.

    13.5 How an election is conducted¶

    The stages of an election¶

    1. Announcement. The Election Commission announces the schedule. The Model Code of Conduct comes into force at once.
    2. Notification. The President (for the Lok Sabha) or the Governor (for an Assembly) issues a notification calling on the constituencies to elect members, on the Commission's recommendation.
    3. Nominations. Candidates file nomination papers with the Returning Officer, along with an affidavit disclosing their criminal record (if any), assets and liabilities, and educational qualifications. A candidate must make a security deposit (₹25,000 for the Lok Sabha and ₹10,000 for an Assembly, halved for SC and ST candidates), which is forfeited if he or she polls less than one-sixth of the valid votes.
    4. Scrutiny and withdrawal. The Returning Officer examines the nominations; candidates may withdraw within a fixed period.
    5. Campaign. Parties and candidates campaign, subject to limits on expenditure fixed by rules for each candidate. Campaigning must stop 48 hours before the close of polling.
    6. Polling. Voting takes place by secret ballot on electronic voting machines. Large elections are held in several phases so that security forces can be deployed.
    7. Counting and results. Votes are counted under the supervision of the Returning Officer and observers, and the result is declared.

    The Model Code of Conduct¶

    The Model Code of Conduct is a set of guidelines for parties, candidates and governments during elections. It forbids, among other things, appeals to caste or communal feelings, the use of places of worship for campaigning, and the announcement of new schemes, projects or grants by ministers after elections are announced. It is not a law, but the Election Commission enforces it through warnings, orders to stop campaigning for a period, and transfers of officials; violations that are also offences can be prosecuted.

    Voting machines, VVPAT and NOTA¶

    Electronic voting machines (EVMs) were first used experimentally in a by-election in Kerala in 1982, and have been used in all constituencies since the Lok Sabha election of 2004. A voter-verifiable paper audit trail (VVPAT) unit attached to each machine prints a slip that the voter can see through a window for a few seconds before it drops into a sealed box. VVPAT was introduced following a direction of the Supreme Court in 2013, and the slips from a sample of machines in each constituency are counted and matched with the machine counts. The Supreme Court has declined petitions to return to paper ballots.

    Since People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2013), voting machines have included a "None of the Above" (NOTA) option, which lets a voter reject all candidates while keeping the secrecy of the vote. NOTA votes are counted and announced, but they do not affect the result: the candidate with the most votes wins even if NOTA receives more.

    Election disputes¶

    Under Article 329(b) and the Representation of the People Act, 1951, an election can be challenged only by an election petition filed in the High Court within 45 days of the declaration of the result, by a candidate or an elector, on grounds such as corrupt practices, improper acceptance or rejection of nominations, or non-compliance with the law. An appeal lies to the Supreme Court. Corrupt practices under the Act include bribery, undue influence, appeals on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language, and spending beyond the limit. A person found guilty of a corrupt practice can be disqualified for up to six years. Disputes about the election of the President and the Vice-President go directly to the Supreme Court (Article 71).

    13.6 Political parties¶

    The role of parties¶

    Political parties are not mentioned in the original Constitution; they first appeared in its text with the Tenth Schedule in 1985. Yet parliamentary government cannot work without them. Parties select candidates, present programmes to voters, form governments or act as the opposition, organise legislative business and connect citizens with the State.

    Registration and recognition¶

    An association of citizens can apply to the Election Commission to be registered as a political party under Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951. It must commit itself, in its constitution, to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy, and to uphold the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.

    Registered parties that perform well in elections may be recognised as national or State parties under the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968. Recognition brings advantages: a reserved symbol for the party's candidates throughout the country (national party) or the State (State party), free broadcasting time on public radio and television, copies of electoral rolls, and a larger number of "star campaigners" whose travel costs are not counted in the candidate's expenses.

    A party is recognised as a national party if it fulfils any one of three conditions:

    1. it secures at least 6 per cent of the valid votes in four or more States in a general election to the Lok Sabha or the Assemblies, and in addition wins at least four seats in the Lok Sabha; or
    2. it wins at least 2 per cent of the seats in the Lok Sabha (that is, 11 seats) from at least three States; or
    3. it is recognised as a State party in at least four States.

    A party is recognised as a State party in a State if it meets criteria based on its share of votes and seats in that State at a general election (for example, 6 per cent of the valid votes in the State together with two seats in the Assembly). The Election Commission reviews recognition after general elections, so the list of recognised parties changes over time.

    Worked example 13.3: National party status

    Situation. After a general election, Party R has won 6 per cent or more of the valid votes in five States and three seats in the Lok Sabha. It is recognised as a State party in two States. Is it a national party?

    Answer. Check each condition. (1) It has 6 per cent of the votes in four or more States (five), but it also needs four Lok Sabha seats; it has only three, so it fails. (2) It needs 11 Lok Sabha seats from at least three States; it has three, so it fails. (3) It needs State party recognition in four States; it has two, so it fails. Party R is not a national party, though it remains a recognised State party in two States. Winning one more Lok Sabha seat would satisfy condition (1).

    How the party system has changed¶

    For about two decades after independence, the Indian National Congress won national elections comfortably and governed most States, while opposition parties were numerous but fragmented. Political scientists described this as a one-party dominant system. From 1967, non-Congress governments came to power in several States, and in 1977 a non-Congress government was formed at the Centre for the first time. From 1989 to 2014 no single party won a majority in the Lok Sabha, and India was governed by coalitions, which strengthened the role of regional parties. A single party won a majority in 2014 and again in 2019; in 2024 no party won a majority on its own, and a coalition government was formed. The party system is now best described as a multi-party system in which national and regional parties both play important roles.

    13.7 Electoral reforms¶

    Electoral law has changed considerably since 1950, often under pressure from the courts and the Election Commission. The main reforms and debates include the following.

    • Disclosure by candidates. In Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002) and People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2003), the Supreme Court held that voters have a right to know the criminal antecedents, assets and educational qualifications of candidates, as part of the freedom of expression. Candidates now file affidavits disclosing them. Later judgments have required parties to publicise the criminal records of their candidates and the reasons for choosing them.
    • Disqualification on conviction. In Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013), the Court struck down the provision that let sitting legislators continue in office pending appeal after conviction; a conviction with a sentence of two years or more now disqualifies at once.
    • NOTA and VVPAT, described above.
    • Anti-defection law, strengthened by the 91st Amendment (2003) (Chapter 6).
    • Funding of parties. Party finance has long been considered a weak spot. The electoral bonds scheme, introduced in 2018, allowed anonymous donations to parties through bonds bought from a bank. In Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (February 2024), a five-judge bench unanimously struck down the scheme, holding that it violated voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a). Proposals for State funding of elections have been made by committees such as the Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998), but have not been adopted.
    • Criminalisation of politics. Concern about candidates facing serious criminal charges remains. Proposals include disqualification on the framing of charges (rather than on conviction) and fast-track trials of legislators.
    • Simultaneous elections. Elections to the Lok Sabha and all State Assemblies were held together from 1951–52 until 1967, after which the cycles separated. A high-level committee recommended in 2024 that simultaneous elections be restored, and constitutional amendment bills for this purpose were introduced in Parliament in December 2024 and referred to a joint parliamentary committee. Supporters cite lower costs and less disruption to governance; critics raise concerns about federalism and accountability. Check the current position.

    Key idea

    Much of Indian electoral reform has come from the Supreme Court, applying the voter's right to information and the guarantee of free and fair elections (a part of the basic structure), and from the Election Commission's enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct. Parliament retains the power to legislate on elections, and the debates over party funding, criminalisation and simultaneous elections continue.

    Summary

    • Part XV (Articles 324–329) vests control of elections in the Election Commission, guarantees one general roll per constituency without separate electorates, and adult suffrage at 18 (61st Amendment, 1988). The Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951 supply the details.
    • The Lok Sabha and Assemblies are elected by first past the post in single-member constituencies; the President, Vice-President, Rajya Sabha and Legislative Councils by proportional representation by single transferable vote.
    • Delimitation Commissions draw constituency boundaries; their orders cannot be challenged in court. Seat allocation per State is frozen on the 1971 census until after the first census following 2026. SC and ST seats are reserved until 2030.
    • Elections pass through announcement, notification, nomination, scrutiny, campaign, polling and counting; the Model Code of Conduct applies from announcement; EVMs with VVPAT are used; NOTA is available; disputes go to the High Court by election petition.
    • Parties are registered under Section 29A and recognised as national or State parties under the Symbols Order, 1968. The party system has moved from one-party dominance through coalitions to a competitive multi-party system.
    • Reforms include disclosure by candidates, immediate disqualification on conviction, NOTA, VVPAT and the striking down of electoral bonds; party funding, criminalisation and simultaneous elections remain under debate.

    13.8 Exercises¶

    Exercise 1

    What does Article 325 provide, and what colonial practice did it end?

    Answer

    There shall be one general electoral roll for every territorial constituency, and no person shall be ineligible for inclusion in it on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. It ended the colonial system of separate electorates, under which certain communities voted separately for their own representatives.

    Exercise 2

    In a constituency, the votes are: Candidate X 45,000; Candidate Y 42,000; Candidate Z 33,000; NOTA 5,000. Who wins? Did the winner obtain a majority of votes cast?

    Answer

    X wins, having the most votes. Total votes cast (including NOTA) \(= 125{,}000\); X has \(45{,}000 / 125{,}000 = 36\) per cent, which is not a majority. Under first past the post, a plurality is enough, and NOTA votes do not affect the result.

    Exercise 3

    A State Assembly of 150 members elects three members of the Rajya Sabha. What is the quota? If Party M has 95 members and Party N has 55, and all vote on party lines, how many seats does each win?

    Answer

    Quota \(= \lfloor 150 / (3 + 1) \rfloor + 1 = 37 + 1 =\) 38. Party M's 95 votes fill two quotas (\(2 \times 38 = 76\)) with 19 to spare, not enough for a third. Party N's 55 votes fill one quota with 17 to spare. Two M candidates and one N candidate reach the quota, which fills all three seats: M wins 2 and N wins 1.

    Exercise 4

    Why can the orders of a Delimitation Commission not be challenged in court?

    Answer

    Article 329(a) provides that the validity of any law relating to the delimitation of constituencies or the allotment of seats, made under Article 327 or 328, shall not be called in question in any court. The Delimitation Acts give the Commission's orders the force of law, so they enjoy this protection. The purpose is to prevent elections from being held up by litigation over boundaries.

    Exercise 5

    State the three alternative conditions for recognition as a national party.

    Answer

    (1) At least 6 per cent of the valid votes in four or more States in a general election to the Lok Sabha or the Assemblies, plus at least four Lok Sabha seats; or (2) at least 2 per cent of Lok Sabha seats (11 seats) from at least three States; or (3) recognition as a State party in at least four States.

    Exercise 6

    What did the Supreme Court decide about electoral bonds in 2024, and on what ground?

    Answer

    In Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (February 2024), a five-judge bench unanimously struck down the electoral bonds scheme and related amendments, holding that anonymous funding of parties violated the voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a), which is essential to free and fair elections.
