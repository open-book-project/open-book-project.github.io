    Chapter 12: Constitutional and Statutory Bodies¶

    The Election Commission, the CAG, the Public Service Commissions, the Finance Commission and the other watchdogs

    Learning outcomes

    When you have finished this chapter you should be able to:

    • distinguish constitutional, statutory and executive bodies, and explain why some bodies are made independent of the government;
    • describe the composition, appointment, tenure, removal and functions of the Election Commission;
    • explain the role of the Comptroller and Auditor-General and the link with the Public Accounts Committee;
    • describe the Union and State Public Service Commissions;
    • recall the constitution and functions of the Finance Commission and the national commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes;
    • outline the main statutory and executive bodies: NITI Aayog, the National Human Rights Commission, the Central Vigilance Commission, the Central Bureau of Investigation, the Information Commissions and the Lokpal;
    • choose the right body for a given grievance.

    12.1 Why independent bodies?¶

    In a parliamentary democracy, the government of the day controls the executive. But some functions are too important to be left to the government of the day, because the government itself has an interest in how they are performed. Who should conduct elections that decide whether the government stays in power? Who should audit the government's own spending? Who should select civil servants, so that jobs do not go to party supporters? The framers answered: bodies that are independent of the government, protected by the Constitution in the same way as judges.

    Such bodies are of three kinds.

    Kind Created by Examples
    Constitutional bodies The Constitution itself Election Commission, Comptroller and Auditor-General, Union and State Public Service Commissions, Finance Commission, National Commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes, GST Council
    Statutory bodies An Act of Parliament (or a State legislature) National Human Rights Commission, Central Vigilance Commission, Central and State Information Commissions, Lokpal, National Commission for Women
    Executive (non-statutory) bodies A resolution or order of the government NITI Aayog; the Central Bureau of Investigation (which draws its legal powers from a statute)

    Table 12.1. Three kinds of body.

    A constitutional body cannot be abolished, nor its basic features changed, without a constitutional amendment. A statutory body can be changed by ordinary law. An executive body can be reshaped by the government itself.

    Key idea

    The independence of a body depends on the same safeguards that protect judges: a secure term, removal only by a difficult procedure, service conditions that cannot be worsened, expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund, and no prospect of further government office after retirement. When you study a body, check which of these safeguards it has.

    12.2 The Election Commission¶

    Composition and appointment¶

    Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, to the State legislatures and to the offices of President and Vice-President in an Election Commission. The Commission consists of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President fixes. It was a single-member body from 1950 to 1989; after a brief period with additional commissioners in 1989–90, it has been a three-member body since 1993, with decisions taken by majority.

    The commissioners are appointed by the President, subject to any law made by Parliament. For many years there was no such law, and appointments were made on the advice of the government. In Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023), the Supreme Court directed that, until Parliament made a law, appointments should be made on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India. Parliament then enacted the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023, under which appointments are made on the recommendation of a selection committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the leader of the largest opposition party) and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. Commissioners hold office for six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier.

    Independence¶

    • The Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court (Article 324(5)).
    • Other Election Commissioners can be removed only on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner.
    • The conditions of service of the Chief Election Commissioner cannot be varied to his or her disadvantage after appointment.

    Critics have pointed out some gaps: the Constitution does not bar commissioners from later government appointments, and the protection of the other commissioners is weaker than that of the Chief Election Commissioner.

    Functions¶

    The Election Commission:

    • prepares and periodically revises the electoral rolls and registers voters;
    • fixes the schedule of elections and supervises the whole process, from nomination to counting;
    • recognises political parties and allots election symbols, and decides disputes about them;
    • enforces the Model Code of Conduct for parties and candidates;
    • monitors election expenditure;
    • advises the President and the Governors on the disqualification of members of Parliament and of State legislatures (Articles 103 and 192);
    • can cancel polls in the event of rigging, booth capturing or violence.

    Chapter 13 discusses elections in more detail. Elections to panchayats and municipalities are conducted not by the Election Commission but by State Election Commissions (Articles 243K and 243ZA).

    12.3 The Comptroller and Auditor-General¶

    Office and independence¶

    Article 148 provides for a Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (CAG), appointed by the President. Dr Ambedkar regarded the CAG as perhaps the most important officer under the Constitution, since he or she is the guardian of the public purse. The CAG's independence is protected in several ways:

    • the CAG can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court;
    • the term is six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier (under a law of 1971);
    • the CAG's salary and conditions of service are fixed by Parliament and cannot be varied to his or her disadvantage; the salary equals that of a Supreme Court judge;
    • the administrative expenses of the office, including salaries, are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India;
    • after ceasing to hold office, the CAG is not eligible for further office under the Government of India or of any State.

    Functions¶

    The CAG audits all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India and of each State (and of Union Territories having legislatures), the Contingency Funds and the Public Accounts, and the accounts of government companies and other bodies substantially financed by government. The audit covers not only whether money was spent according to law and rules (compliance audit), but also whether it was spent wisely and economically (performance audit).

    The CAG's reports on the accounts of the Union are submitted to the President, who causes them to be laid before Parliament; reports on the accounts of a State are submitted to the Governor, who lays them before the State legislature (Article 151). In Parliament, the Public Accounts Committee examines the reports, with the CAG acting as its guide and adviser.

    Comptroller in name, auditor in fact

    In Britain, the Comptroller and Auditor-General controls the issue of money from the treasury as well as auditing it. In India, money is withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund by the executive, without the CAG's prior approval. The Indian CAG is therefore an auditor, not a comptroller, despite the title; his or her control is exercised after the money has been spent.

    12.4 The Public Service Commissions¶

    The Union Public Service Commission¶

    Part XIV (Articles 315 to 323) provides for a Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) and a Public Service Commission for each State. The UPSC was preceded by a Public Service Commission set up in 1926 and a Federal Public Service Commission under the Government of India Act, 1935.

    • Composition and appointment. A chairman and members appointed by the President. At least half of the members must have held office under the Government of India or of a State for at least ten years.
    • Term. Six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier.
    • Removal. By the President, on the ground of misbehaviour, after the Supreme Court, on a reference, has inquired and advised removal (Article 317). The President may also remove a member who is adjudged insolvent, takes up paid employment outside his or her duties, or is unfit because of infirmity of mind or body.
    • Independence. Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund; conditions of service cannot be varied to a member's disadvantage; the chairman is not eligible for further government employment, and a member may be appointed only as chairman of the UPSC or a State Commission.

    Functions¶

    Under Article 320, the UPSC:

    • conducts examinations for appointment to the All-India Services and the Central services;
    • is consulted on methods of recruitment, principles for appointments, promotions and transfers, disciplinary matters affecting civil servants, and claims for legal expenses and pensions in certain cases.

    The UPSC's advice is not binding on the government. But the government must lay before Parliament the UPSC's annual report, with a memorandum explaining any case in which its advice was not accepted, and the reasons (Article 323). The UPSC is not consulted on reservation of posts for backward classes (Article 320(4)).

    State Public Service Commissions¶

    Each State has a State Public Service Commission, whose chairman and members are appointed by the Governor for six years or until the age of 62. They can, however, be removed only by the President, in the same way as members of the UPSC. Parliament may also create a Joint State Public Service Commission for two or more States at their request.

    12.5 The Finance Commission and the GST Council¶

    The Finance Commission (Article 280) and the GST Council (Article 279A) were described in Chapter 8. In brief:

    • The Finance Commission is constituted by the President every five years, with a chairman and four other members whose qualifications are laid down by a law of 1951. It recommends the share of the States in the divisible pool of Union taxes, its distribution among the States, the principles of grants-in-aid, and measures to supplement the resources of local bodies. Its recommendations are laid before Parliament with an explanatory memorandum; they are advisory, but generally accepted.
    • The GST Council is a permanent body of the Union Finance Minister, a Union Minister of State, and the finance ministers of all States. It recommends GST rates and rules, and its decisions require three-fourths of the weighted votes, with the Union holding one-third and the States two-thirds of the weight.

    12.6 Commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes¶

    The Constitution makes special provision for bodies to protect disadvantaged groups.

    • National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338). Originally a single commission covered both Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The 89th Amendment (2003) divided it into two commissions.
    • National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (Article 338A). Created by the same amendment.
    • National Commission for Backward Classes (Article 338B). A statutory body since 1993, it was given constitutional status by the 102nd Amendment (2018).

    Each commission consists of a chairperson, a vice-chairperson and other members appointed by the President. Each investigates and monitors the safeguards provided for its group, inquires into specific complaints of deprivation of rights and safeguards, participates in and advises on planning for the group's development, and reports to the President, who lays the reports before Parliament. While investigating, the commissions have the powers of a civil court, such as summoning witnesses.

    The Constitution also provides for a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Article 350B, added in 1956), who investigates matters relating to the safeguards for linguistic minorities and reports to the President.

    12.7 Statutory and executive bodies¶

    NITI Aayog¶

    NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) replaced the Planning Commission on 1 January 2015. Like its predecessor, it was created by a resolution of the Union Cabinet, not by the Constitution or a law. The Prime Minister is its chairperson; its Governing Council includes the Chief Ministers of all States and of Union Territories with legislatures, and the Lieutenant Governors of other Union Territories; it has a Vice-Chairperson and a Chief Executive Officer. Unlike the Planning Commission, it does not allocate funds to States; it works as a policy think tank and a forum for co-operative federalism.

    National Human Rights Commission¶

    The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) was set up in 1993 under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. Its chairperson must be a person who has been Chief Justice of India or a judge of the Supreme Court; its members include judges and persons with knowledge of human rights, and the chairpersons of several national commissions are members ex officio. It inquires into complaints of violation of human rights by public servants, can intervene in court proceedings, visits jails, and recommends compensation or prosecution. Its recommendations are not binding, though they usually carry weight. States have State Human Rights Commissions under the same Act.

    Central Vigilance Commission¶

    The Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) was set up in 1964 by an executive resolution, on the recommendation of the Santhanam Committee on prevention of corruption, and was given statutory status by the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. It consists of a Central Vigilance Commissioner and up to two Vigilance Commissioners, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Union Home Minister and the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, for four years or until the age of 65. It supervises vigilance administration in central government organisations and superintends the investigation of certain corruption offences.

    Central Bureau of Investigation¶

    The Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) was set up in 1963 by a resolution of the Union Ministry of Home Affairs. It derives its legal powers from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. It investigates corruption, serious economic offences and other important crimes, often on reference from the government or on the orders of the courts. Because police and public order are State subjects, the CBI generally needs the consent of a State government to investigate in that State, unless a constitutional court orders it.

    Information Commissions¶

    The Right to Information Act, 2005 gives citizens the right to obtain information held by public authorities, subject to listed exemptions, and sets up a Central Information Commission and State Information Commissions to hear appeals and complaints. The Central Information Commission consists of a Chief Information Commissioner and up to ten Information Commissioners, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. The right to information has been described by the courts as part of the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a).

    Lokpal and Lokayuktas¶

    The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 created the Lokpal, an anti-corruption ombudsman at the Union level, and required States to set up Lokayuktas. The Lokpal consists of a chairperson and up to eight members, at least half of them judicial members, and at least half from among Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, minorities and women. They are appointed on the recommendation of a selection committee of the Prime Minister, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Leader of the Opposition, the Chief Justice of India (or a judge nominated by the Chief Justice) and an eminent jurist. The Lokpal's jurisdiction extends to the Prime Minister, with safeguards, ministers, members of Parliament and public servants. The first Lokpal was appointed in 2019.

    Other national commissions¶

    Other statutory bodies include the National Commission for Women (under an Act of 1990, constituted in 1992), the National Commission for Minorities (under an Act of 1992) and the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (under an Act of 2005).

    Worked example 12.1: Where should the complaint go?

    Name the body most suited to each complaint.

    1. A government department has refused, without valid reason, to give a citizen copies of documents she applied for under the Right to Information Act, and her first appeal within the department has failed.
    2. A person alleges that his brother died after being beaten in police custody.
    3. A newspaper reports that a ministry spent large sums on equipment that was never used.
    4. A member of a Scheduled Caste alleges that he was denied a promotion in a central government office because of his caste.
    5. A citizen alleges that a Union minister took a bribe.

    Answers. (1) The Central Information Commission (or the State Information Commission, for a State department), by second appeal or complaint under the Right to Information Act. (2) The National Human Rights Commission (or the State Human Rights Commission); the family may also approach a court by writ petition. (3) This is a matter for audit by the CAG, whose report would be examined by the Public Accounts Committee. (4) The National Commission for Scheduled Castes, which can inquire into complaints of deprivation of safeguards. (5) The Lokpal, which has jurisdiction over ministers under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013.

    Summary

    • Independent bodies perform functions in which the government of the day has an interest: elections, audit, recruitment, sharing of revenue, protection of disadvantaged groups. They may be constitutional, statutory or executive.
    • The Election Commission (Article 324), a three-member body since 1993, superintends, directs and controls elections to Parliament, State legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President. The Chief Election Commissioner is removable only like a Supreme Court judge. Appointments are now governed by a law of 2023.
    • The CAG (Articles 148–151) audits Union and State accounts, reports to the President and Governors, and works with the Public Accounts Committee; the office is protected like a judgeship.
    • The UPSC and State Public Service Commissions (Articles 315–323) conduct recruitment and advise on service matters; their advice is not binding, but departures must be explained to the legislature.
    • The Finance Commission (280) and the GST Council (279A) govern fiscal federalism. Articles 338, 338A and 338B create national commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Backward Classes.
    • Statutory bodies include the NHRC, CVC, Information Commissions and the Lokpal; NITI Aayog and the CBI rest on executive resolutions.

    12.8 Exercises¶

    Exercise 1

    Distinguish a constitutional body from a statutory body, with two examples of each.

    Answer

    A constitutional body is created by the Constitution, and its basic features can be changed only by amendment: for example, the Election Commission and the CAG (also the UPSC and the Finance Commission). A statutory body is created by an Act of Parliament or a State legislature and can be changed by ordinary law: for example, the NHRC and the Central Vigilance Commission (also the Information Commissions and the Lokpal).

    Exercise 2

    How is the Chief Election Commissioner protected from removal, and how does this differ from the protection of other Election Commissioners?

    Answer

    The Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court: by an address of both Houses of Parliament passed by special majority for proved misbehaviour or incapacity. Other Election Commissioners can be removed by the President on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner, which is a weaker protection.

    Exercise 3

    Why is the CAG described as an auditor rather than a comptroller in India?

    Answer

    In India, money is withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund by the executive without the CAG's prior approval; the CAG audits expenditure after it has been incurred. Unlike the British CAG, the Indian CAG does not control the issue of money, so in practice the office is that of an auditor.

    Exercise 4

    Is the advice of the UPSC binding on the government? What check exists if the government does not accept it?

    Answer

    No, it is advisory. But under Article 323 the government must lay the UPSC's annual report before Parliament, with a memorandum explaining the cases in which the advice was not accepted and the reasons. Parliament can then question the government.

    Exercise 5

    Which amendments gave constitutional status to (a) a separate National Commission for Scheduled Tribes and (b) the National Commission for Backward Classes?

    Answer

    (a) The 89th Amendment (2003), which divided the earlier joint commission into Article 338 (SCs) and Article 338A (STs). (b) The 102nd Amendment (2018), which inserted Article 338B.

    Exercise 6

    Name one body each that was created by (a) an executive resolution and (b) the Right to Information Act, 2005.

    Answer

    (a) NITI Aayog (2015) (or the Planning Commission before it, or the CBI in 1963). (b) The Central Information Commission (and State Information Commissions).
