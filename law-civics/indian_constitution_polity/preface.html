    Preface¶

    How India is governed, and how to read this book

    Current as of late 2026

    Current as of late 2026; constitutional law changes: check the latest official text (the Constitution of India as published by the Legislative Department) before relying on details. Numbers of seats, the count of amendments and the position on questions still before the courts are the details most likely to change.

    About this book¶

    This book explains how India is governed. It starts from the document at the centre of the whole system, the Constitution of India, and follows it outwards into the institutions it creates: the President and the Council of Ministers, Parliament, the courts, the States, the panchayats and municipalities, the Election Commission and the other bodies that keep the system honest. Along the way it explains the rights the Constitution guarantees to every person, the goals it sets for the State, and the ways in which the Constitution itself can be changed and has been changed.

    The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950. It is a long document, and it can feel like a list of articles to be memorised. This book tries to show it instead as a working design. Every provision answers a question that the framers had to face: who should choose the head of state, how a law should be made, what should happen when the Centre and a State disagree, what a citizen can do when a right is taken away. When you know the question, the answer is much easier to remember, and much easier to judge.

    The book is neutral. It describes the Constitution, the main judgments of the Supreme Court and the arguments on each side of the debates that surround them. It does not take sides between political parties, and where a question is still disputed it says so.

    Who it is for¶

    The book is written for a reader who is meeting the subject for the first time:

    • a student in a first university course in political science, law or public administration;
    • a candidate preparing for a competitive examination with a paper on polity;
    • a school leaver, a teacher, a journalist or any citizen who wants to understand the system they live in.

    No background is needed beyond school. Legal words are explained the first time they appear, and the glossary at the end collects them.

    How the book is organised¶

    The fourteen chapters follow the order in which the system is usually built up in the mind.

    Part of the subject Chapters
    Foundations: how the Constitution was made, its Preamble, the Union and citizenship 1, 2
    Rights and goals: Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, Fundamental Duties 3, 4
    The organs of the Union: executive, Parliament, judiciary 5, 6, 7
    The federal design: Centre and States, State governments, local government 8, 9
    Changing and protecting the system: emergencies, amendment and the basic structure 10, 11
    Watchdogs and the people: constitutional bodies, elections and parties 12, 13
    Putting it together: the Constitution in practice 14

    How each chapter is built¶

    Every chapter has the same parts, so that you always know where you are.

    • Learning outcomes at the top say what you should be able to do when you have finished.
    • Numbered sections explain the topic, starting from the reason a provision exists and moving to its exact terms: the article number, who acts, what majority is needed.
    • Boxes pick out what matters. A key idea box states the one thing in a section not to forget. A note adds a detail or a piece of history. A caution marks a common confusion, the kind that costs marks in examinations.
    • Worked examples take a situation (a bill rejected by one House, a State asking for more money, a person arrested at night) and walk through how the Constitution deals with it.
    • Figures and tables show structures and comparisons that are hard to hold in words.
    • A summary collects the chapter in a few points.
    • Exercises come with answers folded under them. Try each one before you open the answer.
    • A short quiz at the end lets you check yourself; it remembers your answers in your browser.

    How to use it¶

    Read the chapters in order the first time: later chapters lean on earlier ones. Keep a copy of the Constitution beside you; the official text is published by the Legislative Department of the Ministry of Law and Justice and can be read free of charge. When the book cites an article, look it up and read the actual words. The articles are usually short, and reading them is the single best habit you can form.

    Three further habits help.

    1. Ask who decides. For any provision, find out which person or body acts, on whose advice, and who can check them.
    2. Count the majority. Many provisions turn on the kind of majority needed: a simple majority of those present and voting, a majority of the total membership, or two-thirds of those present and voting. Chapter 6 explains the differences, and they come back in almost every chapter after it.
    3. Separate the text from the interpretation. Much of Indian constitutional law is found not in the text but in what the Supreme Court has said the text means. The book marks clearly which is which.

    A note on numbers and names¶

    Articles are written as "Article 21" and parts of an article as "Article 19(1)(a)". Amendments are named by number and year, for example "the 42nd Amendment (1976)"; the year is the year of the amending Act. Court cases are cited by their short names, with the year of the judgment, for example Kesavananda Bharati (1973). The book avoids naming present holders of offices, since they change; it names historical figures where they are part of the story.
