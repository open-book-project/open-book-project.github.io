    Glossary¶

    The main terms of the book, with the chapter in which each is explained

    A to C¶

    Absolute advantage
    The ability to produce a good with fewer resources than another producer. (Chapter 14)
    Adverse selection
    A problem of hidden characteristics before a transaction, which leads the worse-quality or higher-risk types to dominate a market. (Chapter 12)
    Allocative efficiency
    Producing the quantity at which price equals marginal cost, so that the value of the last unit to buyers equals its cost to society. (Chapter 8)
    Anchoring
    The tendency of estimates to be pulled towards a number seen just before, even an irrelevant one. (Chapter 13)
    Average total cost (ATC)
    Total cost divided by output; the sum of average fixed cost and average variable cost. (Chapter 7)
    Barrier to entry
    Anything that prevents new firms from entering a market, such as patents, control of resources or economies of scale. (Chapter 9)
    Bounded rationality
    The idea that people try to choose well but are limited by information, time and computing power. (Chapter 13)
    Budget line
    The bundles of two goods that cost exactly the consumer's income; its slope is minus the ratio of the prices. (Chapter 6)
    Cartel
    A group of firms that agree to restrict output or fix prices. (Chapter 10)
    Ceteris paribus
    "Other things being equal": the assumption that only the variable under study changes. (Chapter 1)
    Coase theorem
    If property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, private parties reach the efficient outcome whoever holds the rights. (Chapter 12)
    Common resource
    A good that is rival but non-excludable, and so tends to be overused. (Chapter 12)
    Comparative advantage
    The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer. (Chapter 14)
    Complements
    Goods used together, so that a rise in the price of one reduces the demand for the other. (Chapter 2)
    Consumer surplus
    Willingness to pay minus the price paid; the area below the demand curve and above the price. (Chapter 4)
    Cross-price elasticity of demand
    The percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another. (Chapter 3)

    D to I¶

    Deadweight loss
    The fall in total surplus when the quantity traded differs from the efficient quantity. (Chapter 4)
    Demand curve
    A graph of the quantity demanded at each price, other things equal. (Chapter 2)
    Derived demand
    Demand for a factor of production that arises from the demand for the goods it helps produce. (Chapter 11)
    Diminishing marginal returns
    The fall in the marginal product of a variable input as more of it is added to a fixed input. (Chapter 7)
    Diminishing marginal utility
    The fall in the extra satisfaction from each additional unit of a good consumed within a period. (Chapter 5)
    Dominant strategy
    A strategy that gives a player the highest payoff whatever the other players do. (Chapter 10)
    Economic profit
    Total revenue minus all costs, explicit and implicit. (Chapter 7)
    Economic rent
    Payment to a factor above its transfer earnings. (Chapter 11)
    Economies of scale
    A fall in long-run average cost as output rises. (Chapter 7)
    Elastic demand
    Demand for which the absolute value of the price elasticity is greater than 1. (Chapter 3)
    Endowment effect
    The tendency to value a thing more once one owns it. (Chapter 13)
    Equilibrium
    The price and quantity at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. (Chapter 2)
    Equimarginal principle
    Utility is maximised when the marginal utility per rupee is equal for all goods bought. (Chapter 5)
    Externality
    A cost or benefit of an activity that falls on people outside the transaction and is not reflected in the price. (Chapter 12)
    Factors of production
    The inputs used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship. (Chapter 1)
    Free rider
    A person who benefits from a good without paying for it. (Chapter 12)
    Giffen good
    An inferior good whose income effect outweighs its substitution effect, so that its demand curve slopes upwards. (Chapter 6)
    Heuristic
    A mental short cut or rule of thumb used to make judgements. (Chapter 13)
    Income effect
    The change in quantity demanded caused by the change in real income that follows a price change. (Chapter 6)
    Income elasticity of demand
    The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income. (Chapter 3)
    Incidence of a tax
    How the burden of a tax is divided between buyers and sellers. (Chapter 4)
    Indifference curve
    A curve joining bundles of goods that give a consumer the same satisfaction. (Chapter 6)
    Inelastic demand
    Demand for which the absolute value of the price elasticity is less than 1. (Chapter 3)
    Inferior good
    A good whose demand falls when income rises. (Chapter 2)

    L to O¶

    Law of demand
    Other things equal, a higher price reduces the quantity demanded. (Chapter 2)
    Law of supply
    Other things equal, a higher price increases the quantity supplied. (Chapter 2)
    Long run
    A period long enough for all inputs to be varied and for firms to enter or leave an industry. (Chapter 7)
    Loss aversion
    The tendency for a loss to be felt more strongly than a gain of the same size. (Chapter 13)
    Marginal cost (MC)
    The extra cost of producing one more unit of output. (Chapter 7)
    Marginal product (MP)
    The extra output from one more unit of an input. (Chapter 7)
    Marginal rate of substitution (MRS)
    The amount of one good a consumer will give up for one more unit of another while staying equally well off. (Chapter 6)
    Marginal revenue (MR)
    The extra revenue from selling one more unit. (Chapters 8 and 9)
    Marginal revenue product (MRP)
    The extra revenue from employing one more unit of a factor: its marginal product times marginal revenue. (Chapter 11)
    Market failure
    A situation in which a market left to itself allocates resources inefficiently. (Chapter 12)
    Monopolistic competition
    A market with many firms selling differentiated products and free entry. (Chapter 10)
    Monopoly
    A market with a single seller of a good without close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry. (Chapter 9)
    Monopsony
    A market with a single buyer, such as a single employer in a labour market. (Chapter 11)
    Moral hazard
    A problem of hidden actions after a transaction, when one party has less incentive to take care because it does not bear the full consequences. (Chapter 12)
    Nash equilibrium
    A set of strategies in which each player's strategy is the best response to the others'. (Chapter 10)
    Normal good
    A good whose demand rises when income rises. (Chapter 2)
    Normal profit
    Zero economic profit: revenue just covers all costs, including the opportunity cost of the owner's time and money. (Chapter 7)
    Nudge
    A feature of the way a choice is presented that changes behaviour predictably without restricting options or significantly changing incentives. (Chapter 13)
    Oligopoly
    A market dominated by a few firms whose decisions are interdependent. (Chapter 10)
    Opportunity cost
    The value of the best alternative given up when a choice is made. (Chapter 1)

    P to Z¶

    Perfect competition
    A market with many small buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, free entry and exit, and perfect information. (Chapter 8)
    Present bias
    A tendency to give the immediate present much more weight than any later time, leading to time-inconsistent choices. (Chapter 13)
    Present value
    The value today of a sum to be received in the future, found by discounting at the interest rate. (Chapter 11)
    Price ceiling
    A legal maximum price; binding when set below the equilibrium price, when it causes a shortage. (Chapter 4)
    Price discrimination
    Selling the same good to different buyers at different prices for reasons unrelated to cost. (Chapter 9)
    Price elasticity of demand
    The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. (Chapter 3)
    Price elasticity of supply
    The percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. (Chapter 3)
    Price floor
    A legal minimum price; binding when set above the equilibrium price, when it causes a surplus. (Chapter 4)
    Prisoner's dilemma
    A game in which each player's dominant strategy leads to an outcome worse for all than cooperation. (Chapter 10)
    Producer surplus
    The price received minus cost; the area above the supply curve and below the price. (Chapter 4)
    Production possibility frontier (PPF)
    The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology. (Chapter 1)
    Productive efficiency
    Producing output at the lowest possible average cost. (Chapter 8)
    Public good
    A good that is non-rival and non-excludable. (Chapter 12)
    Scarcity
    The condition in which resources are too limited to satisfy all wants. (Chapter 1)
    Short run
    A period in which at least one input is fixed. (Chapter 7)
    Shortage
    A situation in which the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied at the current price. (Chapter 2)
    Shutdown point
    The price equal to the minimum of average variable cost, below which a firm should stop producing in the short run. (Chapter 8)
    Substitutes
    Goods that can replace each other, so that a rise in the price of one increases the demand for the other. (Chapter 2)
    Substitution effect
    The change in quantity demanded caused by a change in relative prices, with satisfaction held constant. (Chapter 6)
    Sunk cost
    A cost already incurred that cannot be recovered; it should not affect decisions. (Chapter 1)
    Surplus (excess supply)
    A situation in which the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded at the current price. (Chapter 2)
    Tariff
    A tax on imported goods. (Chapter 14)
    Terms of trade
    The rate at which one good is exchanged for another in trade. (Chapter 14)
    Total surplus
    The sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus. (Chapter 4)
    Tragedy of the commons
    The overuse of a common resource because each user bears only part of the cost of her use. (Chapter 12)
    Transfer earnings
    The minimum payment needed to keep a factor in its present use. (Chapter 11)
    Utility
    The satisfaction a consumer gets from goods and services. (Chapter 5)
