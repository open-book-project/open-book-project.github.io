    Preface¶

    What this book covers and how to get the most from it

    About this book¶

    Every day you make economic decisions. You decide how to spend a limited allowance or salary, whether an extra hour of study is worth more than an hour of sleep, and whether a bus ticket or an auto-rickshaw ride is the better way home. Shopkeepers decide what to stock and what to charge. Farmers decide what to sow. Large firms decide whether to build a new plant, and governments decide what to tax and what to subsidise. Microeconomics is the study of these decisions and of how they fit together in markets.

    This book is a first course in microeconomics. It begins with the idea at the heart of the subject, that resources are scarce and every choice has an opportunity cost, and then builds the main tools one at a time: demand and supply, elasticity, consumer and producer surplus, the theory of the consumer, production and costs, and the theory of the firm in different market structures. The later chapters use these tools to study the markets for labour, capital and land, the ways in which markets can fail, what behavioural economics adds to the standard theory, and why countries gain from trading with one another.

    The book explains each idea in three ways: in plain words, with a diagram, and with numbers. Every chapter contains worked numerical examples whose arithmetic has been checked, and diagrams drawn to scale from the same numbers, so that you can see that the words, the picture and the calculation say the same thing.

    Who it is for¶

    The book is written for a reader meeting economics for the first time. It suits:

    • a student in a first university course in economics, commerce, management or the social sciences;
    • a student in the final years of school who wants a careful introduction;
    • a student of engineering, law or the sciences who needs to understand how markets work;
    • anyone preparing for an examination or a profession in which basic economics is expected.

    No background is assumed beyond school mathematics. You should be comfortable with percentages, with straight lines on a graph and with solving a pair of simple equations. A few sections use the idea of a slope, and one or two optional boxes use a derivative; each of these is explained where it occurs, and none is needed to follow the rest of the book.

    How the book is organised¶

    Part Chapters What you learn
    I. Markets 1 to 4 Scarcity and opportunity cost; demand and supply; elasticity; surplus, taxes, subsidies and price controls
    II. Consumers and firms 5 to 7 Utility and consumer choice; indifference curves and budget constraints; production and costs
    III. Market structures 8 to 10 Perfect competition; monopoly; monopolistic competition, oligopoly and basic game theory
    IV. Beyond a single market 11 to 14 Factor markets; market failure; behavioural economics; international trade

    Table P.1. The four parts of the book.

    The chapters are meant to be read in order. Chapters 1 to 4 are the foundation for everything else. Chapters 5 and 6 (the consumer) and Chapter 7 (the firm) can be read in either order, but Chapter 7 must come before Chapters 8 to 11.

    How each chapter is built¶

    Every chapter follows the same plan:

    • Learning outcomes at the top tell you what you should be able to do when you finish.
    • Numbered sections explain each idea, first by intuition and then precisely.
    • Worked examples show every step of a calculation. Cover the solution and try each one yourself first.
    • Figures are drawn from the numbers of the examples, so that you can read values off them.
    • Boxes pick out what matters most: a key idea to remember, a note that adds detail, and a caution that warns of a common mistake.
    • A summary collects the main points.
    • Exercises follow, each with its answer folded underneath. Try each exercise on paper before you open the answer.
    • A short quiz at the end lets you check your understanding; it can be answered on the page.

    How to use it¶

    Economics is learned by doing problems. Reading a demand and supply diagram is easy; drawing one from a description of a market, and saying what happens when something changes, is the skill that counts. For each chapter:

    1. Read the explanation once, without stopping to memorise.
    2. Redraw every figure yourself, labelling the axes and the curves, and rework every example with a pencil.
    3. Attempt the exercises, then open the answers. Where your answer differs, find the step at which it went wrong.
    4. Take the quiz. If you miss a question, reread the section it comes from.

    A few chapters contain short programs in Python that repeat a calculation of the text. They are optional. If you know a little Python, run them and change the numbers to see what happens; if you do not, skip them, because nothing in the book depends on them.

    Conventions¶

    • Prices in examples are mostly in Indian rupees (₹). The numbers are chosen to make the arithmetic clear; they are not data about any real market.
    • Names of firms, towns and people in the examples are invented, unless the text says otherwise.
    • When the text mentions real countries, markets or policies, it describes them only in general terms. Laws, tax rates and policies change, so check a current official source before relying on a detail.
    • \(P\) stands for price and \(Q\) for quantity. \(Q_d\) and \(Q_s\) are the quantities demanded and supplied. A symbol with a star, such as \(P^*\), is an equilibrium value.
    • "Per unit" amounts are written as ₹6 per unit; total amounts as ₹312.
    • On diagrams, price or cost is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, as is customary in economics.
