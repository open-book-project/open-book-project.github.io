    Glossary¶

    The main terms of the book, with the chapter in which each is explained

    A¶

    Accounting
    The process of identifying, measuring, recording, classifying, summarising, interpreting and communicating financial information about an organisation to its users. (Chapter 1)
    Accounting equation
    Assets = Liabilities + Capital; true after every transaction because every transaction has two aspects. (Chapter 2)
    Accounting period
    The interval, usually a year, for which results are measured and financial statements prepared. (Chapter 1)
    Accounting standards
    Authoritative statements of how particular transactions and events are to be recognised, measured, presented and disclosed, such as IFRS, Ind AS and AS. (Chapter 1)
    Accrual basis
    Recording revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, whether or not cash has been received or paid. (Chapter 1)
    Accrued income
    Income earned during the period but not yet received; a current asset. (Chapter 8)
    Appropriation account
    In a partnership, the account that divides net profit among the partners through interest on capital, salaries and shares of profit. (Chapter 10)
    Asset
    A resource owned or controlled by the business from which future benefits are expected. (Chapter 1)
    Audit trail
    A record, kept by accounting software, of who entered or changed each transaction, when, and what was changed. (Chapter 14)
    Authorised capital
    The maximum share capital that a company is permitted to issue. (Chapter 11)

    B¶

    Bad debt
    An amount owed by a customer that is certainly irrecoverable and is written off as an expense. (Chapter 7)
    Balance sheet
    A statement of the assets, liabilities and capital of a business at a date. (Chapters 2 and 8)
    Bank reconciliation statement
    A statement explaining the difference between the bank balance in the cash book and the balance in the bank statement on a date. (Chapter 5)
    Bill of exchange
    An unconditional written order, signed by the drawer, directing the drawee to pay a certain sum to a certain person or to the bearer; it binds the drawee once accepted. (Chapter 5)
    Bookkeeping
    The recording part of accounting: keeping the journal, cash book and ledger. (Chapter 1)
    Business entity concept
    The principle that the business is accounted for separately from its owner. (Chapter 1)

    C¶

    Calls in arrears
    Amounts called up on shares but not yet paid by shareholders. (Chapter 11)
    Capital
    The owner's claim on the assets of the business; assets less liabilities. (Chapter 2)
    Capital employed
    The long-term funds used in a business: shareholders' funds plus long-term debt, or total assets less current liabilities. (Chapter 13)
    Capital expenditure
    Spending that brings benefit for more than one year and creates or improves an asset. (Chapter 1)
    Capital fund
    In a non-profit organisation, the excess of assets over liabilities; the equivalent of capital. (Chapter 9)
    Capital reserve
    A reserve created out of capital profits, such as the profit on reissue of forfeited shares; generally not available for dividend. (Chapters 7 and 11)
    Cash equivalents
    Short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible into known amounts of cash with insignificant risk of change in value. (Chapter 12)
    Cash flow statement
    A statement of the inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents, classified into operating, investing and financing activities. (Chapter 12)
    Chart of accounts
    The organised list of all ledger accounts of a business, usually coded and grouped. (Chapter 14)
    Compensating errors
    Errors that cancel each other out, leaving the trial balance in agreement. (Chapter 6)
    Contra entry
    An entry in the cash book that affects both the cash and the bank columns; it is not posted to the ledger. (Chapter 4)
    Cost of goods sold
    Opening stock plus net purchases plus direct expenses minus closing stock. (Chapter 8)
    Credit note
    A document sent to a customer stating that his account has been credited, usually for goods returned. (Chapter 4)
    Current assets
    Cash and assets expected to be realised, sold or used up within a year. (Chapter 8)
    Current liabilities
    Liabilities due for settlement within a year. (Chapter 8)
    Current ratio
    Current assets divided by current liabilities. (Chapter 13)

    D¶

    Debentures
    Long-term borrowings of a company in the form of written acknowledgements of debt, usually carrying fixed interest. (Chapter 11)
    Debit and credit
    The left and right sides of an account. Increases in assets, expenses and drawings are debits; increases in liabilities, capital and revenue are credits. (Chapters 2 and 3)
    Debit note
    A document sent to a supplier stating that his account has been debited, usually for goods returned to him. (Chapter 4)
    Debt-equity ratio
    Long-term debt divided by shareholders' funds. (Chapter 13)
    Depreciation
    The systematic allocation of the cost of a fixed asset, less its residual value, over its useful life. (Chapter 7)
    Discounting a bill
    Obtaining cash from a bank for a bill before its maturity, less a charge called discount. (Chapter 5)
    Double entry
    The principle that every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits. (Chapter 3)
    Drawings
    Money or goods taken out of the business by the owner for personal use. (Chapter 1)

    E¶

    Earnings per share
    Profit after tax less preference dividend, divided by the number of equity shares. (Chapters 11 and 13)
    Equity shares
    Shares that are not preference shares; their holders bear the residual risk and have voting rights. (Chapter 11)
    Error of principle
    Recording a transaction in the wrong class of account, such as treating capital expenditure as revenue expenditure. (Chapter 6)

    F¶

    Final accounts
    The trading account, the profit and loss account and the balance sheet prepared at the end of the year. (Chapter 8)
    Forfeiture of shares
    Cancellation of shares for non-payment of allotment or calls, the shareholder losing the money already paid. (Chapter 11)

    G¶

    Going concern concept
    The assumption that the business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future. (Chapter 1)
    Goodwill
    The value of a business beyond its separate net assets, arising from reputation, location, customers and staff. (Chapter 10)
    Gross profit
    Net sales less the cost of goods sold. (Chapter 8)

    H¶

    Historical cost concept
    The principle that assets are recorded at the price paid to acquire them. (Chapter 1)

    I¶

    IFRS
    International Financial Reporting Standards, issued by the International Accounting Standards Board. (Chapter 1)
    Imprest system
    A method of keeping petty cash in which the amount spent is reimbursed each period, restoring a fixed float. (Chapter 4)
    Ind AS
    Indian Accounting Standards converged with IFRS, applicable to specified classes of companies. (Chapter 1)
    Income and expenditure account
    The accrual-basis statement of a non-profit organisation's revenue income and expenses for the year; its balance is a surplus or deficit. (Chapter 9)
    Interest coverage ratio
    Profit before interest and tax divided by interest. (Chapter 13)

    J¶

    Journal
    The book of original entry in which transactions are first recorded in date order, with debits, credits and a narration. (Chapter 3)

    L¶

    Ledger
    The book containing all the accounts of a business. (Chapter 3)
    Liability
    An amount owed by the business to outsiders. (Chapter 1)

    M¶

    Matching concept
    The principle that expenses are recognised in the same period as the revenue they help to earn. (Chapter 1)
    Materiality
    The principle that items which could influence users' decisions must be shown accurately and separately, while immaterial items may be treated simply. (Chapter 1)

    N¶

    Net profit
    Gross profit plus other incomes less indirect expenses. (Chapter 8)
    Net realisable value
    The estimated selling price less the costs needed to complete and sell. (Chapter 1)
    Noting charges
    The fee paid to a notary public for recording the dishonour of a bill. (Chapter 5)

    O¶

    Operating activities
    The principal revenue-producing activities of a business, and other activities that are not investing or financing. (Chapter 12)
    Outstanding expense
    An expense incurred during the period but not yet paid; a current liability. (Chapter 8)

    P¶

    Paid-up capital
    Called-up share capital less calls in arrears. (Chapter 11)
    Partnership deed
    The written agreement setting out the terms of a partnership. (Chapter 10)
    Petty cash book
    The book in which small cash payments are recorded, usually on the imprest system. (Chapter 4)
    Posting
    Transferring entries from the books of original entry to the ledger accounts. (Chapter 3)
    Prepaid expense
    An expense paid in advance for a later period; a current asset. (Chapter 8)
    Promissory note
    An unconditional written promise, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum to a certain person or to the bearer. (Chapter 5)
    Provision
    An amount charged against profit to cover a known liability or expected loss whose amount cannot be determined exactly. (Chapter 7)
    Prudence
    Caution in judgement under uncertainty, so that assets and income are not overstated and liabilities and expenses are not understated. (Chapter 1)

    Q¶

    Quick ratio
    Current assets less inventories and prepaid expenses, divided by current liabilities. (Chapter 13)

    R¶

    Receipts and payments account
    A summary of the cash book of a non-profit organisation, on the cash basis. (Chapter 9)
    Rectification
    The correction of errors by means of entries, without erasing. (Chapter 6)
    Reserve
    An amount appropriated out of profit to strengthen the business or for a future purpose. (Chapter 7)
    Return on capital employed
    Profit before interest and tax as a percentage of capital employed. (Chapter 13)
    Revenue expenditure
    Spending whose benefit is used up within the year; an expense. (Chapter 1)

    S¶

    Sacrificing ratio
    The ratio in which old partners give up their shares of profit to a new partner. (Chapter 10)
    Securities premium
    The excess of the issue price of shares over their nominal value, kept in a separate account whose uses are restricted by law. (Chapter 11)
    Straight line method
    Charging the same amount of depreciation every year: (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life. (Chapter 7)
    Subsidiary books
    Books of original entry for particular kinds of transaction: purchases, sales, returns, cash and petty cash books, and the journal proper. (Chapter 4)
    Suspense account
    A temporary account holding the difference in a trial balance until the errors are found. (Chapter 6)

    T¶

    Trade discount
    A reduction in list price given to traders; deducted on the invoice and not recorded in the books. (Chapter 3)
    Trading account
    The account that compares net sales with the cost of goods sold to find gross profit. (Chapter 8)
    Trial balance
    A list of the balances of all ledger accounts, in debit and credit columns, prepared to check arithmetical accuracy. (Chapter 3)

    W¶

    Window dressing
    Arranging transactions around the year end so that the financial statements look better than the underlying position. (Chapter 14)
    Working capital cycle
    The time between paying suppliers and receiving cash from customers: inventory days plus collection days minus payment days. (Chapter 13)
    Written down value method
    Charging depreciation at a fixed percentage of the book value at the start of each year. (Chapter 7)
