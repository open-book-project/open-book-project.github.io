    Preface¶

    How this book is organised and how to get the most from it

    About this book¶

    Accounting is often called the language of business. Every business, however small, has to answer the same questions: what does it own, what does it owe, did it make a profit last year, and can it pay its bills next month? Accounting is the discipline that answers them, and financial accounting is the part of it that produces the reports a business shows to the outside world.

    This book teaches financial accounting from the beginning. It starts with the purpose of accounting and the ideas on which it rests, moves through the mechanics of recording (the accounting equation, double entry, the journal, the ledger and the trial balance), and then builds up, chapter by chapter, to complete financial statements for a sole trader, a non-profit organisation, a partnership and a company. The last three chapters show how those statements are read and used: the cash flow statement, the analysis of financial statements through ratios, and the use of accounting software together with the ethical duties of the people who prepare accounts.

    Every chapter is built around fully worked numerical examples. Journal entries, ledger accounts, cash books, reconciliation statements and financial statements are all laid out as tables, in the form in which you would draw them on paper, and every total in them has been checked. The amounts are in Indian rupees (₹), written with the Indian grouping of digits (₹1,00,000 is one lakh rupees; ₹10,00,000 is ten lakh).

    Who it is for¶

    The book is written for a reader who has never studied accounting before. It suits:

    • a student in a first university course in accounting or commerce, or in the final years of school;
    • a student of management, economics, law or engineering who needs to read financial statements;
    • a person who runs, or plans to run, a small business and wants to understand the books that an accountant keeps for it;
    • anyone preparing for a professional course who wants a careful grounding in the basics.

    No background beyond school arithmetic is assumed. Percentages, fractions and simple interest are the only mathematics used, and each is shown in full where it occurs.

    How the book is organised¶

    Part of the book Chapters What you learn
    Foundations 1 to 2 Why accounting exists, who uses it, the concepts and standards behind it, and the accounting equation
    Recording 3 to 5 Double entry, the journal, the ledger, the trial balance, subsidiary books, the cash book, bank reconciliation and bills of exchange
    Correcting and adjusting 6 to 7 Finding and correcting errors; depreciation, provisions and reserves
    Reporting 8 to 11 Final accounts of a sole proprietor, of a non-profit organisation, of a partnership and of a company
    Using the reports 12 to 14 The cash flow statement, ratio analysis, accounting software and ethics

    The chapters are meant to be read in order. Chapters 1 to 4 are the foundation for everything else: a reader who is comfortable with the journal, the ledger and the trial balance will find every later chapter an extension of the same few ideas.

    How each chapter is built¶

    Every chapter follows the same plan:

    • Learning outcomes at the top tell you what you should be able to do when you finish.
    • Numbered sections explain each idea, first in plain words and then precisely.
    • Worked examples show every step of a calculation or a set of entries. Work through them with a pencil before moving on.
    • Boxes pick out what matters most: a key idea to remember, a note that adds detail, and a caution that warns of a common mistake.
    • A summary collects the main points.
    • Exercises follow, each with its answer folded underneath. Try each exercise on paper before you open the answer.
    • A short quiz at the end lets you check your understanding; it can be answered on the page.

    How to use it¶

    Accounting is learned by doing. Reading a journal entry is easy; writing one from a description of a transaction is the skill that counts. For each chapter:

    1. Read the explanation once, without stopping to memorise.
    2. Rework every worked example on paper, covering the solution and comparing only when you have finished.
    3. Attempt the exercises, then open the answers. Where your answer differs, find the step at which it went wrong.
    4. Take the quiz. If you miss a question, reread the section it comes from.

    Keep a notebook with ruled columns, or use a spreadsheet, and lay out your answers in the same tables as the book. The habit of neat columns and checked totals is half of good accounting.

    A note on laws and standards¶

    Accounting is shaped by law and by accounting standards, and both change. Company law, tax law and the standards issued by standard-setting bodies are revised from time to time, and the details differ from country to country. This book explains the principles, which change slowly, and describes the legal and regulatory setting only in general terms. Where a rule depends on the current law (a rate of tax, a limit, a required format), check the latest official text before relying on it in practice.

    Conventions in the examples¶

    • The financial year runs from 1 April to 31 March, as it does for most businesses in India. "2025-26" means the year from 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026.
    • Names of businesses and people in the examples are invented.
    • Goods and services tax and other indirect taxes are left out of most examples so that the accounting principle is not hidden by tax entries; Chapter 4 explains briefly where tax columns are added in practice.
    • In ledger accounts, "To" introduces an entry on the debit side and "By" an entry on the credit side, as is customary.
    • "Dr" means debit and "Cr" means credit; "A/c" means account; "b/d" means brought down and "c/d" carried down.
